The McDonald's CEO Took the Smallest Bite. You Don't Get the Choice.

A pricing tool that logs refusals, a pantry that certifies itself, a $1.2 million remodel bill. The same paperwork runs all of it.

From a viral bite to a pricing algorithm to a $1.2 million remodel bill, McDonald's runs on one mechanism: every layer that checks, prices, and approves reports to the company that profits. The papers are real. The signatures are the story.

The CEO of McDonald's is holding a burger toward the camera, pointing out the dent where his bite should be. It is February, the Big Arch is still weeks from menus, and Chris Kempczinski keeps calling the sandwich "the product." Sixteen million views later, the verdict on the clip was in: "That was the smallest first bite I've ever seen." (The Guardian)

Why does the man who runs 46,000 restaurants eat his own lunch like he is being watched? Because at McDonald's, the checkers, the prices, and the approvals all come from the same side of the table, and that pattern runs from the pickle jar to the price of the fries.

The pantry certifies itself. The pricing tool is "a recommendation." The remodel bill is, in the CEO's own words, "a demonstration of our faith." The signatures are real. Almost every signature belongs to McDonald's.

The pantry certifies itself

Start with the food, because the confidence is loudest there. The company's own product pages strike a careful tone. The Big Mac page says the sandwich contains "no artificial flavors, preservatives or added colors from artificial sources." The next sentence is the honest one: "McDonald's pickles contain an artificial preservative, so skip it if you like." (McDonald's)

That preservative, potassium sorbate, sits in a regulatory neighborhood where trust is the architecture. More than 10,000 chemicals can legally go into American food, and the fastest door in is labeled GRAS, short for "generally recognized as safe." By design, it needs no FDA approval before use, and federal auditors found the agency often does not even know the determinations happen. When researchers audited the industry's own safety reviews, they looked at 451 GRAS determinations and found conflicts of interest were routine; one consultant had a seat at 128 of them. The people who vouch for an ingredient are usually the people who sell it. (GAO) (JAMA Internal Medicine)

None of that makes a Big Mac poison, and the difference matters. The interesting evidence lives at the margins, where the labels speak in absolutes and the studies arrive with error bars. A 2010 toxicology study found potassium sorbate broke DNA strands in human lymphocytes at every concentration tested, in cells in a lab dish. A 2024 randomized trial found two weeks of carrageenan, a thickener used since the 1960s, increased intestinal permeability in healthy men, and in the overweight participants it came with signs of inflammation and lower insulin sensitivity. In mice, soybean oil shifted the activity of about a hundred genes in the hypothalamus, the region that governs body weight; the researchers could not say which compound did it, cleared linoleic acid, and warn against reading it as a verdict for people. In lab dishes, three common preservatives hurt the gut microbes that fight inflammation more than the ones that cause it. (Toxicology in Vitro) (BMC Medicine) (UC Riverside) (Folia Microbiologica)

Meanwhile the supply itself keeps shifting. Between 1959 and 2008, the share of linoleic acid in the body fat of American adults rose from 9.1 percent to 21.5 percent, tracking the rise of seed oils. (Advances in Nutrition00114-X/fulltext))

Read each result as a signal rather than a verdict: a dish, mice, twenty men, a systematic review. Now notice the asymmetry. The public claims come in marketing voice. The checkable evidence arrives in conditionals. And the layers that could demand better evidence answer to the layers that profit when nobody does.

Children learned this first, in a study from 2007. Researchers gave 63 kids from low-income families five foods, including carrots and milk, and asked which tasted better. The food was identical; only the packaging changed. For four of the five items, significantly more children preferred the food they believed came from McDonald's. The lead researcher's conclusion was blunt: the branding "physically altered" their perception of taste. (Stanford Medicine)

The fries carry the clearest case, and it is decades old. McDonald's fried potatoes in beef tallow for years until a businessman's heart attack became a $15 million one-man campaign against saturated fat. In 1990 the company switched to vegetable oil, then quietly added "natural beef flavor" during processing to keep the signature taste. It did not announce the addition. Vegetarians, Hindus, and eventually three plaintiffs noticed, and the lawsuit settled for a $10 million donation to vegetarian and religious groups and an apology. Today "natural beef flavor" still sits in the allergen chart, hydrolyzed milk and all, and the FDA does not require every component of a natural flavor to be disclosed. You cannot audit what the label is not required to say. (Daily Meal) (WJBF)

The price is a recommendation

Now point the same pattern at your wallet. In the past month the company's pricing software went from a trade curiosity to the subject of a lawsuit, and the details, first reported by Reuters, read like an operations manual. An engine built with Tiger Analytics scans millions of transactions and produces what McDonald's calls "the optimal price" for each item at each location, from Big Macs to discounted coffee for seniors. It grades each restaurant's price sensitivity, down to a sampled label that reads "MEDIUM SENSITIVITY to Price." It pulls prices from the online menus of nearby Wendy's and Burger King. One example from the reporting: a Big Mac at $5.69 at one restaurant, $6.89 at another, as the tool widened price gaps even between neighborhoods in the same area. (Reuters) (TNW)

McDonald's answered the way it always answers, with the paperwork. The tool is "a tool, not a mandate," built to help franchisees "make informed business decisions." On the company's own site, the arithmetic is laid out in plain sentences: "The Tool provides information. People make the final pricing decisions." Both statements can be true. So can this one, from the same reporting: franchisees were instructed to record every deviation from the recommended prices for corporate review, and in August the CEO told investors that "pricing non-compliance in certain cases is part of those conversations." A recommendation that gets logged when you refuse it is doing compliance work. (McDonald's)

One franchisee, Karen King, told Reuters the part the vocabulary exists to avoid. "You don't really have much of a choice anymore." The portal's own terms warn owners that they "may be competitors of each other," and a former Federal Trade Commission commissioner read that language as "an acknowledgment there's a potential problem." The company called the reporting "speculative and uninformed." It did not call it wrong. (TNW)

On the first Friday of October, a customer turned it all into a complaint in federal court, accusing the company of pressuring franchisees onto the software and of giving them a way to price against each other's data, the raw material of an antitrust claim. The suit says menu prices rose about 40 percent between 2019 and 2024. McDonald's has denied the claims and says it does not set prices for its restaurants. Whether that defense holds is a judge's question. The architecture is not in dispute: the system sees across the estate, the operator sets the final price, and the company keeps the minutes. (Newsweek)

The bill is a demonstration of faith

The operators carrying the consequences have documents of their own. This year the chain rolled out Next, a remodel program expected to cost about $800,000 per restaurant, with another $400,000 in related spending, a total tab near $1.2 million per store before the company's promised $8.5 billion in support lands. The CEO has a phrase for that support. "We're willing to partner with the franchisees as a demonstration of our faith." The faith is doing a lot of work in that sentence. (Financial Post)

The same months brought tighter franchising standards, and the standards have teeth: operators are required to "constructively engage" with approved pricing consultants and tools, and a franchisee's "authorization to continue operating" depends on "their level of compliance," per the lawsuit. The company writes the standards, scores the compliance, and holds the renewals. The operator sets the price and carries the bill. (Restaurant Dive)

Put the pictures together and the collage is not scandalous in the way a scandal is supposed to look. Almost nothing here is hidden. The additive list is published. The pricing tool was explained to investors. The remodel bills are in the franchise documents. The tell is the paper trail's direction, because every layer of this machine grants its own permission. The pantry vouches for itself. The tool that suggests prices grades its own confidence. The standards that bind operators are written by the party they protect.

One document in the whole system goes without a company signature. It is in your hand when you leave the drive-thru, and it is the only one with your name on it. Read it the way an auditor would. Ask who signed each claim you are paying for, and ask what happens to them if the claim is wrong. Recommendations, standards, voluntary reviews, "generally recognized as safe": the words are doing quiet work. The fine print is where you find out for whom.
