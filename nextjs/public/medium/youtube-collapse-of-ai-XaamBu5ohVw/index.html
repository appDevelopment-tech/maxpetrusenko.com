The $730 Billion Bet to Replace You Just Priced What You're Worth

A company spends months telling everyone AI is the future, then quietly hires the humans back. The reason is a measurement problem, and it changes how much a person is actually worth.

Companies are cutting AI spend and rehiring the people they let go. The tell, in the cases we can see: they counted how much everyone used the tool, and hardly asked whether it changed anything. The end of that bet priced what a person is actually worth.

Imagine you run a company and you decide to swap a nine-year employee for an AI. He knows the operation, he costs a lot, and the new hire never complains, never takes a break, and never asks for a raise. Months later you are paying more for the AI than you paid him, and the same charge is landing on plenty of other companies. The tension is simple: usage can rise while business impact falls. Around 60% of enterprises are now throttling or cutting their AI spend, and some employers are reversing the layoffs they blamed on AI. The reason, in the cases we can see, is that almost nobody checked whether it worked. The companies were counting how often people touched the tool, and those two numbers turned out to be very different.

The quiet story behind the AI pullback matters more than the usual bubble argument. The tension is simple: usage can rise while business impact falls. The same UBS research that found 60% of enterprises throttling AI spend describes companies that are being more careful, and CNBC reports employers who laid people off for AI are now reversing those decisions. If you share the fear that the AI boom is collapsing, the truth is stranger: the spend is still enormous, the bet is still running, and the people doing the cutting are the ones who mostly never built a way to know if the tool was worth the invoice.

The playbook that made AI the default

The reason no one measured impact is that the mandates were built to measure effort. In March 2025 Shopify's chief executive told staff that using AI effectively was now a fundamental expectation, and that before any team could ask for another person it had to show why the work still demanded a person. The CNBC report on the memo turned it into the template, and once one CEO published it, everyone repeated it. Coinbase took the same move further, refusing the adoption timeline the vendor suggested. After buying enterprise AI licenses and being told adoption would take a quarter or two, the chief executive rejected that and set a deadline to onboard by end of week, with anyone who did not meeting him on Saturday. Reporting on the firings shows some people did not make the deadline, and they were gone.

Amazon turned the same instinct into a game of rankings, and an internal leaderboard called Kirorank compared how often developers were using AI, with a target that most of them should be using it every week. Staff responded by adding tokens to pointless tasks, climbing the board and pushing the token bill higher along the way. CNET reported on the shutdown, citing the Financial Times: Amazon killed the leaderboard after the token bill exploded and the gaming became the point. Every one of those decisions was about getting people to use the tool, and each one skipped the question of whether it worked.

No one had to show the tool made anyone faster or returned a dollar. Every one of them measured the same number, how much people used it, and that number always goes up when you threaten promotions or jobs.

Who gets paid either way

Once the metric is usage, the money works the same direction for everyone watching. A chief executive is paid mostly in stock, so a statement that moves the share price is a payday. Allbirds sold its footwear brand for about $39 million. Renamed itself Newbird AI. Then Smartbird. Installed a former AWS executive as chief executive. Pivoted to AI infrastructure. The stock spiked roughly 582% in a single day, a move CNBC covered as a pivot that made the stock soar. Chegg's story is worse: the same technology was already gutting the business, shares falling from above $113 in 2021 after students stopped paying for homework answers, yet the stock still jumped 17% the day it launched its own GPT-4-based product. A company that AI was mid-way through killing got paid for announcing it would use AI. The market rewarded the announcement and left whether it worked for later.

The people selling the tools are paid the same way. AI is often billed per token for the API products that dominate enterprise spend, so the more useful the tool becomes, the more you owe. Inference prices have fallen sharply since 2022, and cheaper tokens have a strange effect: they lower the cost of each use, yet companies find more work to feed the model, which is why the bill kept climbing for the enterprises EY surveyed. Economists call that the Jevons paradox, and it is why enterprise AI spend kept climbing until EY found escalating token costs triggering fiscal scrutiny. The biggest labs are not clearing a profit from the boom so far. OpenAI's leaked 2025 books show about $13 billion in revenue against roughly $34 billion in costs, a loss of about $21 billion in one year, while reports pointed to a public listing at a valuation near a trillion dollars.

The math that makes you the prize

The bet stops being somebody else's problem here. Wall Street's consensus for big-tech AI capital expenditure this year has climbed from around $485 billion in January to roughly 730 billion by July. The logic of the bet is that a pile that size only works if it returns something close to what it cost, and the pool of money big enough to carry it is the wage bill — the only line item on the planet large enough to make a $730 billion bet pencil out. That is the literal math: they believe enough of what you do all day can be replicated for a fraction of a cent per token to be worth the wager. Which is why the whole thing hinges on whether AI can do your job for less than you cost. That trade explains why the next few years keep returning to the question of what a human is worth.

The companies are finding out now, because the metric finally flipped. Amazon tore down the usage leaderboard, UBS started watching the throttling, and Ford learned the same lesson in the costliest way. It pushed quality work toward AI tools and, across the same years, absorbed billions in quality and warranty costs. How much of that the tooling directly caused is less clear than the headline numbers suggest. What Ford did next is cleanly documented: it brought back about 350 veteran engineers, the gray beards, and the result was a top finish in the J.D. Power Initial Quality Survey. But read the job description before you call it a win. Ford didn't rehire them because the humans beat the machine. It rehired them to train younger staff and reprogram the AI — temporary scaffolding, not a reversal. The company's own vice president put it plainly: AI is only as good as the information you use to train it. Which means the gray beards were brought back to become that information, then hand the job to the thing that replaced them.

The catch is that the rehiring is not a clean win anywhere else either. A similar pattern is emerging in pockets of other industries where work returns cheaper, moves offshore, or where entry-level roles get absorbed. Where that happens, fewer entry-level hires means a thinner pipeline growing the next gray beards. The human being worth more than the machine, and the human being needed to teach the machine, are two different facts, and only one of them helps you.

The incentive trap that will start it again

The hardest thing to accept is that the people who cut jobs early were not reckless. The incentives made it the right call for their own company. Picture ten shop owners in a town where every worker spends their paycheck at the other nine shops. Swap half your staff for AI and you keep the savings, while nine-tenths of the spending loss lands on your competitors. In a rational market every owner runs that math and arrives at the same answer: if nine of them do it and you hold back, you become the most expensive shop in town. Within that logic, waiting to find out whether AI can do the job looks like the losing move even when waiting is obviously correct.

The counterexample proves the trap rather than breaking it. Apple spent two years as the last big company without a loud AI story and still became the best-performing Magnificent 7 stock of 2026 — but Apple could afford that patience because it had the one thing the ten shop owners don't: a balance sheet and a brand strong enough to survive being the most expensive shop in town while it waited. That is not a replicable strategy. It is proof of how narrow the exit is.

Those incentives are still sitting right there, loaded and unchanged. The next time someone works out how to swap a person for an AI, it will start again, and this time there may not be enough entry-level people left to grow the next experts.

What the bet priced about you

The useful thing to come out of it: a person's worth finally became visible, and only because the measurement problem forced the market to look at what usage metrics hid. What you cost was always the easy part to measure. What stayed invisible was what you prevent — the warranty bill Ford is still paying down — and what you create, the first version of something rather than the thousandth. An AI learns from what already exists, because training data is built that way. It can make a thousand versions of the last thing faster than any human, but the thing that genuinely changes the game still has to come from a person first. As current systems stand, that is a real limitation, and every lab is pouring money into closing it. The gap between recombining the past and stepping into the future is what the market is starting to price.

The practical move isn't to refuse the tool — it's to watch which side of the bet you're on. If you're setting the metrics, replace the usage count with an impact count before the next mandate. If you're under one, listen for the language of the rehiring: are people coming back to do the work, or to train the model? That distinction tells you whether you're the asset or the feedstock.

They just spent what may be the biggest pile of money in corporate history to find out if you were worth what you cost. So far, you've passed — but only because the machine can't yet do what you do first. The pile is still sitting there, waiting for that gap to close. It is not rooting for you.
