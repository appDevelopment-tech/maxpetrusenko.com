No Pill Fills All Six Cups of Happiness

One chemical can change how you feel. That does not mean it can build everything a good life requires.

Bouchon's six-cup model describes positive emotion as a blend of dopamine, testosterone, serotonin, oxytocin, endocannabinoids, and endogenous opioids. It is a heuristic, not a settled map: a drug shifts one or two of those systems at most, and still cannot recreate the relationships and context behind the experience.

The six cups are a heuristic, not a map

Neuroscientist and biotech entrepreneur Axel Bouchon frames positive emotion as six cups: dopamine, testosterone, serotonin, oxytocin, endocannabinoids, and endogenous opioids. The model appears in a Max Joseph video about the chemistry of happiness and in Bouchon's own interviews and company material. None of that makes it consensus, and the six are not even the same kind of thing: dopamine is a classic neurotransmitter, testosterone a steroid hormone with neural effects, oxytocin a neuropeptide, endocannabinoids lipid signaling molecules, and endogenous opioids peptides.

So the cups stay a metaphor, and they work on purpose. They are a way to ask what kinds of experience we keep trying to obtain through one narrow chemical route, and this is where the honesty matters: the moment the six cups start to sound like settled neuroscientific fact, they have stopped being useful and turned back into a metaphor, which is where they were always supposed to live.

Drugs change states, not contexts

The tidy idea that each drug picks its own cup does not survive contact with the pharmacology. Cocaine blocks the reuptake of serotonin, norepinephrine and dopamine together, hitting all three transporters. MDMA favours serotonin but also moves norepinephrine and dopamine, with broader social and empathy-tinged effects. A drug can push several systems at once, and it still does not reproduce the full biological and social context of the experience it imitates.

The real argument is stronger than the one-cup version: no pill recreates a dinner, a friendship, or a project you care about. A drug changes a state, and the state may feel like the experience for a while, then the context, the memory, the meaning, all the parts that made the state worth reaching for in the first place, are still missing.

One molecule rarely means one feeling

A drug-by-drug reading of which cup gets filled is too clean. An SSRI like Prozac inhibits the serotonin transporter and alters serotonergic signaling, but the therapeutic effect is not a matter of topping up serotonin; the downstream adaptation is slower and messier, and it argues against reading antidepressant effects as more serotonin equals more happiness.

Cannabis delivers cannabinoids that imitate the brain's own endocannabinoids and overstimulate the cannabinoid receptors, a mechanism the endocannabinoid system explains in detail, producing euphoria, relaxation and altered sensory perception.

Opioids mimic the endorphins that bind the μ-opioid receptors, easing pain and regulating a general sense of well-being, but they suppress norepinephrine release, and when they leave the suppressed neurons rebound hard, the jitters, anxiety, cramps and diarrhea of withdrawal. Each of these can change how you feel within minutes, which is the whole point and the whole limit.

Medication is a tool, not a whole life

If you take a prescribed medication, keep taking it and let a doctor be the one who adjusts it. The frame here is about the shape of a life, not a prescription change, and reading the six cups should never be a reason to drop a treatment that is working or to feel that a treatment is somehow the wrong cup.

Why relationships are hard to counterfeit

The part of the model that holds up best is the social one. Some of the most durable sources of well-being are relational: attachment, being known, shared laughter, trust, belonging, and their biology draws on many overlapping systems, including oxytocin, endogenous opioids and dopamine. A molecule can perturb one part of that machinery without recreating the relationship itself. People have administered oxytocin intranasally for two decades, and the research has produced mixed, often unreplicated results, which says a great deal about how hard it is to bottle what a real bond delivers.

Endocannabinoid and opioid signaling also happen during solitary exercise, pain modulation, food and music, so the neat split between self-triggered cups and social cups is too clean. The safe version is simpler: the experiences that build durable well-being are relational in a way a molecule cannot replace.

The substitution test

If the six cups are a heuristic, they are best spent as a named question rather than a diagnosis. When you reach for a fast state change, ask:

  • What feeling am I trying to produce or remove?
  • What does this give me right now?
  • What broader need might sit underneath it?
  • Is there a slower, less costly way to meet part of that need?

The substitution test is a self-reflection tool, not a diagnosis: substance use involves dependence, genetics, trauma, psychiatric symptoms, environment, availability and habit, and predicting which exact need a specific drug answers is too clean a story to trust. The test only asks you to look for the need, and it never assumes you have found it.

A good life recruits more than one system

The strongest image in the model is a dinner with friends. You show up, you laugh, you trade stories and remember them, you touch shoulders, argue, finish the food and leave a little fuller. No single drug needs to be the chemical equivalent of any one of those pieces for the larger point to hold: a rich experience recruits many systems at once, and the brain prefers high, stable activation over a long stretch to a single spike. Trying to buy that with one compound is like expecting a shortcut to reproduce the whole evening.

The brake: even a good tool is incomplete

One account of living on mood drugs states the limit plainly: even a daily SSRI for the rest of your life leaves other parts of a life unfilled. A tool holds down one system and never balances the whole. If you are depressed, a clinician is the right person to talk to, and the six cups are not a substitute for that judgment.

No pill has to be bad for being incomplete

No single chemical intervention reproduces the diversity of experiences that make a life feel good. Well-being emerges from overlapping biological systems interacting with relationships, purpose, safety, activity and context. The six cups are a way to find where a life is thin, not a claim that six molecules run happiness. A pill that helps you is still worth taking, and it just does not have to be the whole story.
