The US Keeps Proving Iran's Regime Right

Two centuries of foreign control taught Iran exactly how to build a regime no outsider can topple.

The regime the US is fighting was engineered to survive foreign control. Every attack feeds the fear it was built on.

In August 1953, the shah of Iran fled the country. He had just tried to fire his prime minister, and the streets had answered for him: the coup had failed. Days later he was back in Tehran, and the prime minister was in prison. The CIA had spent the equivalent of 12 million dollars in today's money to make sure the country's most popular politician stayed gone.

That night in 1953 reads like a chapter from a history book. It is also the design document of the regime the United States keeps failing to beat. Iran's government is a survival machine rather than a normal dictatorship that can be decapitated with a few well-placed strikes. It was engineered, layer by layer, to withstand exactly the thing the US keeps doing to it.

The survival question

Before you ask what can break a system, ask what it was built to survive.

The question reads any institution the same way. Bank rules written after 2008 still force lenders to hold capital against a run that is not happening. The American constitution, drafted against a king, makes its own government deliberately slow to act. Each system carries the scar of the failure it fears most. Iran is the extreme case, because its regime was built from two centuries of the same humiliation and studied the pattern closely enough to design against it.

The pattern: a country sold from the outside

For most of its recorded history, Iran was the superpower of its region, the civilization at the center of the map. That memory matters, because the 1800s turned it into terrain. Qajar Iran sat between a Russia pushing toward warm-water ports and a Britain protecting the route to India. Neither empire wanted to occupy the country. It was too big and too mountainous to conquer cheaply, so they built a cheaper machine: control without occupation. Britannica

After defeating Iran in two wars, Russia forced the Gulistan and Turkmenchay treaties that stripped territory, gave Russian merchants immunity from Iranian law, and turned the Caspian into a Russian lake. Britannica Britain answered with the Great Game, a long contest for influence in the gap between empires. Both sides bought influence the same way, and the shah sold it. Railroads, banks, customs and telegraphs became concessions, exclusive rights paid for in cash.

A concession sounded technical. In practice it meant the shah sold pieces of the country's economic future to outsiders for cash and breathing room. The tobacco monopoly of 1890 turned that abstraction into an insult, because tobacco was part of daily social life. Merchants, clerics, and consumers mobilized, and the shah was forced to cancel the deal. Britannica Iranians learned that mobilizing together could reverse a foreign-backed arrangement. They pushed further in 1905, demanding a constitution, a parliament and limits on royal power. It became one of the first constitutional revolutions in the Middle East. Iranica Then Russia-officered troops shelled the parliament building, killed hundreds of lawmakers, and arrested the survivors. The image repeats in Iranian memory: a local ruler preserved by outside violence against the country's own democratic momentum.

Oil made the pattern impossible to escape

The discovery of oil in 1908 turned a strategic buffer into the fuel of an empire. The shah had sold exploration rights to a British businessman in 1901, and after six years of searching he found it. As the British Navy switched from coal to oil, Iranian petroleum became military infrastructure. The Anglo-Persian Oil Company, later BP, sat on more than a commodity. It sat on imperial reach, and the D'Arcy concession gave Iran only 16 percent of the profits while Britain controlled the machinery. Iranica

For Iranians, oil became proof that the country's wealth was being pumped out through a foreign-controlled valve. World War I arrived despite Iranian neutrality, and the fighting and famine killed millions. Britannica The empires kept redrawing the country's fate. In 1941 British and Soviet troops exiled a modernizing shah and installed his 22-year-old son, who was more willing to do what they wanted.

The hinge: Mosaddegh, oil, and the coup

Mohammad Mosaddegh understood the symbolism of oil better than anyone. Better terms were too small for the political moment. If Iran lacked control over the resource under its soil, then parliament and independence were decoration. In 1951, as prime minister, he nationalized the oil industry outright. Britain responded with a boycott, naval patrols, and pressure on Iran's economy. NPR

Iran would not budge, so Britain played its oldest trick: it convinced the CIA that Mosaddegh could hand the oil to the Soviets. Brand-new President Eisenhower wanted to counter Soviet influence anywhere on earth, and the CIA allocated the equivalent of 12 million dollars to get Mosaddegh out. NPR The first attempt failed, and the shah fled. Then the CIA flipped enough of the military, and Mosaddegh was arrested, tried for treason, and sentenced to life under house arrest. NPR

The coup is the hinge of this whole story. It told Iranians that elections, parliaments, and national control over resources could be reversed whenever the wrong asset changed hands. It told every future ruler that outside powers would remove any Iranian government they disliked. And it gave the revolution of 1979 the archive it would turn into armor: proof that the West would always try to choose Iran's future.

The machine the revolution built

The shah returned as the West's strongman, modernizing with oil money, American weapons, and a secret police trained with Western help. CRS The resentment underneath was visible to anyone listening. Khomeini fused religious authority with the anti-imperial archive, and his sermons traveled through mosques and bazaars because the message compressed many grievances into one target: the shah standing in for every older humiliation. In 1979 a coalition of shopkeepers, workers, students and clerics ended the monarchy. Khomeini built the state that still rules Iran today.

That state was designed as a reaction to everything the country had lived through. It kept elections, a parliament and courts, with a filtered democracy in which the decisive control points sit behind the ballot. The Supreme Leader stands above elected power, and the Guardian Council, appointed by him, vets every candidate and every law. CFR The regular military protects the borders, while the Islamic Revolutionary Guard Corps protects the regime itself. CFR The Guard runs its own ground forces, air force and navy, plus a volunteer militia embedded in every city and village.

The design gives the state redundancy: remove an official and another appears, pressure one institution and another absorbs the hit, protests can be brave and massive before colliding with a system built for internal siege. ACLED Iran watchers summarize the architecture in a dark shorthand: if a strike kills a hundred top officials, a hundred more are waiting to take their place.

The survival question, applied

Run the question over the last two years of war and the regime's choices stop looking irrational. Israel and the United States struck nuclear sites, killed commanders, and targeted the regime's leadership directly. The stated goal, repeated by Israeli leaders for decades, is toppling the regime once and for all. And the regime absorbs the blows the way it was designed to, because decapitation only works on systems that depend on a single head. Iran built two militaries precisely so no single head matters.

Sanctions follow the same logic as the strikes. They damage the economy and hurt ordinary Iranians, and the regime reads every bomb and every sanction as confirmation of its founding sentence: the West will never let Iran succeed, so Iran must stay hardened. CRS A regime built around survival studies which states get invaded and which states get negotiated with. Iraq had no nuclear weapons and was invaded. North Korea had them and was courted. From Tehran's reading of history, deterrence is the only insurance that has ever worked.

Iran is larger than its rulers

Iran is larger than the Islamic Republic, and the polls are unambiguous: roughly 80 percent of Iranians do not support the regime. Gamaan Woman, Life, Freedom showed another Iran, with women burning headscarves and students risking prison while the regime's violence exposed how afraid the state is of its own society. ACLED The tragedy is that Iran's rulers and Iran's outside enemies both keep trying to own that democratic impulse, and Iranians pay when either story wins too completely.

So the next time the US sanctions a bank, kills a commander, or signals regime-change intent, the useful question is which story inside Iran that action just made more persuasive. The regime is not hard to beat because it is strong. It is hard to beat because every attempt to beat it proves the story it was built on.
