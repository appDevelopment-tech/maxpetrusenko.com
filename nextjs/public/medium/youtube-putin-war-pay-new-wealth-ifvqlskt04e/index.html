The Cemetery Is Visible From Space. The Payments That Filled It Are Shrinking.

Signing bonuses of 1.6 million rubles and death benefits of 13 million built a Siberian boom. The budget records show the state paying less at both ends.

Russia pays its soldiers through a state price list: a signing bonus, a monthly salary, and a payment to the family when a soldier dies. One poor region in Siberia rebuilt its economy on those numbers, and the budget records of 2025 and 2026 show the state paying less at every step.

In Ulan-Ude, the capital of Russia's Buryatia region, the biggest money in town starts with a signature. A man who signs an army contract collects a one-time payment that climbed from 200,000 rubles to 1.6 million in about fifteen months. If he dies, the package paid to his family comes to roughly 13 million rubles, about $150,000. That is a life-changing amount in a place where it buys an apartment, a car, and a small business.

The advertising is everywhere. Billboards show a soldier in a blue beret under the slogan "Pride of Russia," and a government flyer shows an AI soldier beside the keys to a new flat, with the slogan "Service is caring for your family." The pitch runs on quotas handed down from Moscow (Meduza), and residents describe relentless pressure to sign (reporting).

The money is real. War pay lifted the poorest regions fastest, filled stores, and financed new construction. But the same price list that built the boom in Ulan-Ude is already being unwound. Through 2025 and 2026, regions across Russia started cutting the bonuses they advertised, and several began reducing the payments they make after a soldier dies.

The price list

Russia pays soldiers in three parts: a signing bonus, a salary, and a death payment widely known as coffin money. The federal one-time payment has been 400,000 rubles since a 2024 decree that officials have amended repeatedly (decree), with regions expected to add on top. Buryatia raced upward: 200,000 rubles from the republic at the start of 2024, then 500,000, then a million, then 1.5 million. By April 2025 the advertised offer, combining the federal payment, the republic's share, and business contributions, reached 1.6 million (Buryatia government). The signing bonus turns out to be the dominant reason men enlist, according to researchers who track the program (Q&A).

The monthly salary starts around 210,000 rubles, more than double the average Russian wage (CNN). The death package, federal and regional combined, runs to about 13 million rubles, or $150,000 (Foreign Policy), with the entitlement given as around 14 million rubles (reporting), while a broadcast tally puts the average near $130,000 (BBC).

Who supplies the men

The war pays a region like this one twice: when a man signs, and again when he dies. The men come disproportionately from the country's poorer edges. An analysis of open-source casualty data found that people from Buryatia, Tuva, and Chukotka were about 25 times more likely to die than Muscovites (analysis). A count published in 2023 by a foundation tracking the republic put its mobilized at more than 10,000 and said every 19th fallen mobilized soldier came from Buryatia (foundation). A tally kept by an independent outlet that follows the republic passed 5,000 deaths, and the expansion of one of the city's cemeteries is visible from space (reporting).

The recruiting pitch meets men who are already cornered. Researchers describe the typical signer as a man in debt or in trouble with the police (Q&A). One long report from a village on Kamchatka shows how that math works close up. In Sedanka, a family held out against accepting that a missing son was dead, then relented so it could receive the payments and clear almost a million rubles of debts at grocery stores. A second family lost the father, and a year later sent the eldest son to the front; he told relatives he wanted to repay a debt and shed a criminal record. In a third case, the remains that came home amounted to a fragment of a leg (reporting).

Money this large becomes something to fight over. A slang term has entered circulation for the practice: "black widows," women who marry soldiers for the payout. In one documented case, a woman who worked at a military enlistment office in the Bryansk region married a soldier, with court records later describing the marriage as one entered "to obtain potential financial benefits in the event of the husband's injury or death" (Foreign Policy). The same reporting describes an emerging cottage industry of operators circling the payments.

What the money builds

Replacement brands moved onto the main street: a Stars Coffee, a Lenta, the republic's first Burger King (opening). A wounded veteran used his combat pay to start a farm, and other veterans opened a nail salon and a pastry shop (reporting). The program works as a redistribution, from one part of society to another. The regions recruiting the most, and suffering the most casualties, are usually the poorer ones, so the money can make a huge difference there. Economists call the larger pattern war-induced convergence. A working paper at the Peterson Institute documents military spending pulling the income levels of Russia's regions closer together and warns about the cost of leaning on it (paper).

The same flows that lift one household can price another out. Real estate repriced upward with the war money, and the households that receive none of it pay the new prices (relay). Central bank data put inflation across Russia's macro-regions as high as 6.8 percent at the end of 2025, against 5.6 percent nationally (bank).

The ladder runs both ways

As 2025 ended, the budget records show the ladder stopped going up. At least eight regions cut their signing bonuses, with Samara dropping its offer from 3.6 million rubles to the federal floor of 400,000, and Tatarstan coming down to the same minimum (RFE/RL). In January, at least five regions sharply raised their payments again, in one case restoring a bonus of 2.1 million rubles (Moscow Times).

Then the cuts reached the other end of the pipeline. Since late 2025, regions have begun reducing or canceling the payments they make to the families of the dead, which had been stable through most of the war. One republic cut its payment from 2 million rubles to 100,000 in steps. Two regions halved theirs to 500,000. A third reduced a million to 50,000. A fourth abolished its payment outright (roundup). The math behind those cuts is straightforward. A death already brings a family more than 10 million rubles from the federal budget, and that money does political work. A widow and her children become rich while the neighbors who received nothing watch it happen. The resentment is real enough that state television runs segments about disputes over the payments. The analyst who maintains the bonus data calls the payouts effective at quelling anger that would otherwise build over time.

Hiring is slowing anyway. Just over 422,000 men signed contracts in 2025, about 6 percent fewer than in 2024 (Moscow Times), and by the end of August 2026 the year was running about 20 percent behind 2025 despite the highest payouts yet, with the average regional bonus at a record 1.65 million rubles and one estimate putting the cost per recruit at 1.8 to 1.9 million (roundup, estimate). To find men, regions are now paying finders: 56 regions offer rewards to recruiters, up from 31 a year earlier, and Smolensk raised its bounty from 115,000 rubles to 1 million per head (investigation).

The budgets explain the turn. The number of regions running deficits has almost quadrupled since 2021, to 73, and the finance ministry expects the consolidated regional deficit to widen by another 400 billion rubles this year, to 1.9 trillion (Re:Russia, Moscow Times). Sakha postponed pay raises for public-sector workers to cover recruitment costs, and Volgograd scrapped a youth festival so it could afford bigger bonuses (roundup).

Follow the payments

In a war economy, the payment data is the hardest part of the story to fake. Weapons claims can be inflated, casualties suppressed, and growth itself is partly war production priced at cost (analysis). But a budget line that pays a man to enlist, and pays his family after he dies, has to clear every month. Watch both ends of that line, and watch which budget does the paying. The shift from federal money to regional money, and now toward less of both, says more than any forecast.

The macro numbers are already bending in that direction. National growth slipped to 1 percent in 2025 from 4.9 percent in 2024 as fiscal stimulus faded (World Bank), while military spending climbed past 10 percent of GDP in the first half of 2026 (roundup).

If the war ends or the payments shrink further, places like Ulan-Ude face a problem with a plain name. The cash flow stops, and the costs it created do not. Analysts who study the region expect a local economic crisis in that scenario (relay).

Back in Ulan-Ude, the coffee shops and apartment blocks still stand over streets the war paid for, and the cemeteries keep growing. When the payments stop, the losses stay. The price list that built the boom is being rewritten, and the families who trusted it are first in line for the crisis the analysts expect.
