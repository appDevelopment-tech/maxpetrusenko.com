OpenAI’s Board Could Fire Its CEO in Five Days

The Sam Altman saga exposed a governance system built for an unusual mission

Sam Altman’s 2023 firing and return looked like a personality drama, but the five-day crisis revealed a structural problem. OpenAI combined a nonprofit board, a fast-growing commercial operation, and a mission designed to restrain concentrated power. The result was a governance mechanism that could act suddenly, then struggle to survive its own decision.

At noon on November 17, 2023, Sam Altman opened his laptop for what he expected to be a routine video call. On the other side was OpenAI’s board. Ilya Sutskever read a prepared statement: the board had lost confidence in Altman’s ability to lead, and his employment ended immediately.

Within minutes, the CEO was locked out of company systems. Employees began refreshing the same news tabs. Microsoft, OpenAI’s largest strategic partner, learned about the decision shortly before the public announcement. Four days later, Altman was back as CEO, with a reconstituted board.

The mechanism behind the whiplash was OpenAI’s unusual control structure. The OpenAI nonprofit says its mission is to ensure artificial general intelligence benefits all humanity. The nonprofit controlled a for-profit subsidiary, while outside investors and employees supplied capital and labor. That arrangement gave a small board authority over a company whose products, valuation, and partnerships had grown much faster than its constitutional design.

The useful question is therefore larger than why Altman was fired. It is how a governance system can make an extraordinary decision, lose the organization’s confidence, and still remain the formal guardian of its mission.

The control layer most people missed

OpenAI began in 2015 as a nonprofit research organization. In 2019, it created a capped-profit subsidiary to raise capital while keeping the nonprofit in control. OpenAI’s capped-profit announcement describes the 2019 creation of OpenAI LP. The arrangement tried to solve a real financing problem: frontier AI required resources that donations alone could not provide.

Three concrete examples show how that mechanism worked.

1. The nonprofit board held the mission mandate. OpenAI’s governance materials describe the nonprofit’s mission and structure. The authority was unusual for a technology company with major commercial partners.

2. The commercial arm could attract capital. Microsoft announced a multiyear, multibillion-dollar partnership with OpenAI and integrated its models into Azure. Capital and distribution expanded rapidly while ultimate control stayed outside a conventional shareholder board.

3. The mission was a constraint on the company’s upside. The capped-profit design promised investors a return ceiling, with additional value flowing to the nonprofit. OpenAI’s explanation of its structure presents that design as a way to balance investment with the public mission.

Each example is defensible on its own. Together they create tension. A board can have legal authority without having the operational capacity, information flow, or stakeholder legitimacy needed to exercise it smoothly.

What the board said, and what it did not say

The board’s initial statement said Altman had not been consistently candid in communications, hindering its ability to exercise responsibilities. OpenAI’s November 17 announcement named Mira Murati interim CEO.

The statement did not identify a specific incident. That gap mattered because governance depends on a chain of trust: directors need enough information to supervise management, employees need enough explanation to follow a transition, and partners need enough certainty to keep operating.

Contemporaneous reporting filled in fragments without producing one settled account. The New York Times reconstructed the board’s concerns. The Wall Street Journal reported on tensions around safety and commercialization. The Washington Post described the employee revolt and investor pressure.

The absence of a public explanation created a vacuum. Employees interpreted the removal through their own experience of the company. Investors and partners interpreted it through exposure to product and commercial risk. The board had exercised its power, but the organization did not accept the board’s account as sufficient.

Why the five-day reversal was rational

By November 18, Greg Brockman had resigned as board chair. By November 20, Microsoft CEO Satya Nadella announced that Altman and Brockman would join a new advanced AI team if they could not return.

Employees then signed a letter threatening mass resignation. The board’s formal authority remained real, but the practical cost of using it had become enormous.

The company eventually announced Altman’s return and a new initial board. OpenAI’s announcement about the new initial board described the transition. The reversal did not prove that the original decision was wrong, and it did not prove that Altman’s critics were right. It demonstrated a different point: authority without a credible coalition is fragile.

The reversal also clarifies why governance cannot be judged only by who wins. In a conventional public company, directors can replace a CEO while shareholders absorb the market reaction through a familiar chain of accountability. OpenAI’s arrangement distributed those consequences across a nonprofit mission board, employees whose careers were tied to the product, and a strategic partner whose cloud business was exposed to the decision. That is a concrete governance lesson: unusual control rights require unusually explicit procedures for using them.

The board had legal leverage, employees had collective leverage, and Microsoft had economic leverage. The five-day crisis was the collision of those three forms of power.

The governance test for frontier companies

OpenAI’s crisis also made the company’s outside dependencies impossible to ignore. Microsoft invested billions, supplied cloud infrastructure, and became a distribution partner. Microsoft’s annual report describes its relationship with OpenAI and the associated investment accounting. OpenAI’s compute needs made infrastructure a strategic constraint, while its models made Microsoft’s cloud a competitive platform.

The relationship created three governance tests.

First, mission claims need procedures. A board cannot protect a public-interest mission through private confidence alone. It needs clear escalation rules, minutes, independent review, and a communication plan for decisions that remove a chief executive.

Second, capital changes power even when it does not change legal control. Investors can influence an organization through financing, partnerships, talent markets, and credible alternatives. Commercial relationships can reshape competition and control.

Third, commercial scrutiny is expanding. The Federal Trade Commission announced an inquiry into generative AI investments and partnerships. Frontier-company governance now sits inside a wider debate over competition and control.

Sam Altman’s return was the earned payoff of the story’s central mechanism. The board could remove him. The employees, partners, and capital network could make that removal unsustainable. Both facts were true at once.

That is why the episode deserves more attention than a founder-versus-board drama. Frontier AI companies are building systems with consequences far beyond one firm, while their control arrangements remain experimental. OpenAI’s five-day crisis showed the cost of leaving that experiment implicit. If a small board is meant to hold the line on humanity’s behalf, everyone affected by its decisions needs to understand how that line is drawn.
