Your Body Wants Protein. The Bag Sells the Signal.

A serving of Doritos carries 150 calories and two grams of protein. The umami that makes it hard to stop eating is the taste of a meal it never delivers.

Doritos taste like dinner because they imitate the taste of protein. The imitation costs pennies. The protein it imitates does not, and the gap between the two is one of the most profitable tricks in the grocery store.

I build agents for a living, and one of them drafted this. The opinions and the edits are mine.

At 7:40 on a Tuesday morning, I eat Doritos with chopsticks. The chopsticks are practical. Orange dust and a laptop keyboard do not mix, and I am at the keyboard before I am fully awake.

I have also spent years arguing that American breakfast is dessert with a cereal box around it. Muffins, flavored yogurt, juice, granola: sugar stacked on sugar, sold as the most important meal of the day. The contradiction is not lost on me.

For a long time I filed this under personal weakness. Then I read the ingredient list, and the weakness turned out to be a design feature: the bag sells the taste of protein, but almost none of the protein itself.

What is actually on the chip

A Dorito is a triangle of corn masa, fried, then coated with dust. The manufacturer's label for Nacho Cheese puts one 28-gram serving, about twelve chips, at 150 calories, 210 milligrams of sodium, and two grams of protein. The dust is where the engineering lives. The ingredient list runs to roughly two dozen items and includes monosodium glutamate, disodium inosinate, and disodium guanylate.

Those last two are the tell. Inosinate and guanylate are nucleotides, the same molecules found in meat and dried fish, and they multiply the effect of glutamate rather than adding a flavor of their own. A formulator stacks them because the combination registers far more strongly than either does alone. The result is a snack that tastes like a roast, a broth, a piece of cheese, and a bag of chips at the same time.

Two grams of protein is eight calories. In a 150-calorie serving, protein accounts for about five percent of the energy. A chicken breast gets about three quarters of its calories from protein. The bag delivers the taste of the thing while delivering almost none of the thing.

The taste that means protein

In 1908, a Japanese chemist named Kikunae Ikeda was trying to explain why a broth made from kombu, a kind of kelp, tasted the way it did. He isolated the molecule responsible, glutamate, and argued that it deserved a place beside sweet, sour, salty, and bitter. He called the taste umami, and the FDA's own account of MSG credits him with the 1908 extraction and the patent that followed. The taste took most of a century to be formally accepted, and the receptor that detects it was cloned in 2002: a pairing of two proteins, T1R1 and T1R3, that responds to most of the twenty standard amino acids and not to their mirror images. That specificity matters. That specificity matters, because a detector built for protein building blocks would look exactly like this.

Glutamate and glutamine are the most abundant free amino acids in human milk, which is a strange sentence to put in a food article and a useful one. The first food most people taste is built around the molecule that Doritos use to make corn taste like dinner.

There is a catch in the chemistry. Glutamate locked inside a protein chain does not register as umami. Only the free form does, the glutamate released when proteins break down through cooking, aging, fermenting, or drying. Miso, parmesan, fish sauce, ripe tomato, cured meat: each is a way of freeing glutamate from protein before you eat it. A chip bag does the same thing industrially, with a shaker.

Why the bag beats the bowl

If umami is a protein signal, the next question is why a bag of chips beats a bowl of miso soup at seven in the morning.

Time is part of it, since miso needs dashi and dashi needs a pot and twenty minutes. A bag needs a thumbnail, and it keeps on a shelf for months.

The biology is the interesting part. Humans regulate protein intake more tightly than fat or carbohydrate. When protein is diluted in the food supply, appetite compensates by pushing total calories up, a mechanism called protein leverage. In an inpatient trial at the NIH, twenty adults spent four weeks on two diets matched for calories presented, sugar, sodium, fat, fiber, and macronutrients. On the ultra-processed menu they ate 508 more calories a day than on the unprocessed one, and gained weight. The extra calories came from carbohydrate and fat. Protein intake barely moved.

Then there is speed. A pooled analysis of 327 foods measured how fast calories disappear from a plate. Unprocessed foods delivered about 35 calories a minute. Ultra-processed foods delivered about 69. Twice the rate, which means the stop signal arrives after the second serving instead of during the first.

Put the three findings together and the bag stops looking like a character flaw. It is a delivery system tuned for a body that scans for protein, counts calories slowly, and has twenty minutes less than it used to.

The panic that hid the trick

Nobody had this argument about MSG for fifty years.

In April 1968, the New England Journal of Medicine published a short letter from a physician named Robert Ho Man Kwok describing numbness at the back of the neck, palpitations, and weakness after meals in Chinese restaurants. The letter was a question rather than a study. It became a syndrome, and the syndrome became a panic. The scare ran on tiny, unblinded experiments and a racial frame so obvious in retrospect that it is almost embarrassing. MSG had been in canned soup and frozen dinners for years without complaint, and it became a poison only when it was attached to Chinese food.

The regulators never found the poison. The FDA lists MSG as generally recognized as safe and notes that blinded studies have not consistently triggered reactions in people who identify as sensitive. In 2017 the European Food Safety Authority set a group acceptable daily intake of 30 milligrams per kilogram of body weight per day, a level derived from the highest dose with no observed adverse effect, divided by a hundred. East Asians eat roughly three times more MSG than Americans, and the syndrome does not track them.

So the safety fight ended, mostly, with a shrug. MSG is a seasoning, and the people who spent decades avoiding it were avoiding a molecule their own bodies manufacture and their own milk contains.

Which raises the question the panic never asked. If MSG is harmless, why is it in absolutely everything cheap?

The science historian Sarah Tracy has spent years on that question. Her dissertation on MSG and umami makes the argument plainly: MSG helps make unhealthy, cheap food taste good. The controversy was never the important part. What she found is duller and bigger than the panic: a powder can make refined corn paste taste like a meal, at a cost that lets someone sell it by the pallet.

The part where MSG is not the villain

Here the story gets less convenient, including for me.

MSG is not a marker of junk food. A 2020 sensory trial tested it in vegetable-forward dishes, roasted vegetables, a quinoa bowl, a savory yogurt dip, pork fried rice, and found it cut sodium by more than thirty percent, and by as much as sixty percent in one dish, while ordinary consumers liked the food just as much. If the goal is less salt in a diet that is mostly vegetables, MSG is a tool rather than a trick.

The word hijack is also doing more work than the evidence supports. The strongest critical review of the food-addiction model found fundamental weaknesses in the human neuroscience behind it. What the evidence supports is narrower and less cinematic. Some foods are easy to eat quickly, in large amounts, at low cost, and the modern food supply contains a lot of them. Easy is enough, and nobody needs a brain lesion to overeat a bag of chips.

So I am not arguing that a chemical is addictive or that a flavor is a drug. I am arguing something duller and more useful. The taste of protein has become decoupled from protein, and the decoupling is profitable.

The test I use now

I still eat Doritos. I am eating them now, in fact, which is the least scientific part of this piece.

What changed is the question I ask in the aisle. "Is this bad for me" has no useful answer at the shelf, so I ask a different one: what is this promising, and what does it deliver? A savory snack is making a protein claim. The label will tell you whether the claim is real. A meal making a protein claim backs it up. A snack imitating one does not, and two grams per 150 calories is the tell.

The practical version: for every 200 calories, look for at least 10 grams of protein. Doritos give you about 2.7. A bowl of miso with tofu and an egg clears it easily. Greek yogurt clears it. A handful of peanuts clears it. The rule is mechanical. It survives a bad morning. If a food shouts protein and cannot back it up, you will be hungry again in ninety minutes, and you will buy something else.

The second move is to feed the craving what it is actually asking for. The craving is for glutamate, salt, fat, and crunch, and for the protein those signals evolved to point at. Miso, parmesan, mushrooms, tomatoes, fish sauce, beans, eggs, and meat all deliver the signal with the substance attached. Dinner for breakfast is a better answer to a savory craving than a bag pretending to be dinner.

What the bag is really saying

The chip is not lying. It is answering a question your body has been asking for a very long time: where is the protein?

The answer is a triangle of corn with two grams of it, dressed in the taste of a meal. That is an extraordinary piece of engineering. It is also, at seven in the morning, an expensive way to stay hungry.

I finished the bag while writing this. The chopsticks are in the sink. Tomorrow I will probably do it again, and I would rather know what I am buying than pretend the bag is an accident.
