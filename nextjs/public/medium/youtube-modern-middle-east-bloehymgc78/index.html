America Built a Middle East It Can't Leave

A concession, a runway, a fleet, a war, a base that outlived its war. Every piece was reasonable on the day it was written, and no president has been able to dismantle the sum of them.

The American position in the Middle East was assembled one reasonable deal at a time: a concession, an airfield, a fleet, a war, a base that outlived the war it was built for. The bills came due decades late, so the pattern survived every administration that inherited it. Demand for the oil that started the arrangement is projected to plateau within the decade, and the arrangement itself is still running.

On March 3, 1938, a drilling crew on the Saudi coast hit oil at Dammam No. 7, and the strike turned a kingdom that had been a poor bet into the most valuable address in the energy business (EIA country analysis). The concession belonged to an American company, and what followed belonged, increasingly, to the United States.

By the end of the Second World War, an American president had met the Saudi king aboard a warship in the Suez Canal, and the outline of the trade was set (the meeting)). The company kept the oil, and the protection would be an American job.

The popular story about this region is a story about theft: distant diplomats drew lines in 1916, and everything since is described as the fallout. Historians have spent years taking that story apart (the myth). The record they leave behind is stranger and more useful. What makes this arrangement durable is also what makes it almost impossible to leave. Every piece of it was reasonable on the day it was written, yet the sum of the reasonable pieces is a structure that no president has been able to dismantle and no army in the region can remove. Watch what gets built, and ask what can ever leave. Announcements change with the weather, and runways do not.

An air field, never an air base

In 1945, the United States and Saudi Arabia signed the Dhahran Air Field Agreement, and the name carried deliberate modesty. Washington called the installation an "air field" rather than an air base because "base" sounded like empire, and the young kingdom was sensitive about exactly that (the record). For the next seventeen years, the United States Air Force built and operated the runway next to the oil company's town.

Each later step arrived with the same kind of reassuring label and an exit clause that never came due. When Britain and France invaded Egypt in 1956 to take back the Suez Canal, President Eisenhower forced them out and the United States quietly inherited their role as the region's outside guarantor (Suez). When Arab producers embargoed oil during the 1973 war and prices quadrupled, Washington answered by drawing closer: arms sales, guarantees, and a permanent naval conversation about the Gulf (oil embargo).

None of those decisions was dramatic on the day it was taken. Together they assembled a position: a fleet stationed in the Gulf, airfields in a widening arc, and a security promise that every government in the region learned to bill against. The security promise became the product, and the customers kept renewing.

The bill arrives late

In 1953, American and British intelligence helped remove Iran's prime minister because he had nationalized the country's oil, and Washington spent the next twenty-five years underwriting the monarch it restored (documents; history). The shah fell in 1979 anyway, and the revolution that replaced him defined American policy for the next four decades.

The bills came in smaller denominations too. In 1987, Kuwait asked Washington to protect its tankers from Iranian attacks, and the Navy reflagged them as American ships and escorted them through the Gulf (the escort operation). In July 1988 the cruiser Vincennes, one of the escorts, mistook an Iranian passenger jet for a diving fighter and fired, killing all 290 people aboard Iran Air 655. A Navy investigation blamed the situation rather than the captain, and he received a Legion of Merit at the end of his tour (the Navy's account).

Two facts from that summer stayed in the region's memory: 290 dead, and a medal for the captain.

Two years later, Iraq took Kuwait, and the United States returned at the head of a coalition of 35 nations, with half a million troops and a basing arrangement across the Saudi kingdom that outlasted the war (the Gulf War). The bases did what bases do. An American garrison in the land of Islam's two holiest cities became a grievance that a Saudi exile named Osama bin Laden turned into a war (profile). The attack he ordered on September 11, 2001, produced two more wars, and the case for the second one was built on intelligence that British officials privately described as fitted to the decision they had already made. "The intelligence and facts were being fixed around the policy," the minutes of a July 2002 meeting at 10 Downing Street recorded (the memo; the leak).

Willing instruments

Saddam Hussein used chemical weapons on the Kurdish town of Halabja in 1988, killing about 5,000 people (BBC). His campaigns against the Kurds that decade killed as many as 182,000, by the accounting of the Kurdish regional government (KRG record). When his army collapsed in 1991, Kurdish fighters rose up, asked for help, and did not get it. The uprising was crushed, and Washington watched until it eventually imposed no-fly zones over the north (in pictures).

The pattern outlived the Cold War it was built in. In 2017, Iraqi Kurds voted 92 percent in favor of independence on a 72 percent turnout (BBC). Baghdad refused, the neighbors refused, and almost nothing changed except the negotiating position. Two years later, when Washington pulled its small force back from the Syrian border, Turkey moved in within days and seized a 30-kilometer strip the Kurdish-led forces had been told to vacate (BBC; aftermath).

Fight now and move later is the region's oldest transaction, and it runs in both directions. Nobody in this story is purely a victim or purely a principal. Djibouti, 23,200 square kilometers of desert coast, rents its geography to a half-dozen foreign armies at once. A base beside the Red Sea is a product, and everyone pays rent (profile). The strongest military in the world keeps arriving through doors that local rulers hold open, which makes the locals less powerless than the map suggests and more practiced at the trade than they get credit for.

What stays when the mission ends

The machine is running in real time. The American-led mission against ISIS in Iraq and Syria is scheduled to end this month, and the reporting around it has the familiar shape: an official conclusion, a partial withdrawal, and a footprint that keeps its options (War on the Rocks).

The war that closed the Strait of Hormuz in February is in its seventh month. In peacetime, roughly 85 ships a day transited the strait; this month the daily count is around one, and oil trades near 98 dollars (straits data; shipping forum). Crews are negotiating danger money to cross, and the legal question of whether a closed strait makes every merchant ship a target remains open (the law). When the waterway that carries a fifth of the world's oil becomes a place ships cross one at a time, the American carriers stationed to keep it open are answering the only question that matters about the position: whether it can ever leave (context).

The next oil

The thing the whole arrangement was built to move is finally in question. The International Energy Agency projects oil demand plateauing toward 2030 rather than climbing forever, the first bend of its kind in the long forecast (IEA). The Gulf's answer is already under construction. Saudi Arabia is spending its accumulated oil money on a new city called Neom at a scale meant to replace what the wells provided. The land under it was not, in fact, empty.

In 2020, Abdul Rahim al-Huwaiti refused to let a land registry committee value his property inside the Neom footprint. He was shot dead a day later, during the clearance mission. At least 47 other villagers were detained after resisting evictions, and five of them are on death row (BBC investigation). The same deal is being offered again, land and capital and a future, and the arrival of the surveyors is where the consent gets tested.

The terms repeat because the machine repeats: a resource that needs protecting, protection that needs building, building that decides whose land it was, and a bill that lands decades later with somebody else's name on it. Eighty-eight years after the first well came in, the map has been redrawn again and again. The protection itself has never been redrawn, only expanded. Watch what gets built, because the construction is always the first honest document.
