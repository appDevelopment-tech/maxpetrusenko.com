Two Berlins, Two Feedback Loops

A market answers to buyers. A plan answers to reports. Berlin ran both, and the shelves showed it.

Karl Marx explained where surplus value comes from, then left the operational half unsaid: how does a society decide what to make? A market lets the shopping cart vote. A central plan hands a quota to a factory. When Berlin was split in two, it ran both systems at once, and the divergence showed up on the shelves and in how fast each side could admit it was wrong.

From Ownership to Transferable Claims

Land is finite, and scarcity is the core constraint every system has to manage. Two questions decide how: who owns what, and how is allocation decided? Markets require more than private possession; they require transferable claims, assets that can be bought, sold, pledged, and priced. Once ownership can transfer, resources can move toward whoever is willing to pay for them. That shift helped capital find its way to wherever it could be put to work, though it was hardly the only force behind it.

The Question Marx Left Open

In 1867, Karl Marx published the first volume of Das Kapital, a critique of capitalist production. Its central claim is that profit rests on unpaid surplus value: the worker creates more value than they are paid, and the gap is what the owner keeps. Marx wrote a diagnosis, not an operating manual. The day-to-day mechanics of deciding what to make were left to the systems that came after him, and the governments that later ran a central plan had to invent those mechanisms themselves.

When a shopper spends five dollars, the transaction broadcasts a real-time signal: make more of this, stop making that. Capital, machinery, and labor flow toward what sells and sit still on what lingers. The signal is imperfect, because it reflects willingness and ability to pay rather than need in the abstract, but it updates continuously and it comes from close to where the need is.

How a Plan Decides

In a planned economy, allocation flows down from a central quota rather than up from consumer behavior. The center decides what to make, and the signal travels down to the factory. In the Eastern Bloc this ran through a chain of institutions:

  • The Politburo set the priorities for five-year cycles.
  • The State Planning Commission mapped thousands of goods into physical ledgers.
  • The Office of Prices set what things should cost.
  • The Ministry of Trade and Supply allocated quotas across regions.
  • The state factories and retail produced and stocked assigned quantities regardless of demand.

Those names and responsibilities varied by country and period; this is the East German shape, not a single uniform model. Under Goodhart's law, once a measure becomes a target it stops being a good measure, so participants rationally optimize for the metric rather than the outcome. A factory measured by tonnage has an incentive to make heavier goods. A bakery measured by weight has an incentive to optimize weight over quality. A retailer with no customer-loss penalty has less reason to fight shortages.

What the Wall Split

The Berlin Wall went up on August 13, 1961, and created a vivid comparison: people with shared history and culture ended up inside two different economic systems. The two halves also differed in external support, trade access, migration, and geopolitics, so it was never a controlled experiment. By the time the wall came down, the differences showed up in ordinary goods:

  • Grocery shelves: accounts of everyday life describe East Berlin's stores stocking a narrow range with frequent shortages, while the West offered far more choice. Shoppers in the East often bought whatever happened to be available that day.
  • Cars: an East Berliner could buy a Trabant, which changed remarkably little over the years, while waiting lists could stretch past a decade and used cars sometimes cost more than new; the West offered dozens of models ready to drive away.
  • Health: by 1989 East German life expectancy ran a couple of years behind the West, and a 2024 study puts the gap around 2.4 to 2.6 years, showing it closed for women within about a decade and a half after reunification and narrowed sharply for men.

Why a Plan Cannot Hear the Cart

When local failure has to travel upward through a hierarchy, information can be delayed, softened, or distorted. The people closest to a shortage often know exactly what is wrong, while the people with authority receive an increasingly clean version of the system. Reporting a shortfall reflects on the quota the chain was ordered to hit, so the bad news gets filtered as it climbs, and the center ends up ruling a system that reports only its own success. The feedback a market gets from buyers is one of the first things to break down under a plan.

The Nordic Distinction

The Nordic model combines private ownership and market pricing with high taxes, collective bargaining, and broad social insurance. It is structurally different from Soviet-style central planning, and some systems people call socialist are really free markets with a large safety net. The distinction matters for the question at hand: welfare states still leave prices to buyers and let capital move, so they keep the feedback loop that a central plan removes.

The Correction-Speed Test

To evaluate the resilience of any economic, organizational, or administrative system, look past the labels and apply this test:

  • Origin: where does information about failure originate?
  • Transmission: can bad news reach the decision maker without a filter?
  • Incentives: does the decision maker benefit from hearing bad news?
  • Mobility: can resources and capital move when a decision is wrong?
  • Latency: how long does the correction cycle take?
  • Parallelism: can competing experiments run side by side to replace failed approaches?

A market answers these quickly, though its signals reflect willingness and ability to pay rather than abstract need, and external costs and concentrated power can break the loop just as surely as a quota can. A central plan answers them slowly, because local knowledge has to travel upward through administrative layers before resources can move back down.

So when someone hands you a tidy system, put the label aside. Ask who owns what and who gets to decide, then ask the harder question: when the person who decides is wrong, how does the system find out, and how quickly can it correct? The speed and accuracy of that error-correction loop is a major determinant of how a system performs, though it competes with luck, geography, resources, and the human beings inside it.
