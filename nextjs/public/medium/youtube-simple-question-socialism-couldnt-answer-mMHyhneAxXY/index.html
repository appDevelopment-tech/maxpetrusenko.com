Two Grocery Stores, Twenty Minutes Apart

One was stocked by shoppers with carts. The other was stocked by planners a thousand miles away.

Karl Marx explained where surplus value comes from, then left the operational half unsaid: how does a society decide what to make? A market lets the shopping cart vote. A central plan hands a quota to a factory. When Berlin was split in two, it ran both systems at once, and the divergence showed up on the shelves, on the waiting lists, and in life expectancy.

From Protection to Claims

Land is finite, and scarcity is the core constraint every system has to manage. Two questions decide how: who owns what, and how is allocation decided? Markets require more than private possession; they require transferable claims, assets that can be bought, sold, pledged, and priced. Once ownership becomes transferable, resources can move toward whoever values them enough to pay. That shift, from ownership as a promise to fight to ownership as a liquid claim, is what made capital possible.

The Question Marx Left Open

In 1867, Karl Marx published the first volume of Das Kapital, a detailed critique of capitalist production. The claim at its center is that profit stems from unpaid surplus value: the worker creates more value than they are paid, and the difference is what the owner keeps. Marx wrote a diagnosis, not an operating manual. He left the daily mechanics of economic calculation blank, so the governments that later tried to run the other side had to invent those mechanisms themselves.

When a shopper spends five dollars, the transaction broadcasts a real-time signal: make more of this, stop making that. Capital, machinery, and labor flow toward what sells and abandon what sits on the shelf. The signal is imperfect, because it reflects willingness and ability to pay rather than need in the abstract, but it updates continuously and it originates close to where the need is.

How a Plan Decides

In a planned economy, allocation flows down from a central quota rather than up from consumer behavior. The decision about what to make happens upstream, and the signal travels down to the factory. In the Eastern Bloc this ran through a real chain of institutions:

  • The Politburo set the priorities for five-year cycles.
  • The State Planning Commission mapped thousands of goods into physical ledgers.
  • The Office of Prices set what things should cost.
  • The Ministry of Trade and Supply allocated quotas across regions.
  • The state factories and retail produced and stocked assigned quantities regardless of demand.

Under Goodhart's law, once a measure becomes a target it stops being a good measure, so participants rationally optimize for the metric rather than the outcome. A factory measured by tonnage has an incentive to make heavier goods. A bakery measured by weight has an incentive to optimize weight rather than quality. A retailer with no customer loss as a penalty has less reason to fight shortages.

Twenty Minutes Apart

The Berlin Wall went up on August 13, 1961, and created a vivid natural comparison: populations with shared historical and cultural roots ended up inside radically different economic institutions. The two halves also differed in external support, trade access, migration, and geopolitical conditions, so it was never a controlled experiment. The comparison is still stark. By the time the wall came down, the divergence showed up in ordinary goods:

  • Grocery shelves: the Kaufhalle at Leninplatz carried roughly three thousand items with frequent shortages; a Bolle store was one of more than a hundred, and the shelves reached toward ten to fifteen thousand.
  • Cars: an East Berliner could buy a Trabant, which changed remarkably little compared with Western competitors, while waiting periods for new cars could stretch for years and used ones sometimes cost more than new; West Berlin offered dozens of models you could buy the same day.
  • Health: by 1989, East German life expectancy was about 2.4 to 2.6 years shorter than in the West, and a 2024 study found the gap closed for women within about a decade and a half after reunification and narrowed sharply for men.

Why the Plan Cannot Hear the Cart

When local failure has to travel upward through a hierarchy, information can be delayed, softened, or strategically distorted. The people closest to the shortage may know exactly what is wrong while the people with authority receive an increasingly clean version of the system. Reporting a shortfall reflects on the quota the chain was ordered to hit, so the bad news gets filtered as it climbs, and the center ends up ruling a system that reports only its own success. The feedback loop that tells a market what people actually need is the first mechanism to break down.

The Nordic Clarification

The Nordic model combines private ownership and market pricing with high taxes, collective bargaining, and broad social insurance. It is structurally different from Soviet-style central planning, and many of the countries people point to when they say socialism are really free markets with a large safety net. Keeping the two separate matters for the question at hand, because welfare states and central plans fail in different ways.

The Correction-Speed Test

To evaluate the resilience of any economic, organizational, or administrative system, look past the labels and apply this test:

  • Origin: where does information about failure originate?
  • Transmission: can bad news reach the decision maker without a filter?
  • Incentives: does the decision maker benefit from hearing bad news?
  • Mobility: can resources and capital move quickly when a decision is wrong?
  • Latency: how long does the correction cycle take?
  • Parallelism: can competing experiments run side by side to replace failed approaches?

A market answers these questions rapidly, though its signals reflect ability to pay rather than abstract need, and external costs and concentrated power can break the loop just as surely as a quota does. A central plan answers them slowly, because local knowledge has to travel upward through administrative layers before resources can move back down.

So when someone hands you a tidy system, put the label aside. Ask who owns what and who gets to decide, then ask the harder question. When the person who decides is wrong, how does the system find out, and how quickly can it correct? The speed and accuracy of that error-correction loop determines whether a system thrives or collapses.
