Two Berlins, Two Feedback Loops

A market answers to buyers. A plan answers to reports. Berlin ran both, and the shelves showed it.

In 1986 a West Berlin shopper on Tauentzienstraße could stand in a department store and choose among dozens of jams. Three miles east, an East Berlin Kaufhalle might have one brand of detergent and a decade-long wait for a Trabant. The split city ran two answers to one question at once: when the people deciding what to make are wrong, how does the system find out? A market hears it from buyers. A plan hears it from reports.

A Wall, Two Systems

The Berlin Wall went up on August 13, 1961, and split one city into two systems. People with shared history and culture ended up on different sides of a concrete line. The two halves also differed in external support, trade access, migration, and geopolitics, so it was never a controlled experiment. But the comparison was stark by the time the wall came down.

Consider the Trabant. An East Berliner could buy one, but the waiting list stretched past a decade, long enough that parents registered a newborn in the queue and hoped the car would arrive by the time the child was grown. The design barely changed for twenty-five years, because nothing rewarded a producer for improving it. A West Berliner could walk into a dealership on a Tuesday and drive out in a new car by Thursday.

The gap showed up in health too. By 1989 East German life expectancy ran a couple of years behind the West, and a 2024 study puts it around 2.4 to 2.6 years, narrowing for women within about a decade and a half of reunification. That number has limits as evidence. Reunification changed health care, income, migration, and reporting at once, so the gap closing is consistent with better access to care as much as with markets in the abstract.

The Question Marx Left Open

In 1867, Karl Marx published the first volume of Das Kapital, a critique of capitalist production. Its central claim is that profit rests on unpaid surplus value: the worker creates more value than they are paid, and the gap is what the owner keeps. Marx analyzed production and profit, but he offered far less on the day-to-day mechanics of deciding what to make. The governments that later ran a central plan had to engineer those mechanisms themselves.

A five-dollar purchase is a real-time signal: make more of this, less of that. The signal is imperfect, because it tracks willingness to pay rather than need, and a rich person's craving can outrank a poor person's hunger. But it updates in real time and it comes from the ground, not the penthouse.

How a Plan Decides

In a planned economy, allocation flows down from a central quota rather than up from consumer behavior. The center decides what to make, and the signal travels down to the factory. In the GDR this ran through a real chain of institutions:

  • The Politbüro set the priorities for five-year cycles.
  • The Staatliche Plankommission mapped thousands of goods into physical ledgers.
  • Retail prices were administered by price-setting authorities.
  • The Ministry for Trade and Supply allocated quotas across regions.
  • The state factories and retail produced and stocked assigned quantities.

Names and responsibilities shifted by period, but this was the East German shape. In an HO or Konsum store, a central quota translated into aisles that sat still for weeks, then a surprise glut when a shipment finally landed. The detergent you needed was missing for a month, then it was the only brand on the shelf.

Under Goodhart's law, once a measure becomes a target it stops being a good measure, so people rationally optimize for the metric rather than the outcome. A factory measured by tonnage is rewarded for making fewer, heavier goods. A bakery measured by weight is rewarded for denser bread. A retailer with no customer-loss penalty has little reason to fight a shortage. The point of the measurement distorts what gets made.

Why a Plan Cannot Hear the Cart

When local failure has to travel upward through a hierarchy, information can be delayed, softened, or distorted. A West Berlin grocer could see a spike at the register by mid-morning and reorder that afternoon. An East Berlin manager who saw the same empty shelf could only file a shortfall report up the chain and wait weeks for a revised allocation.

Reporting a shortfall reflects on the quota the chain was ordered to hit, so the bad news gets filtered as it climbs. A manager who admits a miss isn't reporting a problem; they're reporting their own failure. The shortage gets massaged into a temporary transport issue, then a distribution irregularity, then nothing at all by the time it reaches the center. The buyer's signal is the first thing to go.

The Correction-Speed Test

The difference between the two Berlins was never a difference in talent, work ethic, or cultural will. It was an asymmetry in how quickly each side could discover it was wrong. To measure that in any system, look past the labels and ask:

  • Where does information about failure start? In the West, at the register. In the East, in a monthly plan-fulfillment report.
  • Can bad news reach the decision maker unfiltered? A shopkeeper's reorder arrived the same day; a manager's report climbed through layers that each had a reason to soften it.
  • Does the decision maker benefit from hearing the bad news? A market punishes the slow. A quota rewards the report that hits the number.
  • Can resources move when a decision is wrong? West Berlin shelf space shifted to whatever sold. East Berlin stock sat where the plan put it.
  • How long is the correction cycle? Afternoon in the West. Quarters in the East.
  • Can competing experiments run side by side? A market runs thousands at once. A plan runs one.

A market answers these quickly, though its signal is narrow. It measures purchasing power, not virtue, and external costs and concentrated power can break the loop just as surely as a quota can. A central plan answers them slowly, because local knowledge has to travel upward through administrative layers before resources can move back down.

So when someone hands you a system that looks elegant on a whiteboard, that is exactly when to start asking about its failures. Ask who owns what, who gets to decide, and then the only question that matters: when that decision maker is wrong, and they will be, how quickly will they find out?

Berlin made the answer visible. A West Berliner could punish a bad decision by walking to another aisle. An East Berliner could only file a report and wait. The Wall is gone, but the lesson is still standing there on the shelves: the speed at which a system hears its own mistakes is the design.
