Everyone Says Democracy Is Dying. Ask Which of the Five They Mean.

Electoral, liberal, egalitarian, participatory, deliberative: five traditions share one word, and the argument over the 74 percent number is an argument about which one counts.

Democracy is five traditions sharing one name: electoral, liberal, egalitarian, participatory, deliberative. The numbers that say democracy is retreating only make sense once you ask which of the five a country is losing.

In March 2026, a research institute in Gothenburg released its tenth annual count of the world's democracies. The headline number landed like a verdict: 74 percent of humanity, roughly six billion people, now live under autocracy (V-Dem Institute). The result was read as proof of a global collapse (The Guardian). It also landed inside two older fights: whether the United States was ever a democracy to begin with (Vox), and whether the measurements can settle anything at all (Cambridge University Press).

All three readings are downstream of one problem. Democracy is the name five distinct traditions have been fighting over since Athens, and each tradition scores a country by a different test. The V-Dem Institute, the group behind the March count, made the separation explicit a decade ago, grading every country on electoral, liberal, egalitarian, participatory, and deliberative principles at once because the single word kept collapsing under everything people wanted it to mean (V-Dem paper).

The problem is that the traditions disagree about what a democracy is even for. One asks whether power can be voted out. Another asks whether the majority can be limited. A third asks who actually gets heard above the noise of money. When two people argue about whether democracy is dying, they are usually scoring the same country with different tests, and the argument cannot resolve until somebody names the test they are using.

Five traditions, one word

Ask a thousand people whether they support democracy and nearly all say yes. Ask what the word means and the consensus falls apart. A study in Perspectives on Politics surveyed voters and found that support for democracy rises with conceptual complexity. The people who define it in richer terms, elections plus civil liberties plus participation, back it more strongly than those who reduce it to voting (Cambridge University Press).

Each tradition answers a question the others leave open:

  • Electoral. Leaders are chosen in competitive elections, and losing power stays normal.
  • Liberal. Limits on power protect rights and minorities, even against majorities.
  • Egalitarian. Material equality and equal influence matter, so money must not drown out voice.
  • Participatory. Citizens engage beyond the ballot, in meetings, marches, and local institutions.
  • Deliberative. Decisions follow informed discussion, where reasons have to survive scrutiny.

Those five lines are a working definition. Most fights about democracy are arguments over which line gets the most weight.

The thinnest test: elections

Elections are the tradition everyone can point to. North Korea runs them with a single approved candidate in each of its 687 districts (Library of Congress). East Germany held its first competitive election in March 1990, months before it dissolved (Bundestag). A ballot matters, and a ballot alone settles little.

That lesson now has its own category. The largest bucket in the 2026 count is electoral autocracy. These are countries with real elections and no real contest, where voters can disapprove of the government and the government keeps winning anyway. More people live in closed autocracies, 2.3 billion, than in electoral and liberal democracies combined, 2.2 billion (V-Dem Institute).

Watching that category has become its own political fight. One comparison circulating after the report set India's standing against competing indexes: V-Dem labels it an electoral autocracy, 106th of 179, while the Economist Intelligence Unit and Freedom House draw their lines elsewhere (X post). Same country, same year, different labels. The labels are where the argument lives.

Rights and limits

The liberal tradition bolts restraints onto the machinery: courts, constitutions, a free press, protected minorities, all designed to hold even a determined majority.

Its folklore is better known than its arguments. "Democracy is two wolves and a lamb voting on what to have for lunch," the saying goes, often pinned on Benjamin Franklin, who never said it. The earliest versions trace to 1990, including a Usenet post and a Los Angeles Times column (Barry Popik). Winston Churchill's supposed crack about the average voter has no evidence behind it either; the Churchill archive lists it among the famous quotes he never said (International Churchill Society). The quotes are bogus, and the worry underneath them is real. James Madison, arguing for the Constitution, wondered whether majority rule could survive its own majorities: even if every Athenian citizen had been a Socrates, he wrote, every Athenian assembly would still have been a mob (Federalist 55).

When people argue about whether a country is still liberal, they are arguing about restraints, and restraints are always unpopular with whoever holds the votes.

The money problem

The egalitarian tradition asks who the system actually listens to. It treats equal ballots as a starting point and points at money, class, and access as the real distribution of power.

The American version of the question runs through campaign finance. The Federal Election Commission registers super PACs as committees that "may receive unlimited contributions from individuals, corporations, labor unions and other political action committees" (FEC). In the 2024 cycle, donor Timothy Mellon gave more than $150 million to political organizations, and Elon Musk directed at least $250 million to backing one candidate (NBC News, PBS News).

Spending is only the visible layer. Research collected at Berkeley on political inequality documents how the views of the wealthy and organized groups reach lawmakers more reliably than the views of everyone else, a gap the authors trace to unequal voice (Goldman School of Public Policy).

None of this requires a conspiracy. Unlimited money plus unequal access is a design outcome, and the tradition's charge is that a country can hold flawless elections and still fail this test.

Participation beyond the ballot

The participatory tradition counts what happens between elections: town meetings, protests, union halls, school boards, the unglamorous machinery that the chant "this is what democracy looks like" compresses into six words.

The friction is in the fine print. In May 2025, voters in Wayne County, Ohio, faced a ballot question that read, in part: "Authorize the state to issue bonds or other obligations to finance or assist in financing public infrastructure capital improvements for local governments and other governmental entities" (sample ballot). Participation asks citizens to decode that on a Tuesday.

Participation also has a class gradient. A study of meeting minutes from 97 cities and towns around Boston found that the people who show up to planning and zoning meetings are older, likelier to be male, longtime residents, and homeowners (Cambridge University Press). The town meeting was never a cross-section of the town, and the people missing from the room are usually the ones with the least spare time.

The stupid-voter objection

Say democracy and someone eventually says it: people are stupid. The evidence is thinner than the insult and stranger than expected.

In a 1980 survey experiment, researchers asked respondents about the "1975 Public Affairs Act." No such law existed. About a third of them still offered an opinion on whether it should be repealed (Public Opinion Quarterly). It remains one of the cleanest demonstrations that poll answers can be artifacts.

The scholarly version of the insult has flags. Bryan Caplan's The Myth of the Rational Voter argues that voters process politics irrationally, grading policies by feel and tribe (Cato Unbound). Jason Brennan proposes weighting votes by competence, an epistocracy in which the least informed count least (Vox). The challenge is serious. If opinions about imaginary laws are real, what should anyone trust a poll to measure?

The deliberative tradition answers with a question of its own. What happens when people are given information, time, and a stake?

Talking as a technology

Ireland tested the answer at national scale. In 2016, the government asked a research firm to recruit a representative sample of 99 citizens for a Citizens' Assembly, with substitutes on call (Citizens' Assembly). They met over months, heard experts, and voted. Their recommendations preceded a 2018 referendum that changed the country's abortion law.

The most careful American experiment points the same way. In "America in One Room," a representative sample of voters spent a weekend deliberating on policy, and dissatisfaction with democracy dropped 18 points overall, including 31 points among Republicans (Stanford Deliberative Democracy Lab).

The record gets messier. Ireland's assemblies have been criticized as tools politicians use to delay and outsource hard calls; POLITICO's postmortem argues the assembly on abortion "worked because it suited politicians," who could delay a hard issue and outsource it to an unelected body (POLITICO). And after voters rejected two assembly-backed referendum questions by 67 percent and 74 percent in March 2024, a Dublin City University politics professor warned that the process had been "captured" by NGOs, producing what he called unrepresentative deliberations (The Guardian). A room of citizens is a tool, and tools get used.

You cannot max out all five

The five traditions do not stack into a tidy hierarchy. Turn up one and others move.

Direct democracy deepens participation and can collide with liberal restraints, since majorities do not reliably protect minorities. The movement for sortition, choosing citizens by lottery, presses the case against electoral primacy: its advocates argue that random selection opens decision-making to people that elections filter out, and that you cannot buy your way into a fair lottery (Sortition Foundation). Egalitarian goals about money in politics run into liberal rules about speech and association, so campaign finance regulation stays a thicket of constitutional doctrine.

Every working system is a blend that leans on some traditions and tolerates the weak spots of others. Politics is the argument over the mix, and the argument reopens every generation, because the mix can get better or worse but it never finishes.

How to read the next headline

So when the next headline says democracy is in retreat, start smaller. Ask which of the five is slipping.

If it is electoral, watch how elections are administered and how candidates get on the ballot. If it is liberal, watch courts, press freedom, and whichever group the majority would rather silence. If it is egalitarian, follow the money. If it is participatory, look at who shows up, and who can afford to. If it is deliberative, ask whether any institution in your country still rewards a good-faith reason.

The count from Gothenburg will keep moving. The five traditions will keep colliding, because they answer different questions about power and no set of institutions satisfies all of them at once. Every generation re-argues the mix, and the discipline that pays is coming back to one question. Which democracy is meant? The answer decides what is really being lost, and what is actually worth defending.
