Something Is Jamming GPS Over Europe. The Clue Was in Space

A Veritasium investigation follows Todd Humphreys from continent-wide signal drops to Cosmos 2546. GPS is weak radio timing infrastructure, and operators need a fallback.

GPS jamming works because satellite navigation depends on extremely weak timing signals from distant clocks. A receiver compares when several satellite signals arrive, then calculates position and time. A stronger transmission in the same band can drown out that timing data and turn a precise system into noise.

The blue dot feels solid because the interface is solid. You press a button, the map centers itself, the car reroutes, the plane descends, the delivery arrives, and the timestamp agrees with the tower timestamp. Nobody has to think about the radio physics under the glass.

That physics is fragile in a way the interface hides. A phone listens for satellite signals that have crossed roughly 20,000 kilometers of space and arrived almost absurdly weak. Ramsey Faragher compares the received power to an old light bulb two Earth diameters away. The signal still guides aircraft and synchronizes infrastructure because the clocks are excellent and the math is relentless. It has no armor against enough noise in the right band.

A stronger signal can leave the satellites untouched and make the receiver's world too loud to hear them. Modern life treats GNSS like a utility, even though the useful signal reaches Earth more like public hearing than a protected wire.

A continent heard the same drop

In November 2024, University of Texas at Austin GPS researcher Todd Humphreys received a narrow tip. Check two exact times in public data collected years earlier by GNSS reference stations. Humphreys and Zach Clements looked, found the times, and saw receivers across a wide network drop at the same moment.

The measured signal-to-noise ratio fell by roughly a factor of ten. Looking backward, they found 75 days since 2019 with similar disruptions. The arXiv paper, Chasing Lightning, describes "scores of powerful transient wide-area interference events over continental Europe, Greenland, and Canada since 2019" and identifies the likely source as a constellation of Russian early-warning satellites in Molniya orbits. The paper has been submitted for journal review, so the right posture is attention with the temperature kept low (https://arxiv.org/abs/2606.03673).

The early map suggested Poland or Kaliningrad, an area already associated with electronic warfare and GNSS disruption. The geometry made that too simple because a ground source has terrain, curvature, and altitude limits. The disruptions appeared across a huge footprint at the same time, including Svalbard and westward receivers as far as Canada.

GPS is a timing system that happens to draw maps

GPS, Galileo, GLONASS, BeiDou, and related systems sit under the broader name GNSS, Global Navigation Satellite Systems. A receiver solves position by comparing arrival times from multiple satellites. Each satellite tells the receiver where it was and when the signal left, and the receiver compares that with when the signal arrived. Time becomes distance, then distance becomes the dot on the map.

The scale creates the weakness inside the magic trick. A timing error of 100 nanoseconds throws position off by about 30 meters. Atmospheric delay and relativity both matter, and the transcript puts the received GPS signal near 10^-16 watts, a number so small that "signal" almost feels like bravado. Jamming exploits that asymmetry by making the receiver hear something louder than the satellite clocks.

Aviation makes GNSS failures visible because aircraft report them, cross borders, and fly high enough to keep line of sight to interference. The dependency reaches further into cell towers, financial systems, logistics networks, ports, emergency response, mapping apps, and ordinary phones. The map app is only the surface of a shared timing dependency.

A September 2025 Associated Press report carried by PBS said European Commission President Ursula von der Leyen's plane was hit by GPS jamming on a flight to Bulgaria, and it cited Baltic and Nordic warnings about growing interference from Russia. Latvia recorded 820 satellite-signal interference cases in 2024, up from 26 in 2022. Those figures do not assign every event to the same source. They show that satellite-navigation interference has moved from background noise to operational reality (https://www.pbs.org/newshour/world/what-to-know-about-russias-gps-jamming-of-a-european-officials-plane).

The schedule ruled out accident

The events clustered on Tuesdays, Wednesdays, and Thursdays during European business hours. That timing weakens the failing-amplifier explanation without proving anything about motive. An amplifier that misbehaves only during business hours in Europe on Wednesdays is suspicious, not explained.

Three possibilities remained on the table: a terrestrial jammer near Kaliningrad or the Baltic; a faulty or misbehaving satellite transmitting in the wrong band; or a space-based source timed, repeated, and possibly tested. The familiar answer was ground jamming. A misbehaving satellite was technically plausible. The space-based answer carried the broadest consequence because the failure can sit between a weak signal, a crowded spectrum, receiver assumptions, and a political actor operating near plausible denial.

The suspect list moved into orbit

The team narrowed candidates by asking which satellites were visible to the affected stations during multiple events. Geostationary satellites were an early suspect because one can see much of Earth from about 36,000 kilometers up. Requiring repeat visibility across events shrank the list to 14 possible suspects.

One Algerian satellite looked good on paper because it could transmit in the right band and was visible to the stations. The details got inconvenient once distant stations showed it barely above the horizon and receivers tracking it recorded the same signal drop as ordinary navigation signals. It was another victim in the noisy room, not the source.

The researchers needed raw radio data because standard receiver outputs were too clean, too simplified, and too slow. Each burst lasted only three to five seconds, while standard measurements arrived about once per second. The standard data could show an event, but it was too blunt to locate the source precisely.

Two stations, Trondheim and Amsterdam, eventually captured the same event at high resolution. By comparing the tiny time difference between arrivals at the two stations, the researchers could define a geometric surface in space, a hyperboloid, where the source had to be. The margin was only meters thick across tens of thousands of kilometers.

Humphreys and Clements ran their analyses separately for a week, compared notes, found disagreement, and found a code error. The final match mattered because it survived the thing that ruins plenty of technical certainty: wanting your own result to be right.

The candidate that stayed aligned was Cosmos 2546, a Russian satellite launched in 2020. It tracked the hyperboloid within the uncertainty of public orbital data. Cosmos 2546 cannot explain events before its launch in May 2020, but it belongs to a broader Russian early-warning constellation in Molniya orbits. The arXiv abstract says the paper uses received-power and time-difference-of-arrival techniques to identify that constellation as the interference source (https://arxiv.org/abs/2606.03673).

Orbital altitude changes the threat model. A jammer on the ground has geography working against it. A source in space has line of sight to a continent.

The map is already noisy

Most public GPS interference maps are built from aircraft reports. GPSJam.org visualizes degraded navigation by aggregating aircraft data, which is useful because planes become sensors. It is incomplete because it shows the world through systems that report their own navigation quality (https://gpsjam.org/?lat=55.22483&lon=17.23509&z=5.8&date=2024-10-14).

Spire's 2025 Baltic report makes the same point from a commercial satellite-data angle. In an October 2024 case around the Baltic Sea, Spire described GNSS disruption near Kaliningrad and confirmed aircraft navigation impacts from satellite-based monitoring. It also reported that Polish researchers and GPSPATRON detected 84 hours of GNSS interference over six months, with position errors exceeding 30 meters in cited maritime reporting (https://spire.com/blog/space-reconnaissance/gnss-interference-report-russia/).

Each layer does a different job in the evidence stack. Aircraft show navigation failures in flight, ground stations show signal behavior at fixed receivers, satellite data can reveal patterns that heat maps miss, and raw radio captures can turn a mystery into a source hypothesis. Jamming and spoofing live in the ambiguity between those layers, so the honest claim has to stay narrower than the scary one.

The narrow claim is still large enough to matter. Satellite navigation has become contested infrastructure. A map can show disruption while leaving intent open, a diplomatic statement can blame a state while hiding methods, a research paper can identify a likely source while still needing peer review, and an aircraft can lose reliable GPS and land safely using other systems.

The dependency is older than the app

The consumer version of GPS is that it tells you where you are. GNSS gives machines a shared sense of time and position, and modern systems quietly built themselves around that gift. Once a free, precise, global signal existed, engineers absorbed it into aviation procedures, port operations, cell network synchronization, financial timestamps, logistics, and phones.

The dependency then disappeared into normal operation. Dana Goward, president of the Resilient Navigation and Timing Foundation, calls GPS an invisible utility supporting virtually every technology. An invisible utility is hard to defend, and nobody owns the full map of dependence.

Treat GNSS as an input with a trust state. For aviation: redundant navigation, pilot training, terrestrial backups, and procedures that still work when the blue line lies or disappears. For maritime operators: radar, charts, inertial systems, landmarks, and crews who remember that GPS is a tool rather than the sea itself. For software and infrastructure people: ask one boring question before the crisis hits. What breaks if accurate time or position becomes untrusted for one hour, one day, or one region? Systems that degrade gracefully survived past failures. Fragile ones assumed the whisper would always be there.

Audit where the whisper enters

A GNSS resilience audit starts with six questions that are boring on purpose:

1. What parts of the system require accurate position? 2. What parts of the system require accurate time? 3. Which alerts detect jamming or spoofing instead of logging only downstream weirdness? 4. Which fallback works during GNSS loss? 5. Who has permission to declare GNSS untrusted? 6. How often is the fallback tested under realistic failure instead of documented once and forgotten?

Russia, Europe, Cosmos 2546, and Molniya orbits are the evidence in this story. The wider lesson is older and more practical. GPS became a gift, then a habit, then a hidden assumption. Near-perfect uptime made the risk feel theoretical.

The public claim to watch is the Chasing Lightning paper's space-based attribution as it moves through review. The practical claim can move before journal publication finishes. If your system needs position or time, find the GNSS assumption, give someone authority to distrust it, and test the fallback before you need it.
