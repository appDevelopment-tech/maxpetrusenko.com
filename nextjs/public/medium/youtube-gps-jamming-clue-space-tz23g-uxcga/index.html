Something Is Jamming GPS Over Europe. The Clue Was in Space

A Veritasium investigation follows Todd Humphreys from weird signal drops to Cosmos 2546, and the uncomfortable lesson is simple: modern life depends on a whisper that enough power can drown out. (“Something is jamming GPS over Europe. Here's what we found”) (Max Petrusenko) (Cryptobase) (Medium) (Twitter) (Claude Code: The AI Developer's Secret Weapon)

GPS jamming works because satellite navigation is built on extremely weak timing signals. A receiver listens to distant clocks, compares arrival times, and calculates position. A stronger signal on the same band can drown out the timing data, turning a miracle of precision into noise.

The strange thing about GPS is that it feels solid because the dot on the map is solid.

You press a button, the blue dot appears, the car reroutes, the plane descends, the delivery arrives, the bank timestamp agrees with the tower timestamp, and nobody thinks about the physics holding the whole thing together.

The physics is much more fragile than the interface.

A phone is listening for satellite signals that have crossed roughly 20,000 kilometers of space and arrived as a whisper. In the Veritasium source that sparked this piece, Ramsey Faragher compares the power at the receiver to an old light bulb two Earth diameters away. The signal can still guide aircraft and synchronize infrastructure because the clocks are absurdly good and the math is patient. The weakness is that patience gives no armor.

Enough noise in the right slice of spectrum turns navigation into a guessing problem.

That is the uncomfortable frame. GPS is treated like a utility. Technically, it is closer to public hearing.

A continent heard the same silence

The story starts with a boring-looking dataset.

In November 2024, University of Texas at Austin GPS researcher Todd Humphreys received a tip: check two exact times in data collected years earlier by GNSS reference stations. Those stations continuously record the strength of satellite navigation signals against background noise. Humphreys and Zach Clements looked at the moments and saw receivers across a wide network drop at the same time.

The measured signal-to-noise ratio fell by roughly a factor of ten, according to the transcript and the later research paper. Looking backward, the researchers found 75 days since 2019 with similar disruptions. The published arXiv paper, Chasing Lightning, describes “scores of powerful transient wide-area interference events over continental Europe, Greenland, and Canada since 2019” and identifies the likely source as a constellation of Russian early-warning satellites in Molniya orbits, while noting the work had been submitted for journal review (https://arxiv.org/abs/2606.03673).

That last clause matters. The claim is strong, but the paper is still moving through formal review. The right posture is attention, with the temperature kept low.

The early pattern looked like ground interference. The apparent center sat around Poland or Kaliningrad, an area already associated with electronic warfare and GNSS disruption. A normal reader can stop there and feel the story is solved: Russia, war, jammers, Baltic region.

The data killed the simple version.

If the source were on the ground, receivers would see a geometry that made sense from the ground. The disruptions instead appeared across a huge footprint at the same moment, including far-north stations such as Svalbard and westward receivers as far as Canada. The source seemed too high.

The first hard lesson is that an infrastructure failure can look local from the map and global from the physics.

GPS works because satellites whisper accurately

GPS, Galileo, GLONASS, BeiDou, and related systems sit under the broader name GNSS, Global Navigation Satellite Systems. A receiver solves position by comparing the arrival time of signals from multiple satellites. The satellite tells the receiver where it was and when the signal left. The receiver compares that to when the signal arrived. Time becomes distance.

That sounds clean until you notice the scale.

A few nanoseconds of timing error can matter. Atmospheric delay matters. Relativity matters. The signal itself is tiny by the time it reaches a phone, aircraft, ship, or timing receiver. The Veritasium transcript puts the received GPS signal near 10^-16 watts, a scale that makes “signal” feel like too confident a word.

Jamming exploits that asymmetry. It can leave the satellite untouched and make the receiver’s world louder than the satellite’s whisper.

This is why GNSS interference reaches beyond aviation. Aviation makes the failures visible because aircraft report them, cross borders, and sit at altitude with line of sight to jammers. But GNSS also provides time. Financial systems, cell towers, logistics networks, ports, delivery systems, mapping apps, emergency response, and ordinary phones all lean on satellite timing and positioning in ways users rarely see.

The dependency graph is bigger than the map app.

A September 2025 Associated Press report carried by PBS said European Commission President Ursula von der Leyen’s plane was hit by GPS jamming on a flight to Bulgaria, and it cited Baltic and Nordic warnings about growing interference from Russia (https://www.pbs.org/newshour/world/what-to-know-about-russias-gps-jamming-of-a-european-officials-plane). The same report said Latvia recorded 820 satellite-signal interference cases in 2024, up from 26 in 2022. Those figures do not give every event the same source. They show that the background noise around satellite navigation has become foreground.

The clue was the schedule

A failing amplifier rarely respects office hours.

One of the details that pushed the investigation away from accident was timing. The interference events clustered on Tuesdays, Wednesdays, and Thursdays during European business hours. That leaves motive open, but it pressures the random-failure explanation. The source behaved like a system being operated.

That single observation changes the emotional temperature of the story.

A technical glitch is annoying. A repeated, wide-area signal in protected navigation bands is a capability. If the events are testing, they are a rehearsal. If they are some other transmission that happens to land in navigation bands, they are still evidence that the spectrum can be made hostile at scale.

The researchers had to separate three possibilities:

  • a terrestrial jammer around Kaliningrad or the Baltic region
  • a faulty or misbehaving satellite transmitting in the wrong band
  • an intentional or semi-intentional space-based source that could be tested, timed, and repeated

The first answer was familiar. The second answer was technically plausible. The third answer was the one nobody wanted to be true.

Here the piece becomes less about GPS and more about modern dependency. We like infrastructure that fails inside a category we already understand. A road closes. A cable snaps. A server goes down. A satellite-navigation failure is stranger because the break can happen in the relationship between a weak signal, a crowded spectrum, a receiver’s assumptions, and a political actor testing the edge of plausible denial.

The suspect list moved into orbit

The investigation narrowed the candidates by asking which satellites were visible to the affected stations during multiple events. Geostationary satellites were an early suspect because one can see a large portion of Earth from about 36,000 kilometers up. The list shrank to 14 possible suspects.

One candidate, an Algerian satellite, looked good on paper. It could transmit in the right band and was visible to the stations. Then the details became inconvenient. Some distant stations saw it barely above the horizon. More importantly, when receivers tracked that satellite during events, its signal dropped in the same way as the ordinary navigation signals. It looked less like the attacker and more like another victim in the same noisy room.

The researchers needed raw radio data. The usual receiver outputs were too clean, too simplified, too slow. Each interference burst lasted only three to five seconds, and standard measurements arrived about once per second. That was enough to see an event, while leaving the source location blurry.

Raw samples changed the problem.

Two stations, Trondheim and Amsterdam, had high-resolution captures of the same event. By comparing the tiny time difference between arrivals at the two stations, the researchers could define a geometric surface in space, a hyperboloid, where the source had to be. The margin became absurdly tight relative to the scale, only meters thick across tens of thousands of kilometers.

That is the best part of the story because it shows what good engineering judgment looks like. Humphreys and Clements did their analyses separately for a week, compared notes, found disagreement, and then found a code error. The final match mattered because it survived the human weakness that often ruins technical certainty: wanting your own result to be right.

The candidate that stayed aligned was Cosmos 2546, a Russian satellite launched in 2020. The source tracked the hyperboloid within the uncertainty of publicly available orbital data. Cosmos 2546 only explains events after its launch, but the satellite belongs to a broader Russian early-warning constellation in Molniya orbits. The arXiv abstract says the paper applies received-power and time-difference-of-arrival techniques to identify the interference source as that constellation (https://arxiv.org/abs/2606.03673).

The important phrase is “space-based,” because geography changes at orbital altitude.

A ground jammer has terrain, curvature, buildings, and altitude constraints. A space-based source sees too much.

The map is already noisy

Most of the GPS interference maps people see today are built from aircraft reports. GPSJam.org visualizes degraded navigation by aggregating ADS-B and related aircraft data (https://gpsjam.org/?lat=55.22483&lon=17.23509&z=5.8&date=2024-10-14). It is useful because planes become sensors. It is also incomplete because it shows the world through the systems that report.

Spire’s 2025 Baltic report makes the same point from a commercial satellite-data angle. In an October 2024 case around the Baltic Sea, Spire described GNSS disruption near Kaliningrad and wrote that satellite-based monitoring confirmed aircraft navigation impacts. It also reported that Polish researchers and GPSPATRON detected 84 hours of GNSS interference over six months, including 29 hours in October, with position errors exceeding 30 meters in cited maritime reporting (https://spire.com/blog/space-reconnaissance/gnss-interference-report-russia/).

These are different layers of the same problem:

  • aircraft show where navigation is failing in flight
  • ground stations show signal behavior at fixed receivers
  • satellite data can reveal patterns conventional heat maps miss
  • raw radio captures can turn a mystery into a source hypothesis

The truth sits in the overlap.

That matters because jamming and spoofing live in ambiguity. A map can show disruption while leaving intent open. A diplomatic statement can blame a state while hiding methods. A research paper can identify a source while still moving through peer review. An aircraft can lose reliable GPS and still land safely using other navigation systems. The honest article has to hold all of that at once.

The sharper claim is this: satellite navigation has become contested infrastructure. It is contested infrastructure.

The dependency is older than your app

The clean consumer myth is that GPS tells you where you are.

The colder version is that GNSS gives machines a shared sense of time and position, and modern systems quietly built themselves around that gift. Once a free, precise, global signal exists, engineers naturally absorb it. They make aviation procedures cheaper, ports more efficient, cell networks better synchronized, financial timestamps cleaner, logistics more fluid, and phones more useful.

Then the dependency becomes invisible.

Dana Goward, president of the Resilient Navigation and Timing Foundation, says in the source that GPS is an invisible utility supporting virtually every technology. That is the sentence to keep. A utility that fades from attention becomes hard to defend because the full map of dependence has no single owner.

This is the operator lesson: treat GNSS as an input with a trust state.

For aviation, that means redundant navigation, pilot training, terrestrial backups, and procedures that still work when the blue line lies or disappears. For maritime operators, it means radar, charts, inertial systems, landmarks, and crews who remember that GPS is a tool rather than the sea itself. For software and infrastructure people, it means asking a boring dependency question before the crisis: what breaks if accurate time or position becomes untrusted for one hour, one day, or one region?

The answer will be messier than the architecture diagram.

The strongest systems assume satellite navigation can fail and degrade gracefully when the whisper gets drowned out.

The practical rule is to audit the whisper

A good GNSS resilience audit starts with six questions:

1. What parts of the system require accurate position? 2. What parts require accurate time? 3. Which alerts detect jamming or spoofing rather than merely logging bad downstream behavior? 4. Which fallback works during GNSS loss? 5. Who has permission to declare GNSS untrusted? 6. How often is the fallback tested under realistic failure instead of merely documented?

At this point the lesson expands beyond Russia, Europe, or one satellite constellation. Those are the story’s evidence. The durable lesson is broader.

Infrastructure fails most dangerously when it was too convenient to notice.

GPS became a gift, then a habit, then a hidden assumption. The blue dot made the planet feel indexed. The clocks made machines agree. The fact that the system works almost all the time made the risk feel theoretical.

A weak signal from space can coordinate civilization. A stronger signal in the wrong band can remind civilization how much it forgot to question.

What to remember

GNSS is more than a map feature. It is timing, positioning, aviation, shipping, logistics, telecom, finance, and emergency coordination wrapped in a weak radio signal. The practical move is redundancy: know where the whisper enters your system, and know what still works when it disappears.
