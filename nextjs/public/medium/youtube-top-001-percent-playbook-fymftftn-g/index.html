A Business Becomes an Asset When a Stranger Can Buy It

What an operator learned in rooms with the ultra-rich, checked against survival rates, exit data and the price of equity.

About half of new U.S. businesses close within five years, and roughly 3% of large venture-backed companies exit in a year. The advice built on the survivors gets the mechanics right: equity has a post-money price, and a sale is what turns a claim into cash. The test is whether a stranger would buy what you built.

I build agents for a living, and one of them drafted this. The opinions and the edits are mine.

Richard Branson has said, and Virgin's site repeats, that he has founded more than 400 companies. The count is self-reported and unaudited. It is also consistent with a portfolio strategy: hundreds of attempts, a few large outcomes, and a failure rate that the U.S. Bureau of Labor Statistics puts near 50% within five years for new private-sector establishments.

One operator's account of the ultra-wealthy, published here, organizes their behavior into luck, currencies, enemies and exits. The behaviors are real, but the sample is selected by outcomes, which makes causality hard to see.

PitchBook counts roughly 40 exits in a year among more than 1,300 large U.S. venture-backed companies, about 3%. A person who studies the survivors is studying the group that already cleared that filter. The structural moves that still make sense are the ones that change what you own, who you can reach, or who would buy the business.

More chances that someone can value

The first structural move is exposure, and luck research supports a narrow version of the claim that you can improve it. Robert Frank finds that successful people are "almost always talented and hardworking," and that "very few would have succeeded if they hadn't been lucky, too". Luck includes birth country, timing and introductions. An operator can increase the number of chances they are exposed to, a smaller claim than control.

Branson's portfolio is the clearest version. If about half of new establishments close within five years, a single attempt carries worse odds than a set of attempts. The failures are expected in that reading, and a smaller operator can keep two or three experiments alive and treat each as evidence rather than a verdict. The portfolio buys time for one result to compound.

A chance is worth more when someone else can value it. A customer who pays, an investor who prices, or a buyer who bids turns an attempt into information. More chances matter because one of them may produce something another person will pay for.

An asset someone can value

The second move is to create a claim that can be priced. The example is equity. Using the SEC's definitions, $10 million for 10% works out to a $100 million post-money valuation and a $90 million pre-money valuation. The pre-money number determines how much of the company the new money buys.

Shares can serve as acquisition currency when the board authorizes new shares and the market accepts them. A priced company gains that option, and it also takes on dilution: every share issued to buy something is a claim on the same future cash flows. For an operator without a venture round, the practical version is smaller. Build something a buyer can own: recurring revenue, a customer contract, a library of work with rights, a list of people who trust you. The asset has to survive your departure.

Distribution you control

The third move is distribution. The scale list is useful here: intellectual property, distribution channels, trained people and software. Each one lets the same value reach more people without rebuilding it for every customer.

The economics underneath the list are real. Software and content are expensive to build once and cheap to reproduce, so the next customer costs less than the first. That arithmetic is why scale appears in large fortunes. The absolute claim that the economy rewards only value at scale is too strong: nurses, teachers and craftspeople create enormous value at human scale, and service businesses can earn premiums on trust and judgment.

For an operator, the checklist is practical. Intellectual property captures value in a form that can be licensed. Distribution is the right to reach people without paying for each impression. Trained people deliver a standard through hands you do not have to be. Software delivers the same outcome to the next thousand customers. Each changes the unit economics, and each carries a cost that founders underestimate, especially distribution.

A business someone can buy

The fourth move is the exit. The claim is that nine out of ten wealthy people had an exit, with multiple exits producing learning rounds. The base rate for founders is much lower. PitchBook's count of about 40 exits among 1,300 large venture-backed companies works out to roughly 3% in a year. UBS's 2025 billionaire report attributes most new billionaire wealth to business creation, and also to "the largest, multi-generational inheritance wave in the history of this report."

An exit adds two things: liquidity and a completed cycle. A founder who sells a small content business at a low single-digit revenue multiple has cash, time and one finished cycle to apply to the next attempt. The nine-in-ten claim describes a hand-picked sample of wealthy people rather than a base rate for founders, and the design implication survives either way.

Run the sellability audit before the next growth tactic. Could a stranger buy this business without you? What would transfer: contracts, code, brand, customer list, licenses, team? Who owns the distribution, and would it survive a change of owner? A business that cannot answer those questions depends on the owner's continued work. A business that can answer them has an asset, even if the exit is years away.

The parts that do not transfer

Talent and rivalry are offered as causes of wealth. The evidence supports a narrower reading.

Beethoven wrote nine symphonies (Britannica). The research on star performers supports the claim that top output is disproportionate. Aguinis and O'Boyle argue that individual output follows a power law in many fields, so a small group produces a large share. Groysberg reports that in highly complex jobs the top 1% outperforms average workers by 127%.

Portability is conditional. Groysberg's research on star analysts and athletes finds that stars who change employers often decline, while stars who move with their teams do not. The performance lived partly in the team, the systems and the clients. One anecdote describes a billionaire who screened 10,000 people for one role and says the startup became worth $2.5 billion. The anecdote is unverified, and it leaves out the team that the research says carries the performance. Hire for the context you can provide.

Rivalry has a similar shape. Baumeister's review finds that "bad is stronger than good": negative events carry more psychological weight than equivalent positive ones. That supports the direction of the enemy claim. It does not support the 70/30 motivation split, which arrives without a source, and it does not show that manufacturing an enemy improves a business. In one practitioner thread, some operators describe rivalry as a useful constraint; others warn that defining a company against a rival makes the rival the strategist.

A standard offers a testable alternative to an enemy. "We answer support tickets in under an hour" is a commitment a team can verify. "We beat that other company" borrows its agenda, and the agenda changes when the rival does.

Status works the same way. Federal Reserve data show the top 0.1% of households held 15% of U.S. net worth in mid-2026, and the statistics do not separately measure the top 0.01%. Awards, books and talks can open doors. They sit downstream of something scarce. The sample chose visibility, which makes the preference for positioning a poor guide for everyone else.

The test

The moves that transfer change one of three things: what you own, who you can reach, or who would buy the business. The moves that do not transfer change how you describe yourself or how the team feels about a rival. Talent and rivalry can still help. They do not change the three things a buyer pays for.

Write the list for your own business. What would a stranger get if they bought it, and what would they pay? If the list is empty, build one item on it this quarter. A contract. A piece of software with a license. A list of customers who would follow the work to a new owner. The first item is the beginning of an asset.
