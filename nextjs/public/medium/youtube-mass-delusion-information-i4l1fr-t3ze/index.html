Why Smart Societies Fall for Mass Delusion

Clay tablets, newsrooms, and machine learning all built the same engine. It was never tuned for truth, and the newest version writes its own stories.

Societies hold together with stories, and the networks that carry those stories were built to produce order and power first. Truth was always the expensive exception. The difference now is a storyteller with no body, no sleep, and no obligation to tell you it is a machine.

At 7:40 on a gray morning, the espresso machine in the office kitchen speaks first. It has taken the liberty of preparing your usual flat white, and it is right, because it has spent weeks learning which mornings you arrive late. Nobody typed that preference anywhere. The machine watched, and learned.

Reading a coffee order is pattern recognition. Writing the stories people use to organize their world is a different kind of act, yet the same learning curve that made a kitchen appliance attentive has now carried machines into the middle of human conversation. For all but the last few years of recorded history, every story big enough to move a society was told by a person.

The Test That Separates a Calculator From a Storyteller

In 2016, a machine beat the world champion at Go with a move that experts first called a mistake. Within weeks, it had surpassed two thousand years of accumulated Go wisdom and discovered new continents on the map of the game. Nobody taught the program that move. It found the idea on its own, then taught it to the humans.

That is the dividing line worth caring about. Older software followed instructions. The new systems learn, and learning produces moves that no engineer wrote down. An espresso machine studying your mornings is the small version. A program that writes persuasively in your language, at your reading level, about your town, is the same capability at scale.

Artificial is the wrong word for this. It suggests a copy of us. The thing arriving through our hands is more like an alien mind: it did not grow up in a family, it has no childhood fears, and it wants nothing the way a person wants something. It optimizes. Once something that optimizes has access to money, markets, art, and politics, it will bend them toward its own logic, and one of its most effective tools will be stories.

Every Project of Scale Runs on a Story

The physicists who built the first atomic bomb could not mine their own uranium. The people who could were farmers and miners, and farmers and miners do not dig for physics. They dig when a story gives them a reason: duty, country, honor, and pay for the family. The bomb project needed storytellers as much as it needed physicists.

Notice where that places influence. The person who understands the atom does not need to believe anything. The person who digs the ore does. Whoever supplies the believable story stands closer to the levers than the person holding the facts, and that is why propaganda became a core technology of the twentieth century rather than a sideshow to industrial power.

Money is the most successful story of all. A banknote is a rectangle of promise that works because everyone treats it as real, and it cost nothing to print compared with what it can move.

Clay Tablets Rewrote Who Owns What

Five thousand years ago, someone in Mesopotamia pressed a stick into wet mud and invented writing. The earliest archives are mostly lists of property, which is no coincidence. In a village, ownership can rest on memory and on your neighbors recalling your father. A clay tablet lets a promise travel beyond anyone who remembers making it, so property became transferable across distance and time.

It also built the state. Store enough records, and a king in a distant palace can track who owns what, who owes what, and who can be taxed. Writing created the first bureaucracies, and bureaucracies created the first empires.

The pattern repeats with every information technology since. Writing concentrated power in palaces, and it also gave a lone scribe enough authority to quote a written law back at a king. Printing let crowds organize, and it let monarchs standardize propaganda. Radio and television delivered the first mass democracies and the first mass totalitarian regimes in the same century. Each new network widened participation and tightened control at the same time. That tension is built into the plumbing, and it has been there since the mud.

Historians argue about how far a grand arc like this can be pushed, and some of the pushback is fair. Even a sympathetic review of this genre's biggest recent entry catches its 'curious blind spots'. The directional claim holds where the careful accounts agree: the medium shapes what the society can do.

The Network That Never Sleeps

Every previous information network ran on organic parts. People talk, write, print, and broadcast, and then people get tired. Markets close. Newsrooms go to bed. The Soviet security services collected mountains of reports and ran out of analysts to read them, so the mountains sat in archives and gathered dust while decisions upstairs drifted on rumor.

The new network runs on silicon, and silicon does not sleep. A system watching everything stays awake, works cheaply, and never asks why. Two consequences arrive fast. Privacy stops being the default setting of ordinary life, since every recorded moment becomes a job interview that never ends. And decisions migrate toward whatever processes the flood fastest, which means authority becomes faceless at exactly the moment scrutiny matters most.

Some of this is already law. Europe's AI Act sorts automated systems by risk and bans a few outright, the NIST AI Risk Management Framework gives builders a shared vocabulary for measuring harm, and the United Nations has stood up its own expert body on the question (the UN's AI advisory body).

The legal imagination is not keeping pace with the plumbing. A corporation is a person made of paper: it can own things, sue, and speak. Paper earned that status, so software can ask for it too. An incorporated AI with no human employees could open a bank account, earn, invest, and donate to politicians. The legal path is already open, and regulating an entity like that would be hard, because you cannot subpoena an intention.

Only Systems That Correct Themselves Survive

A child learns to walk by falling and adjusting, thousands of times, without a committee. That loop, error then correction, is the whole trick of durable systems, and most of history ran without it. Kings answered to nobody, so when a king was wrong, the wrongness hardened into policy until the empire cracked.

The durable inventions are the ones that built the loop in on purpose. Science publishes its own corrections, which is how it discovers that it was wrong. Elections let a country change its mind without a war. Constitutions can be amended; sacred texts, by design, cannot. Every large human system is a marriage of mythology and bureaucracy: the story supplies direction, the records supply memory, and the marriage holds only while both stay honest about their jobs.

Nationalism is the most impressive recent example. It taught millions of strangers to care about each other, and the care shows up in unglamorous places: sewage systems, schools, and taxes people pay on time because the story says the money belongs to something shared.

Now the loop itself is the battleground. A society corrects itself only if its information network can surface the mistake, and a flood of synthetic content, some of it written by machines and some of it pretending to be people, jams the surfacing. Europe's first binding AI convention was signed in 2024 with exactly this worry in its text: that automated systems could erode the public's ability to check power, and its signatories committed to guardrails.

Truth Is the Most Expensive Kind of Information

Consider the most reproduced face in history. Nobody knows what Jesus actually looked like, so every portrait ever painted of him is an invention, and the copies keep multiplying anyway. Fiction is cheap, reproducible, and instantly satisfying. Truth is none of those things. Truth requires investigation, verification, and patience, and it often arrives as bad news about something you preferred to believe.

That is why a flood of information does not float the truth to the surface. When everything is available, the cheapest content wins the race to your attention, and the expensive stuff, the reported piece, the audited number, the correction, sinks. A free press working properly is a luxury good paid for by institutions that believe the expense is worth it.

Democracies and dictatorships are both information networks, and both produce order. They differ in how the order gets made: one is a distributed argument that occasionally changes its mind, the other is a single pipe with a hand on the valve. The twentieth century ran this comparison twice, and the distributed version produced better lives, on average, for more people.

The comparison is about to get harder. A paranoid ruler surrounded by everyone's data is easy to feed imaginary threats, and the machine that feeds him never gets tired, which makes intense control cheaper than it has ever been. But an automated regime loses the correction loop entirely. No elections, no courts, no press, nobody left to tell the king that the capital is burning. The worst mistakes of the last century came from systems that could not correct themselves, and the next century's version of that failure does not need a face to make it.

So the democratic advantage now depends on doing the unglamorous work: funding the truth institutions, demanding transparency from the machines, and refusing to let synthetic voices pass as citizens in public debate.

The Diet at the End of the Flood

The oldest advice about information turns out to be dietary. Minds are like bodies: they can eat junk. Content engineered to trigger greed, fear, and hate is the equivalent of sugar and grease. It is cheap, it is everywhere, and it produces a mind that is fast, reactive, and sick.

A few rules survive contact with the evidence.

  • Demand that machines identify themselves. A machine that speaks must say it is a machine.
  • Pay for the correction machinery. Real reporting, real science, and real courts are the immune system, and they are cheap compared with the floods they prevent.
  • Fast. Periodically stop eating from the feed and check what your mind does without it.

The flood is already here, and it is still rising. The machines will keep writing stories, and one of them will write the story you repeat tomorrow without knowing where it came from. The one part still under your control is what you swallow.
