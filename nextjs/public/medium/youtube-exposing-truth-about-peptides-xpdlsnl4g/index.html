The FDA's Scientists Said No to All Seven Peptides. A Rebuilt Committee Said Yes to Six.

An advisory panel built weeks before the vote waved six unproven peptides toward federal compounding. The paper trail runs through two letters, eight fresh appointments, and a gray market that was already selling the result.

The FDA's pharmacy advisory committee recommended six unproven peptides for federal compounding in July, weeks after the agency's own scientists recommended against all seven. The record behind that reversal runs from a congresswoman's letter asking for a lighter evidence standard to a gray market that was already selling the future.

On July 23, the FDA's Pharmacy Compounding Advisory Committee voted 8-6-1 to recommend BPC-157, a peptide that athletes and longevity clinics have injected for years, for the federal list of substances compounding pharmacies may use (MedPage Today). An audible gasp rippled through the room after the first tally (TIME).

By the next afternoon the committee had recommended six of the seven peptides on its agenda, over the objections of the agency's own review staff, which had recommended against every one of them.

The evidence in the government's briefing documents was the same evidence that had failed in 2023, when these compounds went onto the FDA's list of substances that may present significant safety risks. Although that record had hardly changed, the deciders had. The FDA published eight new panelists on June 29, and a majority of them were involved with businesses that promote and prescribe peptides (STAT).

What the panel was actually voting on

Peptides are short chains of amino acids. Some are approved medicines with trials, labels, and warnings behind them; insulin and the GLP-1 obesity drugs are the famous examples (NPR). The seven on the FDA's agenda lived in a different world, their reputations built in animal studies, forums, and clinics that sell them directly to patients.

The FDA moved against the class in 2023, adding nearly 20 injectables to a list reserved for substances that may present significant safety risks, BPC-157 and TB-500 among them (FDA). What the list controls matters. Compounding pharmacies mix these substances from bulk ingredients for individual patients, a route that skips the trials and labeling a drug approval requires.

When the FDA's review staff published their briefing documents in July, they recommended against all seven, calling most of the compounds "not well characterized" and noting that KPV, MOTS-c, and TB-500 had no published human studies (FDA briefing documents). Health secretary Robert F. Kennedy Jr. had promised to reverse the restrictions anyway, calling himself "a big fan of peptides" who used them for his own injuries (AP).

The committee was rebuilt first

The clearest statement of what came next arrived in writing on November 10, 2025. Rep. Diana Harshbarger, a Tennessee pharmacist, asked the FDA to reassess the peptides "through an improved PCAC process that prioritizes safety and quality over evidence of clinical efficacy," describing the right standard as a substance's safety profile and suitability for compounding rather than clinical-trial data (the letter). Sen. Tommy Tuberville had written in the same direction that summer.

On June 29, the FDA published eight new panelists for the committee. STAT reported that most of the eight new members were involved with businesses that promote and prescribe peptides (STAT). The list includes a Tennessee state senator who works in his family's pharmacy selling compounded pain, longevity, and weight-loss drugs, and a physician who charges $500 for "peptide and hormone" consultations (Healio). Panels in previous years had leaned on academics from major medical centers, and they had rejected peptide after peptide (MedPage Today).

A bodega, a laced vial, and twelve hours of testimony

Day one ran nearly twelve hours, and across the two days the panel heard from more than two dozen speakers. The arguments for access were practical. Dr. Anant Vinjamoori, chief medical officer of the telehealth company Hims & Hers, said a colleague had recently sent him a photo of a bodega in Queens selling peptides. "This is the real alternative to FDA's permission of legitimate pharmacies to compound peptides in controlled conditions," he said (TIME).

Dr. Melissa Loseke, chief medical officer of the Re-New Institute in Omaha and a committee member, described a patient who brought her a peptide vial laced with MDMA and ecstasy, and said she voted yes hoping a listing would let regulators put tighter restrictions on the drugs (MedPage Today).

Russell Wesdyk, an official in the FDA's office of pharmaceutical quality, said a listed substance's name has no legal meaning, so "there's no way to know what the substance actually is, was or will be tomorrow" (AP). He also described the trap the votes were walking into. Members kept asking for "a yes vote, but with quality guardrails," while the agency, he said, lacks "the regulatory authority that put those guardrails in place" (TIME).

Opponents outside the panel made the same point from another direction. Nina Zeldes of Public Citizen's Health Research Group testified that the peptides' benefits "are largely based on anecdotal evidence, and the interest in it appears to be driven by those who stand to benefit from increased demand." Committee member Dr. Elizabeth Rebello, an anesthesiologist at MD Anderson, was blunter: "I'm concerned we're responding to a market-induced demand rather than a decision based in solid science" (TIME).

Six yes votes, close margins

Day one produced three 8-6-1 recommendations, for BPC-157, KPV, and TB-500, plus a 7-5 vote with two abstentions for MOTS-c. Day two added epitalon and semax on votes of 7-5 and 8-5, each with one abstention, while emideltide failed on a 7-6 vote. Six of seven, with margins close enough that one or two seats decided most votes, and five more peptides queued for early 2027 (MedPage Today).

Members who voted yes talked about access, not certainty. Dr. Haleem Mohammed, who runs Florida clinics that sell peptide and testosterone injections and is chief medical officer of Gameday Men's Health, said the choice between rejection and the gray market left him with a question he told the room he loses sleep over: "am I doing greater harm?" (TIME). Tennessee state senator Bobby Harshbarger, a pharmacist whose family business sells compounded medications, said the committee's duty was to apply the compounding law's standards and that the vote "doesn't approve a drug or indication" (MedPage Today). David Pope, chief pharmacy officer of Xifin Pharmacy Solutions, said after the vote that it was time to put the decision "back in the hands of the patient, the physician and the pharmacist" (AP).

The dissenters warned about the signal. Committee member Todd Durham of the Foundation Fighting Blindness, who voted no, said he worried "what placing a bulk substance on the list conveys in terms of FDA approval." Pharmacist John Hertig, speaking during the public comment period, told the panel it risked "undermining the integrity of our entire FDA approval framework" by opening "a parallel pathway" (MedPage Today). A peptide company founder celebrated the outcome as a win for a committee that looked at "the real utilization data" (LinkedIn).

What the vote does not do

A recommendation is not a shelf. Being added to the compounding list would not make these compounds approved drugs. There would be no approved label with dosing and warnings, no requirement to follow federal manufacturing standards for sterile injectables, and no mandatory reporting of adverse events. Timothy Fensky, a liaison from the National Association of Boards of Pharmacy, abstained from all three of the second day's votes and urged the FDA to build a framework with the state boards of pharmacy that would include adverse-event reporting (MedPage Today).

The FDA isn't required to follow the committee's advice, though it usually does, and formal listing would require new regulations that can take months or years to draft. FDA-watchers expect the near-term outcome to be a temporary policy that reassures pharmacies rather than a completed rule (AP). Nathan Beaver, an attorney specializing in FDA issues, said the process would "more than likely end up at the result that Secretary Kennedy wants" (AP). Money moved faster: analysts projected a $2.2 billion telehealth marketing opportunity around the class, and Hims & Hers stock rose more than 10 percent after the BPC-157 vote (TIME).

The gray market made the argument

Look at the market the votes were answering. TRM Labs found that cryptocurrency payments to vendors listing both peptides and fentanyl precursors rose from $28.2 million in 2023 to $37.3 million in 2025, a 32 percent increase, with about 57 percent of the payments coming from the United States (Medical Daily). More than 200 Chinese sellers of fentanyl precursors are on the firm's list, and at least 50 of them are now selling peptides, a number that has grown in recent months (WSJ).

Customs sees the same pipeline. Cincinnati officers identified a Chinese "master carton" scheme in December, which hides multiple orders inside a larger box, and have since thwarted roughly 400 attempts; nationwide, seizures of noncompliant pharmaceutical shipments passed 2,400 in the fiscal year through August (WSJ). One vendor indicted in 2023 over fentanyl precursors now sells semaglutide and Melanotan II through the same channels.

The harm is documented too. At a Las Vegas anti-aging conference last year, two women, 38 and 51, were hospitalized in critical condition after receiving peptide injections at an exhibitor's booth (ProPublica).

Put the sequence in order. Demand grew first, in a market nobody supervised. Then demand became the argument for a supervised marketplace, and that argument carried the room. The strongest case for the peptides rarely rested on trial data. Usage was the case. Millions of people inject them, the market grows fastest where regulators say no, and the committee concluded, by narrow margins, that a supervised route was better than an unsupervised one.

How to read the next peptide headline

1. Know what a vote is. A compounding recommendation lets pharmacies mix a substance from bulk ingredients. It is not an approval, a dose, or a finding of effectiveness (AP). 2. Check the evidence ladder. For BPC-157, the animal literature is substantial and the human literature is small. A recent review found that every published study reports positive effects, a pattern its authors flag as likely publication bias (the review). A certificate of analysis from a seller tests what is in the vial. It says nothing about what happens in a body. 3. The slow path exists. A Phase 2 randomized, double-blind trial of BPC-157 in hamstring injuries is recruiting now, with primary completion projected for early 2027 (ClinicalTrials.gov). That pace is the price of a real answer, and it is slower than any vote. 4. Take it to someone accountable. The advice from researchers and regulators points the same way: talk to a doctor first, because consulting a physician can mitigate some of the risk (NPR).

What comes next

In October, reports said the administration plans to force quick access to unapproved peptides before the required rulemaking is finished (The Niche). If that happens, the July votes will look like the quiet, procedural version of a fight that is only starting. Dr. Peter Lurie, president of the Center for Science in the Public Interest and a former FDA official, predicted the deepest cost: companies will stop chasing approvals for peptides when compounders can sell the same molecules without generating evidence (AP).

Two letters, eight appointments, twelve hours of testimony, and a handful of narrow margins moved six compounds past a gate that was built to wait for data. The trials may still arrive. They are no longer the price of admission. That is the part of this summer that will outlast BPC-157.
