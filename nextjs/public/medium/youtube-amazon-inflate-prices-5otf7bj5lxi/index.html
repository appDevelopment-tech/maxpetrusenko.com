The Same Toy Is $27.99 Everywhere. That Is Not a Coincidence.

Amazon's real product is the price floor under everything you buy.

The burning Godzilla figure was for the three-year-old. More Perfect Union's reporter wanted the classic Godzilla vs. Destoroyah design, the one where the monster is wreathed in flames, and Amazon had it for $26.98. Then he checked Walmart, just to make sure he was getting the best deal: $26.98. The same oddly specific price. A few weeks later the toy sold for $27.99 at Amazon, $27.99 at Walmart, and $27.99 at GameStop. Matching low prices, moving in tandem.

Physical stores spend their existence trying to outdo one another on price. Online, the numbers happened to line up everywhere he looked. A pack of toilet paper: $8.44 at Amazon, Walmart, and a few other stores, then $8.96 in all of them a few weeks later. A soccer goal for the kids: $24.97 across the board. Weird numbers, probably no accident.

It was not an accident, the lawsuits allege. What looked like coincidence was the visible edge of an alleged scheme to rig prices, one that the federal government, the state of California, and a class of millions of shoppers are now pursuing in court. The company accused of running it is Amazon, and the trick is simple: instead of lowering its own prices to beat the competition, it pressures the competition to raise theirs.

The lowest price on Amazon is a rule that other stores are made to follow.

California's attorney general, Rob Bonta, has spent years investigating Amazon, and in April 2026 he unsealed what he calls smoking-gun evidence of price fixing. California says the emails it published show Amazon employees working with vendors to raise prices at rival retailers, including Walmart, Target, and Chewy. In one exchange, Amazon told Levi's that its khaki pants were selling for less at Walmart, around $25.47 to $26.99, while Amazon charged $29.99. "I hope these can get resolved over the next few days," an Amazon employee wrote. The next day, Levi's reported that it had talked to Walmart, and Walmart agreed to take the pants back up to $29.99 immediately. Amazon confirmed it had matched the higher price. One vendor, in another email, promised Amazon it was "very determined to help you hunt the disrupters in the market."

The Buy Box is the leverage

Why would a brand like Levi's obey? Because of the Buy Box, the yellow and orange panel on the right of every Amazon product page where the Add to Cart and Buy Now buttons live. It is the recommended offer when multiple sellers carry the same product, and the FTC says nearly all Amazon sales flow through that box. If Amazon strips a seller of the Buy Box, that seller effectively disappears from the site. One vendor told More Perfect Union it lost 80 percent of its sales the moment it lost the box. Steve Berman, the lawyer leading the biggest class action against Amazon, calls losing it the kiss of death. When Amazon says change the price or you are out, he says, sellers change the price.

Molson Hart runs Viahart, a toy company that sells the Brain Flakes building discs. He has owned the whole chain since 2014, from designing the product to packing it. When he listed Brain Flakes at Target for $19.98, one cent less than the $19.99 on Amazon, the product got suspended. His team scrambled, emailing, calling, and working through the night to fix it. Later, one of his products was flagged as overpriced on Amazon for a reason that took days to find: it was cheaper on Viahart's own website. He raised the prices on his own site, even though his costs there were lower, because the alternative was losing the Amazon sales that keep the company alive.

The fee that makes lower prices impossible

Sellers cannot simply lower their Amazon prices to match what they charge elsewhere, because it costs more to sell on Amazon than almost anywhere else. The researcher Stacy Mitchell has documented that ten years ago, sellers paid about 20 percent of every sale to Amazon, on average. Today the take can reach 50 percent for sellers stacking FBA, ads, and storage fees; the average burden is lower. There are referral fees, advertising fees, and fulfillment fees. There are long-term storage fees, aged inventory fees, and fees that appear when stock runs thin in Amazon warehouses. In a competitive market, Mitchell argues, sellers would raise prices on Amazon to absorb those fees and sell cheaper on leaner platforms, and shoppers would drift toward the cheaper stores. That does not happen, because the California emails show rivals being pushed to charge more.

The FTC alleges Amazon polices its own products the same way. When a supplier's product is cheaper elsewhere and Amazon lowers its own price to match, the complaint says it sends the supplier a bill for the lost margin. In the FTC's telling, suppliers quickly learn the safer move: pressure every other retailer to keep prices high instead of writing checks.

The machine that raises prices

The coercion is backed by software. The FTC's complaint against Amazon describes a massive web-crawling apparatus that constantly tracks prices across the internet and immediately matches any discount a rival dares to offer. According to the complaint, rivals learn that cutting prices never wins them customers, because Amazon always matches, so they stop cutting and instead raise prices to widen their margins. Amazon then matches the increase. Over time, the complaint alleges, prices across the entire online market drift upward on a floor that Amazon built.

The most striking allegation is a tool the FTC says Amazon codenamed Project Nessie. According to the unredacted complaint, the algorithm found products where rivals would likely follow Amazon's price increases, raised those prices, and kept them high once competitors matched. The FTC says Nessie generated more than $1 billion from American households between 2014 and 2018 and was switched off around events like Prime Day, when journalists would notice. Amazon disputes the account, saying Nessie was meant to stop prices from falling too low and was scrapped years ago. The FTC counters with internal emails calling the project an incredible success and a 2022 message from a retail executive musing about reviving "our old friend Nessie."

The data firm SmartScout, which tracks more than ten million products on the US marketplace, found that Amazon price inflation has outpaced national inflation since 2022, in an era when national inflation was already high. The class action alleges the cumulative overcharge to American shoppers is in the tens of billions of dollars. It was certified as the largest in US history.

The floor under everything you buy

Amazon competes on the price floor itself, the cases allege: it sets the lowest price that is allowed to exist, and every rival has to stand on it. The Godzilla figure, the toilet paper, the khaki pants. Different products, different sellers, same mechanism. Sometimes, when the exact same price appears everywhere, you are seeing one market's rules propagate through the rest.

What the courts can do

Europe already forced a version of the fix. In 2022, after a long investigation into the Buy Box, Amazon agreed to treat all sellers equally when ranking offers and to stop using marketplace data to favor its own retail. The commitments are legally binding in the EU. In the United States, the FTC's case is heading to trial in 2027 and California's preliminary injunction hearing is set for July 2026. Bonta wants a court order stopping the practices, civil penalties, and disgorgement of profits, with a possible breakup of the company on the table. Amazon denies the allegations and argues the lawsuits define its market too narrowly, ignoring the competition from Walmart, Target, and the rest of retail.

The kid, for the record, changed his mind about the Godzilla figure and asked for a King Ghidorah statue instead. The statue was $18.07 at Amazon, and the same $18.07 at Walmart.

The dangerous part of the scheme is not the price you pay on Amazon. It is the lower price that no longer exists anywhere else.
