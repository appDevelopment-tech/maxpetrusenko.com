Panera Kept the Price. Then Came the Frozen Dough.

A $7.5 billion buyout turned fresh bread into a cost line. The customers who paid for it kept the receipts.

Panera sold fresh bread and charged accordingly. After a $7.5 billion buyout, the company held the price and defunded the promise behind it: frozen dough, thinner portions, a dangerous drink in the self-serve line. Any turnaround now has to re-earn trust one audited dollar at a time.

Fresh baked daily

Fresh baked daily was the promise: dough made in company kitchens, shipped to stores, and baked there daily. The chain passed 2,000 cafes and more than $5 billion in annual sales, and the daily bake was still the selling point.

In 1987, Ken and Linda Rosenthal opened a bakery in Kirkwood, Missouri, and called it the St. Louis Bread Company. The name stayed local, the prices carried a premium, and the promise was specific: fresh baked daily.

The trust account

That belief was the product, and selling it comes with arithmetic. When a company spends money on the thing it promised, on bakers and ovens and ingredients and hours, the account fills. The catch is that it drains the moment the spending is redirected toward the brand. Every cost cut is also a product change, and the customers keep the receipts.

Panera held one of those accounts for years. The trade it offered was simple: pay a little more, and get bread that was baked fresh every day. Most categories live on a short list in the customer's head, and Panera spent its best years on that list. Customers kept choosing that trade, and repetition is what builds a brand: a promise kept often enough to be believed.

What $7.5 billion bought

In April 2017, JAB Holding put $315 a share on the table, about $7.5 billion including debt and a premium of roughly 30 percent over the recent trading price (the deal announcement, coverage of the close). The deal closed that July, and by November Ron Shaich, the founder who had turned one Missouri bakery into a national chain, had stepped down as chief executive (the company's announcement). JAB, the European private equity giant, was assembling a portfolio of coffee and bakery brands, and Panera joined the collection.

The announcement spoke the standard language of a sale, all about the value being delivered to shareholders. What actually changed hands was the trust that three decades of customers had paid to build. The promise now belonged to an owner whose plan for it was efficiency, and on the sign outside, nothing moved.

The first withdrawals

The cuts started small. Portions shrank, premium ingredients gave way to cheaper ones, and by 2025 customers were posting receipts for sandwiches near $16 and family orders near $71 (the turnaround coverage). The chief executive, Paul Carbone, described the era as "death by a thousand paper cuts" (food-industry coverage).

Then came the dough. By 2016 the company ran two dozen central bakery plants making fresh dough daily; in April 2025 it announced it would close the remaining fresh-dough plants and move the whole system to par-baked frozen dough over about two years (local coverage of the change). Closure notices kept arriving through 2026, including a Massachusetts plant and nearly a hundred jobs (Boston-area coverage).

The stores still finish the dough in their ovens, but it increasingly arrives frozen.

The Unlimited Sip Club was capped in 2026 at one drink every two hours and thirty a month (the change and its terms), and the word unlimited stopped doing any work.

A drink in the self-serve line

The most expensive withdrawal did not come from the kitchen. In 2022, Panera added Charged Lemonade to the self-serve line, alongside the regular lemonade. A 20-ounce serving carried 260 milligrams of caffeine; the 30-ounce size carried 390. Federal guidance cites 400 milligrams a day as an amount not generally associated with dangerous effects for healthy adults (the FDA's guidance).

Sarah Katz, a 21-year-old student who had a heart condition, died after drinking a Charged Lemonade in September 2022, and her family's wrongful-death case became federal litigation in Philadelphia (the court record). In October 2023, Dennis Brown died at 79 after drinking multiple cups at a Panera in Florida, and his family also sued (news coverage). The cases ended in settlements and dismissals across 2024 and 2025 with no admission of liability by the company, while the drink itself left the menu in 2024 (settlement coverage, the BBC's account).

A mistake like that cannot be filed away or outspent. The trust was the product, and the product was now the evidence.

The franchisee bill

The people who owned the cafes felt the same math. Franchise-disclosure summaries put a typical location's sales around $2.6 million a year, down from closer to $2.8 million. Royalty and advertising fees take six to eight percent of every dollar before rent, labor, and food (the disclosures). Remodels run into six figures per cafe on a schedule the parent company sets, and the fees come due whether the customers do or not.

Eduardo Diaz ran more than a dozen Panera cafes around Houston. In June 2025, the company won a consent judgment against his operation (the enforcement case). Two months later, EYM Cafe of Texas filed for Chapter 11 with debts reported between $10 million and $50 million, and by December the bankruptcy was dismissed with the judgment still standing (the bankruptcy docket).

The relationship can still work, and one operator offers a counterexample: Covelli Enterprises speaks warmly about the partnership after nearly forty years (a local profile). Franchising works when the parent's plan and the operator's arithmetic agree, and around Houston they stopped agreeing.

The verdict

The numbers eventually said what the customers had been saying. System sales slid from about $6.5 billion in 2023 to roughly $6.1 billion in 2024, the first meaningful decline in years, and it kept falling in 2025, by about 2.8 percent (earnings coverage). The rankings moved too: the chain that once defined the category now sits third in fast casual, behind Chipotle and Panda Express. Carbone said publicly in November 2025 that the company had disappointed its customers, and Panera answered with RISE, a turnaround plan promising a better menu and better service and $7 billion in sales by 2028 across 2,239 cafes (the plan's announcement; coverage of it).

Some of the recent pressure is shared: fast casual has been losing steam as a category (category data), and through the summer of 2026 a cyclospora outbreak pulled traffic down across the sector, Panera among the affected chains (traffic coverage). Recent months have shown stabilization, with sales improving as new value menus and service changes land (analyst coverage, traffic counts).

A buyout also does not produce an outcome like this on its own. Research into private-equity takeovers in other industries has found measurable operational effects after the ownership change, which makes Panera's arc a pattern worth watching rather than a verdict on ownership forms (the study). JAB returned to profit and Pret, one of its other brands, posted a strong start to the year (the profit report, Pret's numbers). The founder moved within the category: Shaich now chairs Cava, the fast-casual chain growing fast while Panera's sales fell (Fortune).

The plan now on the table gets read through the same test the customers ran by leaving: watch where the money goes. Dollars into bakers and plants and ingredients and hours count, and slogans count for nothing.

What trust costs to rebuild

Chipotle's recovery from its own food-safety crisis shows the window exists: years of visible spending on operations, fixes in public, and a place kept near the top of the category (how it was done). The window stays open longer than executives think and closes faster than they expect.

For Panera, the audit is the same one the customers already ran. Money into the promise is what brings the customers back. Skip the spending and dress up the language, and the math keeps compounding in the other direction. The deal's arithmetic was always going to meet the customer's arithmetic, and the customer's arithmetic wins.

Trust re-earns at full price, one audited dollar at a time, and the customers who left are the auditors.
