What Would You Feel If Your Body Was Replaced?

The strangest question in transplant medicine is also a test of where you think “you” live.

A severed dog’s head followed a hammer with its eyes.

It reacted to taste. It responded to sound. Its pupils moved toward a stimulus while an external machine circulated oxygenated blood through the brain. The experiment, conducted by Soviet physiologist Sergei Bryukhonenko in the 1920s, became one of the most disturbing demonstrations in the history of perfusion research. A modern account of the experiment is preserved by the National Library of Medicine.

The image feels like science fiction because it attacks a quiet assumption: that a living body is one indivisible object. The dog’s head appeared to remain responsive while the heart and lungs had been replaced by pumps, tubing, and chemical control.

That scene gives us a useful spine for a much larger question. Your sense of being a person depends on several systems working together, but those systems do not all belong to the same piece of flesh.

The machine kept the blood moving

Bryukhonenko’s apparatus, often called an autojector, separated two jobs that the body normally hides inside the chest. One pump sent oxygen-rich blood toward the brain. Another removed blood carrying carbon dioxide. Glucose and other conditions had to be managed as well. The head could respond because the machine supplied part of the environment that the body usually supplies for free.

That distinction matters. The experiment did not prove that a head is a complete human being. It showed that some brain functions can continue when circulation is maintained outside the body.

Modern extracorporeal support uses the same broad principle with far better safeguards. The NHLBI describes ECMO as a treatment that circulates and oxygenates blood outside the body while the heart or lungs recover. A heart-lung machine can temporarily perform related work during surgery, as the American Heart Association explains.

The apparatus can preserve conditions for a brain. It cannot supply a biography, a voice, a face, or a future.

A body transplant would be a different problem

A body transplant is often described as though surgeons could simply connect a head to a new torso. The biology is harder. Blood vessels can be joined. The spinal cord is the central obstacle.

When the spinal cord is severely damaged, the communication lines between brain and body are disrupted. The National Institute of Neurological Disorders and Stroke describes spinal cord injury in terms of lost movement, sensation, and autonomic control below the injury. Reconnecting the cord would mean restoring an enormous network of precise pathways, not joining one cable to another.

In 2017, Italian surgeon Sergio Canavero and Chinese surgeon Ren Xiaoping announced a human head-transplant project. The claims drew intense criticism from neuroscientists and ethicists. A review in Surgical Neurology International argued that the scientific and ethical barriers were profound. No publicly verified human head transplant has been performed.

Animal experiments have shown something narrower. Vladimir Demikhov attached a second dog’s head and forequarters to another dog in the 1950s. The two heads could eat and drink, but the result was a short-lived surgical spectacle, not a functioning new person. The historical record is discussed in The Journal of the History of the Neurosciences.

These cases are useful because they separate three questions that are usually collapsed into one:

1. Can tissue remain biologically alive? 2. Can a brain continue producing responses? 3. Does the original person continue into a new body?

The first two have partial answers. The third is still a philosophical and medical problem.

Where would the self move?

Imagine waking after such an operation. Your memories are intact. You recognize your partner. You remember your childhood address. You can name the people in the room.

Your face is different. Your hormonal signals are different. Your immune system now belongs to another body. Your muscles have a new history of movement. Your internal organs release signals that influence sleep, appetite, stress, and mood.

The brain would carry the strongest continuity of memory and personality. That is why philosophers often treat the brain as the likely seat of personal identity. Yet the brain does not operate in isolation. The interoception literature links bodily signals to emotion, decision-making, and the feeling of inhabiting a self. The National Institute of Mental Health describes the brain as part of a nervous and hormonal system that coordinates the body, not as a detached computer floating above it.

A new body could preserve the autobiography while changing the background music.

Your pulse would come from a different heart. Your immune system would treat the new organs as its own only because of extraordinary immunosuppression. Transplant medicine already manages the danger of rejection, but the cost is permanent vulnerability to infection and other complications. The Mayo Clinic’s overview of organ transplant describes rejection and immunosuppressive treatment as central realities of transplantation.

The transplanted brain would remember the old life. The new body would still participate in creating the next one.

The two-headed dog exposes the boundary

Demikhov’s animal experiments are grotesque partly because they make the usual boundary visible. Two heads shared one body. Each could receive food and drink. Neither became a clean replacement for the other.

The experiment turns identity into a practical question. Which head owns the body? The answer is obvious only because the animal cannot tell us what the arrangement feels like. We see two centers of response attached to one circulation system, and our language starts failing.

That failure matters for ordinary medicine. A patient is never only an organ. A heart transplant is a surgical procedure, a long period of uncertainty, a drug regimen, and a changed relationship with mortality. A face transplant affects recognition and social life. A uterus transplant can make pregnancy possible for some recipients. Each operation changes a body part, but each also changes the person’s future possibilities.

The United Network for Organ Sharing tracks transplantation as a system of donors, recipients, surgeons, waiting lists, and long-term care. Its structure quietly rejects the fantasy of a single dramatic moment in which one body becomes another. Transplantation is an ongoing relationship between tissue, medicine, identity, and time.

What the experiment really changes

The most unsettling part of the old experiment is not that a machine could keep a head alive for a while. It is that “alive” turned out to be a layered word.

A reflex can continue. A pupil can move. A brain can metabolize glucose. A patient can breathe with assistance. A person can recognize a loved one. These are different achievements, measured at different levels.

Medicine often works by isolating one level. A perfusion machine asks whether blood can circulate. A neurologist asks whether the nervous system is functioning. A transplant team asks whether tissue is compatible and whether the recipient can survive the operation. A family asks whether the person they love is still present.

Each question is legitimate. None is the whole question.

That is the mental model worth carrying away: when a biological system seems to have crossed a boundary, ask which layer has actually changed.

Use it in three places:

  • Life support: circulation can be maintained while consciousness remains absent.
  • Brain injury: a preserved heartbeat does not describe the person’s neurological state.
  • Transplantation: a replaced organ can alter the body without replacing the recipient’s identity.

The layer test prevents a machine demonstration from becoming a claim about personhood. It also prevents a philosophical answer from ignoring the plumbing that keeps a brain alive.

The answer is still uncomfortable

Would you feel like yourself after a body transplant? Memory and recognition might make continuity feel immediate. Hormones, immune signals, movement, sensation, and the social shock of a changed appearance could make the new life unfamiliar.

There may be no single moment when the old person ends and a new person begins. Identity could persist as a pattern while the body supplying that pattern changes around it.

The severed dog’s head did not answer the question. It made the question visible. A response to a hammer is evidence of functioning tissue. It is not a declaration of a self.

The person you call “you” lives in the continuity between brain, body, memory, relationships, and time. Change one part dramatically, and the continuity may bend without breaking.
