What Would You Feel If Your Body Was Replaced?

The brain would keep your life's memories. The new body would make them feel like they happened to a stranger.

A severed dog's head could track a hammer with its eyes. Tracking can happen without awareness. A body transplant is usually framed as a surgical stunt. The stranger question is which part of you belongs to the brain and which part belongs to the body, and whether a body would change the part that feels. This piece separates the layers of "alive" and "you," and lands on one with no clean answer.

In recordings from the Bryukhonenko experiments, an isolated dog's head appeared to track a hammer with its eyes, its pupils moving while an external machine kept oxygenated blood moving through the brain. The Soviet physiologist Sergei Bryukhonenko ran the experiment in the 1920s, and a modern account of it is preserved by the National Library of Medicine.

The image attacks a quiet assumption: that a living body is one indivisible object. Here the head kept responding while the heart and lungs had been replaced by pumps, tubing, and chemical control.

That distinction matters. Pupillary and oculomotor responses can persist without any awareness. The scene shows automatic responses continuing; it does not show that a person is present. A machine can keep blood moving, and a brain can respond, and neither one tells you whether a person is there. Those are three separate achievements we usually collapse into the single word "alive."

Layer 1: Circulation

Bryukhonenko's apparatus, often called an autojector, split two jobs the body normally hides inside the chest. One pump sent oxygen-rich blood toward the brain. Another removed blood carrying carbon dioxide. Glucose and other conditions had to be managed as well. The head could respond because the machine supplied part of the environment the body usually provides for free.

The experiment did not show that an isolated brain is a complete person. It showed that some brain functions continue when circulation is maintained outside the body.

Modern extracorporeal support uses the same broad principle with far better safeguards. The NHLBI describes ECMO as a treatment that circulates and oxygenates blood outside the body while the heart or lungs recover, and a heart-lung machine can do related work during surgery. The apparatus can preserve conditions for a brain. It cannot supply a biography, a voice, a face, or a future.

With those layers in mind, consider what a body transplant would actually have to reassemble.

Layer 2: Communication

Blood vessels can be joined between a head and a new torso, and other systems could in principle be connected too. Among the barriers, the spinal cord is the highest. Severing the cord does more than silence voluntary movement. It severs the sensory highway that tells the brain it has a physical presence at all, and spinal cord injury is described in terms of lost movement, sensation, and autonomic control below the injury. Reconnecting the cord would mean re-growing and precisely re-myelinating millions of axons across dozens of tracts with correct synaptic targets, not joining one cable to another.

In 2017, Italian surgeon Sergio Canavero and Chinese surgeon Ren Xiaoping announced a human head-transplant project. The claims drew intense criticism from neuroscientists and ethicists, and a review in Surgical Neurology International argued that the scientific and ethical barriers were profound. No publicly verified human head transplant has been performed.

These cases separate three questions that are usually fused:

1. Can tissue remain biologically alive? 2. Can a brain keep producing responses? 3. Does the original person continue into a new body?

The first two have partial answers. The third remains unsettled in philosophy, in neurology, and in law.

Layer 3: Integration

Imagine waking after such an operation. This is a thought experiment, not a clinical description; every detail below is plausible, and each one depends on anatomy that no one has tested.

You wake to a heartbeat you do not recognize, faster at rest, a different thump in a different place. Your hands may not land where your mind predicts; the reach could be longer, the grip stronger but clumsy. The smell of your own skin might be wrong. Your voice could exit at a pitch your ears do not expect. You might be hungry at odd hours, sleepy at noon, tearful without a story. In the mirror your face could argue with your memory; in your body your memory could argue with your balance.

This is the part most accounts skip. If autobiographical memory and personality did carry over, the brain would probably hold the continuity we call the self, which is why philosophers treat the brain as the likely seat of identity. Yet the brain does not operate in isolation. The interoception literature links bodily signals to emotion, decision-making, and the feeling of inhabiting a self, and the National Institute of Mental Health describes the brain as part of a nervous and hormonal system that coordinates the body, not a detached computer floating above it.

A new body could preserve memory while retuning mood, appetite, and stress responses. The pulse would come from a different heart. The immune system would fight a transplanted head and torso the way it fights any foreign tissue, and only extreme immunosuppression would hold it in check. For a whole-body graft that problem is far larger than for an ordinary organ, where the same tradeoff is still permanent. Infection risk, malignancy risk, and medication side effects become part of the recipient's baseline, as the Mayo Clinic's overview of organ transplant makes plain.

Even a smaller operation does this to a person. In 2017 Andy Sandness received the first complete face transplant at the Mayo Clinic, after a self-inflicted gunshot wound had destroyed his lower face. The operation took 56 hours and roughly sixty surgeons, nurses, and anesthesiologists. When he saw the result in a mirror, the only word he could reach for was "wow." Two and a half years later he could eat, smell, speak, and kiss, and he called the new face "part of me. It's, like, my face." The nerves were still growing.

The transplanted brain would remember the old life. The new body would still participate in creating the next one.

The two-headed dog

Vladimir Demikhov attached a second dog's head and forequarters to another dog in the 1950s. The two heads could eat and drink, but the result was a short-lived surgical spectacle, not a functioning new person. The historical record is discussed in The Journal of the History of the Neurosciences.

The experiment forces a practical question: which head owns the body? There is no clean answer. Two centers of control share one circulation, and our language starts failing. The dog cannot tell us anything about ownership; it only shows us two responses attached to one body. A head does not own a body. A self depends on a set of needs, and transplanting a brain would transplant every need it has for a body that no longer knows how to serve them.

That failure matters for ordinary medicine. A patient is never only an organ. A heart transplant is a procedure, a long uncertainty, a drug regimen, and a changed relationship with mortality. Each operation changes a body part, and each also changes a person's future possibilities. Organ transplantation is an ongoing relationship between tissue, medicine, identity, and time, and its very design rejects the fantasy of one dramatic moment when a body becomes another.

What the experiment really changes

The most unsettling part of the old experiment is not that a machine could keep a head alive for a while. It is that "alive" turned out to be a layered word.

A reflex can continue. A pupil can move. A brain can metabolize glucose. A patient can breathe with assistance. A person can recognize a loved one. These are different achievements, measured at different levels, and medicine works by isolating one level. A perfusion machine asks whether blood can circulate. A neurologist asks whether the nervous system is functioning. A family asks whether the person they love is still present. Each question is legitimate. None is the whole question.

Would you feel like yourself after a body transplant? No one knows. Your memories and language might light up as before, or they might arrive in a body whose moods, instincts, and history do not match them. The head on a machine shows that tissue can respond. It does not tell you what it is like to live there.

Face and hand transplants, spinal cord injury, deafferentation, vestibular disruption, interoception, endocrine effects, and body-ownership illusions each point at one piece of what a body transplant would touch. None of them reproduces the procedure itself, so the whole answer stays a guess.

The severed dog's head did not answer the question. It made the question visible. A response to a hammer is evidence of functioning tissue. It is not a declaration of a self.

Name the layers and the test sharpens. The metabolic layer asks whether tissue is alive. The neurological layer asks whether the nervous system is working. The conscious layer asks whether there is awareness. The identity layer asks whether autobiographical continuity holds. The social layer asks whether relationships and recognition still map onto the same person. When anything in biology seems to cross a boundary, ask which layer changed, and which layer is still you.
