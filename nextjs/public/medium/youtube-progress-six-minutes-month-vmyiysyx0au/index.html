In the 32nd month of her clarinet lessons, a student known as Clarissa practiced a jazz tune called Golden Wedding for 4.3 minutes, then returned to it for 1.6 more minutes, 5.9 minutes in all, according to the full text of Renwick and McPherson's 2002 article on ResearchGate, which may need a browser or an account to open. She had chosen the tune herself. Videotapes of her practice put her at 0.79 seconds per note in the score on assigned pieces and 9.83 seconds per note on this tune. A later chapter by McPherson and Renwick gives nearly 10 seconds, a twelvefold increase, for a clarinetist of twelve.

Daniel Coyle, author of The Talent Code (2009), tells this session as an example of deep practice, his term for working at the edge of current ability. In Coyle's retelling, Clarissa, 15 in the talk and about twelve in the papers, progressed more in a six-minute segment than in a month of normal practice. The 2002 article and the later chapter report no month of progress.

His account offers a learner or coach three figures to plan practice around, a month of progress from six minutes, fastest learning at roughly 20% mistakes, and 37 times better after a year of 1% daily gains. The sections below trace each to the papers and records, then give a practice rule and a check.

What the Clarissa papers measured

Renwick and McPherson's 2002 article measured time per note, which is the practice time she spent on each note in the score, and strategy use. Its minute and per-note figures appear in the full text and are absent from the Cambridge abstract page. The full text analyses two Year 3 sessions, and Golden Wedding came in the second, which the authors call "surprisingly atypical."

On Golden Wedding the article reports a marked increase in silent fingering, silent thinking and singing, deliberate changes of tempo when she repeated sections, which the authors call unexpected, and, in its discussion, humming, silent analysis and repetition of larger sections. The chapter describes what appears to be the same return as two more times and adds that in every other Year 3 observation she played a piece once. These are behaviors of one child in one session, and the article reports no outcome measure for the tune.

The 2002 authors say the case study confirms, "with strong empirical data," the common-sense view that children play pieces they like for longer. The chapter describes remarkable perseverance when she faced difficulties. Its authors write: "While of course limited in its generalizability, this vignette strikes us as a powerful example of the effect of interest and choice on self-regulated learning and mastery-oriented strategy use." Neither document tests whether the pattern carries over from a clarinet to software, sport or coaching.

Coyle's excerpt from The Talent Code says Clarissa used her energy to accomplish "a month's worth of practice in six minutes" and files the vignette under ignition, which it describes as the fuel for deep practice. His page for the book says specific kinds of practice can increase skill "up to ten times faster than conventional practice" and cites no study there. The 2002 article and the chapter report no tenfold speedup. Two other McPherson papers, a 2001 paper with Renwick (abstract only) and a 2005 paper (partly read), were not fully opened, so these negations cover the 2002 article and the chapter.

The 0.79 and 9.83 figures set assigned classical pieces against a jazz tune she chose, so choice and kind of music cannot be separated in these numbers, an inference from how the comparison is built.

Where the 20% error rate comes from

Coyle says "I call it the rule of 20%": someone making mistakes about 20% of the time is "probably in the sweet spot," no mistakes means no learning, and 80% mistakes is outside the sweet spot. He cites no study for the rate in the talk. An error rate here is the share of attempts that go wrong.

The closest research is a 2019 analysis in Nature Communications by Wilson, Shenhav, Straccia and Cohen. Under an assumption of Gaussian noise in the errors, the authors find an optimal training error rate of around 15.87%, or about 85% accuracy, and demonstrate it in simulations of artificial and biologically plausible neural networks on simple two-choice tasks. They say no formal theoretical work has addressed the effect of training accuracy on learning, and call a test of it an important direction for future work. Coyle's rule corresponds to 80% accuracy, the simulations' optimum to about 85%.

The paper also cites a design convention and an expectation. Staircase procedures, which adjust task difficulty so that the error rate stays fixed during learning, are commonly designed to produce about 80 to 85% accuracy, and participants given a free choice of difficulty will spontaneously choose tasks of intermediate difficulty as they learn. A University of Arizona summary has the lead author saying that human perceptual learning is the most likely fit.

Bjork and Bjork write that a difficulty is desirable when the learner can respond to it successfully, and undesirable when the learner lacks the background knowledge or skills to do so. Their chapter gives no numeric error rate. Among the sources opened, none measures learning rate against error rate in human skill practice.

Hotbed stories carry more than one explanation

Coyle calls the places he visited talent hotbeds, places that produced unusually many top performers, and counts Spartak, a run-down Moscow tennis club, among them, crediting its embrace of struggle and slow-motion stroke repetition. ESPN reports selection, with tryouts for five- and six-year-olds and a head coach saying Spartak keeps only the best. The Washington Post wrote in 2010 (archived copy) that parents provided the push, funding and often superb genes, and that many players say hardship helped build determined stars. The Independent reported in 2004 that tennis funding rose under Yeltsin.

For Florence, Coyle names the plague among historians' reasons for the Renaissance and says other places shared them. His answer is craft guilds, which he calls workshops that brought in young people. Smarthistory, writing about Renaissance Italy in general, treats the workshop as where training happened and describes guilds as professional or devotional associations. Britannica adds the patronage of merchants and bankers, chiefly Cosimo de' Medici and Giovanni Rucellai.

For Brazil, Coyle credits futsal and says that "until about 2009, I think" there were 39 international championships and Brazil won 38. FIFA's archive lists the first six editions, and RSSSF's table of winners shows Brazil winning four of them (1989, 1992, 1996 and 2008). The 38-of-39 figure could not be matched to those records or to those of AMF, a rival body. A peer-reviewed study names pelada, informal street football, as another pathway for Brazilian players.

Bilalic and colleagues, in an accepted manuscript, argue that samples of elite performers are filtered on the success being explained. The manuscript does not discuss hotbeds, so applying it to them is an inference from their definition by output.

Practice curves flatten, and estimates of practice's share of performance differ

A 2014 meta-analysis by Macnamara, Hambrick and Oswald, as revised in a 2018 corrigendum, put deliberate practice's share of performance variance at 23% in music and 20% in sports, from 1% in professions to 24% in games. Variance here is the share of differences between people a factor accounts for. Ericsson and Harwell report 29% uncorrected and 61% after a correction that uses reliabilities they estimated, 0.6 for practice estimates and 0.8 for performance. Hambrick, Macnamara and Oswald contest that reanalysis in a 2020 paper, which reports that with reliabilities of 0.8 for both, practice explains 49% of the reliable between-person variance in performance rather than 61%. In a re-run of the 1993 violin study, Macnamara and Maitra did not replicate the core finding that accumulated practice corresponded to each skill level. Less accomplished violinists did practice less, and the effect was substantial and considerably smaller than the original, 26% of variance against 48%. For a learner planning practice, the spread across fields and methods leaves the return on extra hours uncertain.

Coyle says improving 1% a day for a year makes a person 37 times better. The arithmetic holds, since 1.01 raised to the 365th power is about 37.8. Heathcote, Brown and Mewhort fitted curves to 40 datasets from laboratory response-time tasks, and their introduction states that improvement is rapid at first but decreases as the practitioner becomes more skilled.

New Scientist's 2009 review says the book's myelin mechanism boils down to little more than "practice makes perfect" though the reviewer would buy the book, and myelin remains a hypothesis for human skill learning, since Gibson and colleagues found in mice that oligodendrogenesis and myelination appear necessary for the observed improvement in motor function and Zatorre, Fields and Johansen-Berg call tying human imaging to cellular events challenging.

A practice rule drawn from the sources

These four steps are an inference from the Clarissa documents, Bjork and Bjork's chapter and the practice-curve study, and they were not tested as a package.

  • Choose material. Where you have a say, choose material you care about; the later chapter calls the Clarissa vignette a powerful example of the effect of interest and choice on self-regulated learning and mastery-oriented strategy use.
  • Stay on hard spots while attempts lead toward a fix. When repeated attempts do not, add the missing prerequisite or simplify first. The Bjork and Bjork condition on successful response is the basis, and the 2002 article reports Clarissa repeating larger sections and altering tempo when she repeated them.
  • Treat error targets as starting guesses. Whether the target is Coyle's 20% or the 15.87% from the 2019 analysis, adjust it by whether errors get corrected from one attempt to the next.
  • Expect smaller gains as skill rises. Set expectations from your own measured progress, since Heathcote, Brown and Mewhort open their study of practice curves by stating that improvement is rapid at first and then slows.

A check to run before relying on the rule

Over two or three sessions on one piece or task, a learner can record each spot stayed on and the number of attempts it took. At the start of the next session, before warming up, the learner can try each spot again and record whether the fix holds. Spots where it does not hold go back on the list with a new attempt count.

A coach who keeps one such log per student can compare, across material the student chose and material assigned, how many attempts a fix takes before it holds.
