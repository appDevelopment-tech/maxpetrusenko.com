The Spark Is a Slot Machine

Kindness wins the long game. First impressions stay contested. So why does distance still feel like love?

The research says kindness wins the relationships people say they want, and the culture swears the chase wins first impressions. There is truth in both, and the chase keeps its pull the way a slot machine does, through intermittent rewards and repeated tries. The safer bet is one rule, applied early, over any strategy built on distance.

The romance blueprint

On a relationship forum, a man describes an experiment he ran without meaning to. He had been cold and aloof, and it helped him win her over. Later, she asked him to go back to being distant, the way he was at the start. She eventually left him for an emotionally unavailable man, and he closed with the question that has echoed around this debate ever since: whether women wanted real feelings less, and the unavailable men more.

The movies agree with him, which is the first sign that something is off. In the romantic canon, a woman is engaged to the steady man, and the story treats him as an obstacle. Then the chaotic one arrives, and the story hands him everything. The love triangle has anchored romantic fiction for centuries, and the shape barely changes: the audience learns to root for the least stable option, and to read the safe man as a consolation prize.

One essay on enthusiasm dates the habit at two thousand years. What the culture calls a love story is at bottom one person trying their hardest to persuade another, disengaged and elsewhere, to look their way. The lesson installs early: love is what you win from someone who is holding back, and the difficulty becomes the proof. Yet in the choice experiments, the pattern flips, and the folk theory survives on a different mechanism, one that pays out in secret.

What the studies actually found

Start with the studies that tested the folk theory head on, because the theory loses there. In a set of studies published in 1995, women rated prosocial men as more physically attractive and more desirable as dates, and the dominance trait on its own added nothing.

A 2003 experiment went further and manipulated how nice a target man acted. Niceness improved women's actual choices, and it mattered most for serious relationships. In one scenario study cited there, 54 percent of the women chose the nice guy, while 18 percent chose the bad boy.

The strongest version of the bad-boy story lives in first impressions, and there the literature splits. A 2014 study found women rated a darker personality profile as more attractive than a control character, with looks held constant. A 2025 study found the opposite at both time scales: lower dark-triad levels were preferred, especially for long-term commitment.

Together, the direct-choice results line up with the long-term findings, even where the short-term ratings split. Kindness is no handicap in the relationships people describe wanting, and the chaotic profile is no reliable winner. The bad-boy pull shows up in attraction ratings that conflict across studies, which is exactly where a folk theory gets its fuel.

So the folk theory needs a different explanation, and the best one available is a machine.

The chemistry of the chase

In the psychology literature, the schedule that produces the most persistent behavior is the variable ratio: a reward after an unpredictable number of attempts. Gambling is the canonical case, and the machine's payouts arrive on a schedule the player can feel and cannot anticipate, which makes the behavior the hardest to stop.

Scarcity supplies the second piece: commodity theory holds that any good is valued to the extent that it is unavailable, and its formal statement counts delays and restrictions as forms of unavailability, alongside plain rarity.

Carry the analogy into attention, and dating clicks into focus. Attention that arrives in bursts, warm one week and gone the next, gets priced like a scarce good, and the person receiving it starts treating the quiet stretches as stakes and the warm ones as winnings. The schedule is indifferent to who runs it or why. It pays on its own terms, and the terms are the message.

A 2025 explainer notes that when care was inconsistent early in life, people "start to equate uncertainty with love" and small crumbs of affection register as "something real."

A psychologist's post draws the full cycle: idealization, withdrawal, anxious overcompensation and reconnection. The loop and romance blur together from the inside. From the outside they separate cleanly, because one of them is a conditioning schedule.

One caution keeps the claim precise: asked directly, anxiously attached people do not reliably prefer distant partners. The volatile pairings that show up in real relationships tend to form over time, once the cycle is running, rather than attract at first sight.

Meanwhile, the market has industrialized the schedule: a 2024 investigation found dating apps selling paid features that researchers likened to gambling products, and an addiction researcher quoted in the investigation compared the swipe design to a slot machine, endless chances with unknown payoffs.

What the market does with it

If the schedule rewards distance, someone will start supplying it on purpose. A 2012 thread contains the whole folk playbook in two lines: one poster reports that coldness helped him, and a reply generalizes the rule. People want what they think they cannot get, so act like you do not care.

The same thread carries its own rebuttal, which makes it worth reading past the first reply. One woman writes that healthy women want connection, and that acting distant attracts the wrong kind of person. Another flips the complaint: what gets rejected is often just the absence of attraction, with emotional distance cast as the cause.

In a 2021 marriage-forum thread, a woman writes that genuine interest from emotionally normal men reads as weakness, and that she is only at ease chasing men who need fixing. One poster on a support forum describes stable interest ending within weeks and the chaotic relationship becoming the obsession, up to being "almost addicted to the ups and downs."

The loudest online version treats it as an axiom: a woman will choose chaos over stability and the bad boy over the nice guy, "every single time." A pushback post with thousands of likes skips the argument and asks how he would know, since he is doing none of the choosing.

Underneath the axiom sits a real debate, and the debate has an entitlement problem on one side. A commentary that has circulated for a decade argues the friend-zone premise treats niceness as deserving of love, time, and affection, which converts generosity into an invoice. A recovering red-pill forum reaches the same place from the other side: the loudest complaints tend to come from pro-forma kindness performed with an expectation of reward, and the anger when the reward does not arrive is its own tell. A Guardian commentary from that era adds the correction underneath both: the friend zone is mostly confirmation bias, and couples who were friends first stay invisible to anyone sampling for evidence that the nice guy loses.

The incentive logic under all of this has a famous formal version: a 2004 theory paper analyzed heterosexual dating as a marketplace, with men offering resources and women setting the terms. Its sharpest line holds that men will do whatever is required to obtain sex. That line is also its most hazardous, because it implies the market standard, whatever it is, gets met. The performances track the feedback: distance appears to work, so the distance gets repeated.

The theory itself is contested, and treating it as settled would repeat the culture's mistake. A 2017 review catalogs a growing body of research contradicting the marketplace premise and argues the model weakens under the evidence. Use it as one lens, because the mechanism here does not stand or fall with it. What unpredictable reward reliably produces is persistence: the pursuit continues past reason, and the gesture that caused it rarely gets a clean answer.

A matchmaker's post describes the paradox from the supply side: the responsive, kind, emotionally available partner sometimes lands as flat, and the flatness gets filed under incompatibility.

The enthusiasm test

The diagnosis leaves one rule standing, and it is embarrassingly plain. The same essay states the rule without decoration: go out only with people who are "very enthusiastic about us," from the start, without the need for persuasion.

The second half of the test runs in the other direction. Kindness that arrives as a strategy is not kindness, and distance performed to create longing is not attraction. Perform kindness as a debt and it stops being a gift. Perform coldness as a strategy and it curdles into bait, and the fish eventually see the hook.

So the rule splits in two, and the second direction is where most people slip. If you are choosing, back the one whose enthusiasm needs no engineering. If you are being chosen, make your yes easy to read, because someone spent years learning the wrong lesson about what love costs.

The plot worth wanting

What is left is the story the movies rarely bother with, because it has no triangle. Two people are plainly keen on each other from the start, and the plot moves at the speed of their actual interest.

It has no chase, and anyone trained on the old blueprint will read that calm as a missing spark, which is the final trick: stillness gets discounted, and the discount is the price of admission.

The quiet traits score well wherever they get measured. It just loses to the drama in the stories people grew up on. So the correction is small and personal: stop auditioning for people who need convincing, and stop holding auditions of your own. Someone who is plainly glad you exist is not the boring option. Given how the culture's love stories tend to end, they are the rare happy one.
