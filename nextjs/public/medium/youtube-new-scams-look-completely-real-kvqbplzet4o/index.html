Nothing About These Ten Scams Looks Fake. That's the Scam.

From a read-back code to a cloned voice, ten documented schemes show that spotting fakes stopped working, and what to check instead.

Ten documented scams, one shared upgrade: almost nothing in them is fake. The code arrives from the real platform, the plea arrives from your friend's real account, and the test payment really clears. When every piece is genuine, reading messages more carefully stops working, and the check that survives is a second channel the requester cannot control.

It starts with a couch. You list it on Facebook Marketplace, a buyer messages within minutes, and the buyer has one small request. Facebook is about to text you a code to confirm you are a real seller. Could you send it over?

Nothing in that request is invented. Facebook really sends the code. The person asking triggered a login attempt on your account minutes earlier, and the text is the platform asking permission to finish it. Reading it back completes the login, and an account you have used for years changes hands. The new owner's first move is to message your friends as you: "Hey, I'm in trouble. Can you send me $200 on Venmo real quick?"

Every piece of that sequence works exactly as designed, and the problem is where the fake sits. There often is not one. Line up ten current scams and the same assembly shows up in all of them.

The code was real, and that was the problem

The friends who send money have a good reason. The message comes from you, your photo and your history attach to it, and it sounds like you. A stranger's plea gets a closed tab. Yours gets an answer, which is the reaction the theft was engineered to produce.

The read-back request is a documented marketplace trick, sometimes dressed up as a Google Voice number claim (ScamAdviser), and platform safety pages have warned for years about contacts that push a sale off the marketplace (Meta).

The stakes are not hypothetical. Reported fraud losses in the United States topped $12.5 billion in 2024, up about 25 percent in a single year (FTC).

One rule covers every version of the opening scene. A code texted to your phone exists for one reason, to prove that you are the person signing in. Nobody legitimate ever asks you to share it. No platform, no buyer, no bank. On Marketplace, the request to read back a code is the scam.

The sticker on the meter

You park, and the meter carries a sticker over its payment hardware: scan to pay. The page that opens wears the city's logo, takes the card number, and shows a confirmation screen, and by the time you walk away the number is being spent elsewhere. Scammers print overlays and stick them over real meter codes; the FTC has issued alerts (FTC), and police departments keep finding fake codes on their streets (New Westminster police).

The setup costs a printer and sticker paper, and cheap setups spread fast. The check starts in your fingers. Real codes sit printed into the machine or set behind metal, and a sticker peels at the corner. If you scan anyway, read the web address before typing anything, because the city collects its parking money on its own domain (FTC). The coin slot and the card reader on the machine have never been cloned.

A friend reports you, then a stranger helps

A friend messages you in a panic. They accidentally reported your account, it is flagged for deletion, and they already found an admin who can fix it. A second profile arrives, official-looking and urgent, and the fix takes one step: confirm your password.

The friend did not send that. Their account was stolen first, and the thief worked down their friends list because a message from someone you trust clears a bar a stranger never clears (Malwarebytes). The admin is the same thief wearing a second profile.

No support person will ever ask for your password, and none of this gets fixed in a direct message. Stop replying. Call your friend on the number already in your contacts, because they most likely do not know they are locked out of their own account.

The flaw was added on purpose

The blender has thousands of five-star reviews, and they sound like neighbors: the smoothies come out smooth, the kids actually drink them, the lid is a little loud. The farms behind that shelf run on prompts, and the small complaint is engineered. Real reviews carry flaws, so the fakes arrive with one (The Verge).

August 2024 gave buyers a receipt. The FTC finalized a rule that bans fake and AI-generated reviews, with civil penalties attached (FTC). Until enforcement catches up, the durable filter is boring. Skip the average and read the one- and two-star reviews, and check the dates, because hundreds of reviews landing inside one week is a production schedule.

The money that was never real

A stranger sends you money by mistake, with an apology attached. So sorry, wrong person, please send it back. You send it back, because returning a stranger's money is what decent people do. A week later the app pulls the same amount from your balance: the original payment rode a stolen card, the cardholder disputed it, and the reversal landed on the account that still had funds in it (r/Scams).

The transfer you received was never money. The transfer you sent was. The setup never needs you to be greedy. It runs on politeness, which is the default most people bring to a stranger's mistake.

The fix is to route it through support instead of your own transfer history. Report the payment and let the platform unwind it, because a legitimate mistake does not need money back from your balance, and that difference is exactly the seam the scam uses.

A friendship with a withdrawal problem

The first message is meant for someone else. Hey, are we still on for dinner? You answer that it is the wrong number, and the stranger is gracious, then keeps talking. Weeks pass. The new friend remembers your dog, your job, the trip you mentioned, and one evening mentions an investment platform that has been treating them well. You test it with a modest deposit, and the test passes: the balance grows, and a withdrawal lands in your bank account. So you commit more.

The withdrawals stop cooperating at size. A tax clearance fee, then a verification fee, then silence, and the site goes dark (National Cybersecurity Alliance). This scheme, known as pig butchering, carries the largest numbers in the set: scam networks in the Asia-Pacific pulled an estimated $88 billion to $114 billion in a single year, according to a UN estimate (Reuters).

The first withdrawal that clears is the product demo. It exists to convert you, and every fee after it is collection.

The job that was never open

A recruiter finds you on LinkedIn about a remote role at a company you recognize. The screening moves unusually fast. The offer letter arrives on letterhead before the process should be able to finish, and onboarding asks for your Social Security number, a copy of your license, and bank details for direct deposit. The company never posted the role, and its HR department does not know your name (FBI).

The tells stack up: contact out of nowhere, a process compressed into days, an offer with no real video call, and a recruiter writing from a Gmail address while the company's actual people write from its own domain. Before documents move, find the company's HR number yourself and ask whether the role and the offer are real. The pipeline is built on applicants being too excited to make that call.

IC3, the FBI's complaint center, logged 16,012 victims of employment scams in 2020 alone, with more than $59 million in losses (FBI release).

A boardroom of ghosts

A finance employee in Hong Kong sat down for a video call with his chief financial officer and several colleagues, and the meeting walked him through a transfer of about $25 million before it ended. Every other participant was generated from public footage, and the engineering firm Arup later confirmed it had been the victim, at a loss of about £20 million (CNN, The Guardian).

What made the room work was its ordinariness. Familiar faces, routine business, nothing that announced itself as a crime. It felt like a Wednesday.

Companies have a countermeasure even though it sounds like a spy movie. Before large transfers move, the requester must supply a code phrase that was never spoken on a call or written into an email where a scraper could reach it. The phrase is the part of the company a generated face cannot wear.

Your phone number is the master key

Your phone loses service and a restart does not help, because your number now sits on a thief's SIM. A caller who sounded prepared answered your carrier's security questions from old breach data and talked a support representative into moving the number, and the codes that open your bank, your email, and your wallet begin arriving on a device in someone else's pocket (FCC). A documented case in California drained about $24 million in crypto this way (Krebs on Security).

Two settings change the math. A port freeze or SIM lock at your carrier means your number cannot move without photo ID presented in a store, and almost nobody turns it on. Wherever an app can generate your login codes, replace text-message two-factor, because the app keeps its codes on your device, out of reach of a swapped SIM (FCC order).

A few seconds of your voice

A mother picks up her phone and hears her daughter crying, kidnapped, begging her to pay and stay on the line. The voice is right, and cloning one has gotten cheap: a vendor demo showed a usable clone built from a few seconds of audio (McAfee). Independent researchers note that clone quality tracks audio length and quality, so read the few-seconds figure as a vendor benchmark (WIRED). One mother, a Navy veteran, wired $5,400 after a call built from her daughter's voice (Fox News). The FBI logs the pattern as virtual kidnapping and has warned that generative tools now supply the audio evidence, recordings included (IC3).

The countermeasure costs nothing and has never failed for lack of technology. Pick a family word, random and never posted or texted, and make the first question of any panicked call the same one. What is the word? A clone can reproduce a voice, a panic, and crying in the background. It cannot supply a phrase it has never heard.

The second step that ends all ten

Line the schemes up and the shared costume is visible. Each one verifies itself with something real: your login code, your friend's account, the city's meters, the shelf of reviews, your manners, your job search, your boss's face, your number, your voice. Every piece gets borrowed because it is trusted, and the borrowing keeps getting cheaper. A careful eye alone does not hold, because there is often nothing false in the message to catch. What holds is a second step, taken in a channel the requester cannot reach:

  • Codes. Anyone who asks you to read, forward, or confirm a code is attacking you. No exceptions for employers, buyers, or banks.
  • Money. Requests to return or resend money go through the platform's support flow, never through the requester's instructions.
  • Identity. Claims from a friend, a bank, or an employer get a callback to the number you already have, never the number in the message.
  • Voice. Claims that depend on a familiar voice get tested against a word that never went online.

None of it requires treating strangers as enemies. It requires one extra step, taken on your own side of the table.

The cheapest test left

The imitation will keep improving, because the tools under it improve, and the next versions will arrive with better voices, cleaner pages, and faces that move more naturally. The defense that holds is older than any of it: a word your family knows, a freeze on your number, a number you dialed from your own contacts. It is undramatic and nearly free, so almost nobody sets it up before the first call comes.

Pick the word this week. Ask your carrier about the freeze. Build the second step while the stakes are hypothetical, because the reason you will need it will arrive sounding exactly like someone you love.
