Lilly's $2.8 Billion Psychedelic Bet Hangs on One Hidden Number

Eli Lilly paid about $2.8 billion for a depression drug that reported a 12-point win. Before believing it, ask the question no trial in the class has answered: could patients and raters tell who got the drug?

One dose of a short-acting psychedelic, most of it over by the 90-minute discharge check, is what Eli Lilly paid about $2.8 billion upfront to own. The bet inside that price is on trial design: a control dose small enough to feel like nothing, four layers of masking aimed at the problem no trial in this class has solved. The design is documented, and the proof it held remains unpublished.

One dose, one chair, one check at 90 minutes

One dose of BPL-003 is an intranasal spray, and most of it is over within a lunch break. A staff member stays in the room, marks the 90-minute point, and a discharge check decides whether the patient leaves. That is the treatment for depression that has survived two or more drugs, and it is what Eli Lilly paid about $2.8 billion upfront to own.

The deal was announced in July and closed in September. An established drug company now owns a psychedelic program outright. But what it paid for is mostly a set of decisions about how the drug gets tested.

Psychedelics keep failing to become medicine less because they do not work in trials than because you cannot hide the trip. Patients know when they have been dosed, and so do the raters scoring them. The design of BPL-003 is engineered around it: a control dose small enough to feel like nothing, four layers of masking, a chair that empties at 90 minutes.

That is the bet inside the price. The design sits on the record in a registry entry, a protocol, a filing. The guess rates that would settle it are not public, and the price hangs on that gap.

Two walls and the FDA's MDMA file

The first wall is hours: a psychedelic session runs long, and someone sits with the patient throughout, in a room that bills nothing else. That is closer to inpatient care than to psychiatry, and payers notice. The second wall is the one this deal is built around.

The evidence is a program that fell on that wall. In June 2024, an FDA advisory committee voted 9-2 that MDMA-assisted therapy had not demonstrated efficacy, and 10-1 on its risk-benefit profile. The Complete Response Letter followed in August, citing how adverse events were characterized and finding durability beyond 18 weeks not demonstrated. The rejection rested on conduct and follow-through.

The guessing numbers did the most damage. The FDA's briefing document recorded about 90 percent of MDMA participants and 75 percent of placebo participants guessing their assignment correctly; a published analysis put investigator guesses at 94 and 76 percent. When guessing runs that high, the blind is gone, and the thing a randomized trial exists to do goes with it.

The trial under the application reported a real effect: MAPP2 found CAPS-5 improvements of 23.7 points against 14.8, and 71.2 percent of the drug group no longer met the criteria for PTSD. The agency still could not get past the guessing.

The field carries other scars. Compass Pathways cut about 30 percent of staff and pushed timelines in 2024; atai did the same after a 2023 miss. ICER, which prices drugs for insurers, judged the evidence for MDMA-assisted therapy inadequate in 2024 and declined to set a cost-effectiveness benchmark. And trialists are blunt: there is no true placebo for psychedelics, active placebos rarely succeed in masking, and every design is an approximation.

Everything BPL-003 does differently answers those two walls.

What they built

BPL-003 is mebufotenin, a synthetic benzoate form of 5-MeO-DMT, a molecule humans have used for decades, and studied in numbers measured in dozens. It is a Schedule I controlled substance, and its pharmacology leans on serotonin 1A receptors rather than the 2A receptors that drive a psilocybin trip, one reason the experience runs short.

The delivery is the engineering: a benzoate salt improves stability, and the nose does the rest. In a phase 1 study of 44 healthy volunteers, peak concentrations arrived in about 8 to 10 minutes, the mean half-life ran under 27 minutes, and effects generally resolved within 45 to 90 minutes. That profile is the product: a two-hour visit, a chair, a check.

Psychological support is part of the design. The registry lists support before, during, and after each dose, and the trial stacks every safeguard the field has learned to want: 193 patients, 38 sites, six countries, randomized to 12 mg, 8 mg, or a 0.3 mg control, masked four ways (participant, investigator, assessor, sponsor), with checkpoints at day 2, 8, 29, and 57.

The company reported the results on July 1, 2025, and the middle dose won: 12.1 points at 8 mg and 11.1 at 12 mg on the MADRS at day 29, against 5.8 for the control. The curve came out inverted, and the company selected 8 mg for phase 3.

At day 57 the active arms still led: 10.8 points at 12 mg and 10.2 at 8 mg, against 5.2 for the control. Keeping most of an effect eight weeks after a single dose is the claim the deal leans on.

The price list

The deal's fine print reads better than its headline. Shareholders got $6.75 a share in cash plus a Contingent Value Right worth up to $2.50 across three milestones; one pays $0.50 if BPL-003 wins U.S. approval and the DEA reschedules it within five years. A payout tied to a scheduling decision is a price tag on a political problem.

The politics are real: 5-MeO-DMT sits on Schedule I and has stayed there through every wave of psychedelic enthusiasm. An approved drug can remain a Schedule I drug; the CVR prices that possibility.

The scale: about $2.8 billion upfront, up to $1 billion more on milestones, up to about $3.8 billion total. Underneath sits a single 193-patient trial and a plan.

Analysts put peak sales above $1 billion a year. Lilly's neuroscience president, David Skovronsky, told CNBC the class amounts to a very different type of medicine, dosed a couple of times a year. The framing does a job: it sells the appointment rather than the experience.

The sharpest skepticism came from the same place as the money. In investor forums, the complaint was less that the drug cannot work than that the value sits in a salt, a spray, and a schedule. One thread reached for the morphine-to-oxycodone analogy, a good drug repackaged for a bigger market. That analogy is an argument, and people are making it.

The clock is long: Phase 3 is planned for the second quarter of 2026, data expected in 2029. The program's head start, Breakthrough Therapy Designation from October 2025, speeds review but is not approval.

The experiment inside the experiment

The field's record on hiding the trip is measurable, and it is bad. A systematic review of 112 randomized trials found only 29.5 percent checked whether masking held. Where they did, unblinding exceeded 90 percent in several drug classes, and 57.1 percent of the studies flagged blinding as a limitation. No strategy, inert, active, or low-dose, consistently produced an ideal blind.

The drugs make hiding harder. A meta-analysis of therapeutic psilocybin doses found significantly more anxiety, about 2.3 times the placebo rate, plus nausea and blood-pressure elevation; in a DMT infusion study, higher rates meant significantly more anxiety. The experiences trials try to hide are memorable, physical, and loud.

BPL-003's answer is visible in the choices. The 0.3 mg control registers as something subtle without being the full trip, and the four-way masking keeps the assignment from everyone with a stake in the result. What is missing is evidence that either worked: the phase 2b guess rates remain unpublished. The inverted dose-response sharpens the question, since the 12 mg arm, which performed worst, plausibly produced the most memorable experiences.

Watch one number in the next readout: the guess rate before the effect size. If participants and raters were near chance, the results mean more; if the guessing looks like the MDMA file, discount accordingly. That question is what the money is underwriting.

What the price still has to survive

Durability is the first test. The effect holds through day 57 on the company's numbers, but the program is one trial old, the full dataset has not been peer reviewed, and at least one market note flags weeks four to six as the soft spot, where the effect dips before recovering. The price sits on that curve.

The safety file is thinner than the efficacy file. A systematic review found just three clinical trials of 5-MeO-DMT in humans, 78 participants, most followed a week or less; transient heart-rate and blood-pressure rises resolved within 90 minutes in trial data.

Outside trials, the picture changes. In a survey of past-year 5-MeO-DMT users, 95 percent reported at least one of 13 adverse outcomes, with worsening and improvement roughly even. That survey covers self-selected users in uncontrolled settings, a different population from monitored patients; documented fatalities also sit outside clinical settings.

Payers are the last wall. ICER judged the evidence for MDMA-assisted therapy inadequate in 2024 and declined to set a cost-effectiveness benchmark. BPL-003 is unpriced, and the bar for a real effect is modest: the average antidepressant beats placebo by roughly two points on these scales, below the 3-point line NICE treats as clinically significant, and establishing that the class beats placebo at all took 522 trials and 116,477 patients. Against that baseline, a 12-point reading is large, and the guess rate decides how much of it survives scrutiny.

The mundane constraints may matter most. The business model needs rooms, hours, and staff trained to sit with someone in an altered state, and practitioners modeling the rollout point to monitoring capacity rather than drug supply as the binding constraint. The two-hour slot that makes the math work is the hardest thing to scale.

The 1922 file

The template for this deal is a century old. In May 1922, researchers in Toronto reached a collaboration agreement with Lilly to scale up insulin; by 1923 the company was selling Iletin, the first commercial insulin, and the Nobel had gone to Banting and Macleod. Lilly did not win by owning a better molecule; it won by making a strange substance ordinary: stable, dosed, shipped.

By 2026, ordinary means a two-hour clinic slot and a trial masked four ways. The molecule was never the hard part. The blind is the last strange thing left, and the guess data that would show whether it held are still unpublished.
