The Easiest Way to Make the World Smarter Was Already in Your Kitchen

It cost almost nothing, required no new habit, and reached millions through something people already used every day.

TLDR: Iodized salt is a tiny public-health intervention with an outsized payoff. Iodine lets the thyroid make hormones that build the brain before birth and regulate metabolism throughout life. Salt delivers the dose cheaply, reliably, and without asking millions of people to change their daily routine or visit a clinic.

The source then moves from the salt shaker to history. Switzerland began adding iodine to salt in the 1920s, and Michigan followed with iodized salt in 1924 after local surveys found the Great Lakes region had a visible goiter problem. The intervention worked because the carrier was already on the table; nobody had to remember a new tablet.

The salt on my kitchen counter is a small engineering decision disguised as a condiment.

It looks like a white crystal. It behaves like a mineral. Yet some of it carries a trace amount of iodine, a chemical element that, in larger quantities, is poisonous and reactive. The useful dose depends on context, and it is small enough to disappear into a routine.

The element that has to arrive in almost invisible amounts

Iodine is the heaviest element the human body requires for normal biology. The thyroid gland uses it to make two hormones, T3 and T4. Their names are chemical bookkeeping: T3 contains three iodine atoms; T4 contains four.

Those hormones set the pace at which cells use energy. During pregnancy and early childhood, they also help build tissues and organs, especially the brain. When iodine is scarce, the thyroid keeps asking for more. The pituitary gland continues releasing thyroid-stimulating hormone, and the thyroid can enlarge into a goiter. The National Institutes of Health iodine fact sheet describes the same mechanism and the risks of both deficiency and excess.

That visible swelling is the symptom people recognize. The more serious damage can happen before a child is born, when inadequate thyroid hormone can impair physical development, hearing, speech, and cognition, as summarized by the World Health Organization. The required amount is tiny, yet a deficiency can alter development across an entire population.

Iodine deficiency is easy to misunderstand because iodine is abundant in the world. The oceans contain an estimated 90 billion tons of it, but seawater spreads that supply so thinly that the concentration is only about 0.06 parts per million, according to the U.S. Geological Survey. In many inland regions, the soil and water contain little iodine, so local crops and livestock cannot reliably provide enough.

In iodine-deficient regions, geography once predicted a meaningful part of developmental risk. Mountain valleys, inland plains, and other areas with iodine-poor environments carried heavy burdens of goiter and childhood developmental impairment.

Switzerland found the distribution system

In the early twentieth century, Switzerland became the clearest example. Some remote areas reported goiter rates approaching 94 percent among men, and as many as 10 percent of babies showed signs of severe iodine-deficiency disease. These figures come from the historical account documented in Bürgi, Supersaxo, and Selz, a review of iodine prophylaxis in Switzerland.

Swiss physician Heinrich Hunziker proposed that the environment was starving the thyroid of iodine. His practical question was harder: how could a country give everyone the same tiny amount without asking every household to measure a chemical?

The practical answer was to add potassium iodide to salt.

Salt has properties a public-health intervention normally has to buy at enormous cost. It is cheap, stable, easy to transport, available year-round, and consumed in a fairly regular pattern. People already seek a baseline amount of sodium because nerves and muscles need it, while taste limits how much most people add.

In 1918, physician Otto Bayard tested the idea in a remote village affected by goiter, as documented in the historical record summarized by Bürgi, Supersaxo, and Selz. He mixed potassium iodide into salt and gave it to five families. The winter train stopped running before he could return. When he came back in spring, no one had been harmed and the families' goiters had disappeared.

Bayard expanded the trial to more than 1,000 people over six months. The result held. In 1922, Hunziker, Bayard, and Swiss health leaders formed a commission to implement iodized salt nationally. Within a year, iodized salt was sold across more than half the country, accompanied by local education rather than a complicated medical bureaucracy.

The intervention succeeded because it attached a biological requirement to a habit that already existed.

Michigan had a military problem before it had a nutrition policy

The United States arrived at the same solution through a different pressure.

In 1917, more than 20,000 men in northern Michigan were disqualified from military service because of goiters. School surveys found goiter rates between 70 and 100 percent in some communities, according to the historical account in the NIH Office of Dietary Supplements review. The region became known as the goiter belt.

Dr. David Cowie of the Michigan Medical Society spent two years organizing nearly 200 statewide lectures for about 26,000 people, figures reported in the NIH iodine review. He persuaded local salt producers that iodized salt could improve health without changing the price or taste.

In May 1924, five producers introduced iodized salt. Within months, it reached 90 percent of salt sales in Michigan. Morton Salt, the largest American brand, initially hesitated because separating Michigan-bound product from the rest of its supply chain was impractical. Cowie's results changed the calculation. Morton began offering iodized salt nationwide.

Within a decade, fewer than 2 percent of children in the Detroit area had goiters, a decline of more than 90 percent from the peak, according to the NIH iodine review.

This is the part that makes the kitchen counter historically strange. The breakthrough did not depend on persuading every family to buy a new device or attend a clinic. It changed a processing step at the factory, then let ordinary distribution do the rest.

The cognitive effect was discovered later

Goiters were visible, so early programs could measure them. The largest effect of iodization was harder to see.

In 2013, Feyrer, Politi, and Weil used military draft records to compare roughly two million American men born before and after salt iodization. The study's published full text is available through PMC. In the quarter of the country with the greatest iodine deficiency, recruits born after iodization scored about 15 IQ points higher on military cognitive exams in the most deficient areas, and about 3.5 points when averaged across the United States.

The researchers estimated that iodization added around 180 million IQ points across American births from the beginning of the program through World War II. That comparison is quasi-experimental historical inference, not a randomized trial, and the 180 million figure is an estimate, not a literal pile of points. It should not be treated as a guarantee for any individual; it measures a population-level shift associated with correcting a widespread deficiency.

Later work in China, Albania, and New Zealand found related effects from iodine deficiency and supplementation, although results vary by population, baseline nutrition, and study design. The cautious conclusion is still enormous: preventing iodine deficiency can protect cognitive development at a scale individual treatment rarely reaches.

The economics are equally unusual. The source video places the cost at roughly five cents per person per year; that is an estimate, not a universal price. The WHO iodine guidance and NIH fact sheet support the broader point that iodization uses tiny amounts and requires monitoring. The exact price varies by country, salt-production method, and program, but the basic intervention remains cheap because the iodine is added in tiny concentrations. In the United States, iodide is mixed into salt at about 0.01 percent, per the NIH iodine fact sheet.

The addition happens at the end of the process, where a nozzle applies the iodide solution before packaging.

The intervention can succeed and still need maintenance

There is a catch. Salt iodization is not automatic just because the chemistry is simple.

Only about 53 percent of table salt sold in the United States is iodized, according to the NIH iodine fact sheet. Many people now get less iodine from iodized salt because they eat less packaged salt, use specialty salts, avoid salt, or rely on foods whose iodine content varies. Seafood and dairy can contribute iodine, but access and dietary pattern differ widely.

Too little iodine is dangerous. Too much can also disrupt thyroid function, especially for people with existing thyroid disease. Universal supplementation therefore needs standards, quality control, monitoring, and public communication. The goal is a reliable small dose, not a race toward more.

This is where many simple public-health stories become misleading. A successful intervention still has to be measured. It has to account for changing diets. It has to watch for groups at risk of deficiency and groups that may be sensitive to excess. It must keep the production and testing step reliable after the original crisis fades from memory.

The forgotten nature of iodization is evidence of success, but it is also a maintenance problem. People do not remember the world before a prevention program works.

What this teaches about making systems smarter

The most useful lesson is not “put iodine in salt.” It is to look for the existing path that reaches the most people with the least new behavior.

The pattern applies beyond nutrition. Fluoride in municipal water and folic acid in flour use the same design instinct: move the intervention closer to the point where a system is already operating. Find the bottleneck, attach a safe correction to an existing route, measure the outcome that matters, and keep monitoring after the original crisis fades from memory.

That approach has limits. It can hide important choices from the people affected. It can create overconfidence in a population average. It can fail when the carrier habit is uneven or when the dose is not monitored. Good infrastructure is quiet, but it still needs accountability.

A better question is practical: what is the smallest safe change that can travel through a behavior people already have?

Iodized salt answered that question with a few invisible atoms. The result was fewer goiters, fewer cases of severe developmental impairment, and a measurable rise in cognitive performance across millions of people. The world became smarter through a supply chain most people never noticed.
