The Easiest Way to Scam the Government Is to Follow the Rules

When ownership, technical judgment, and accountability live in different rooms, waste does not need a villain.

Government waste survives because the process launders responsibility. Every individual step looks compliant while the result is absurd: a modernization program that never arrives, a contract that dodges scrutiny, an engineer who knows the answer and holds no power.

On the IRS customer-service calls, a taxpayer who needs to hand over a document gets told here is your fax number. The most common reply in the history of that sentence is, I do not know how to send a fax. The agent has no alternative, because fax is the only channel the IRS will accept for a document. The person on the other end has to go figure out how to use one. In the interview, the Treasury executive says the agency takes in about sixty million faxes a year across roughly fifty thousand active fax lines.

The people who run the IRS still treat fax as the gold standard of information security, and that claim held up twenty-five years ago. It describes a memory now rather than a working safeguard. When the new executive at Treasury asks which office owns the fax policy, the Office of Tax Policy points at the IRS, and the IRS points somewhere else. Nobody owns the fax machine, and everybody can name a rule that nobody can be blamed for. That machine is the whole subject of this piece.

A country loses money while every step of the loss follows a rule, and nobody in the chain has to be corrupt. Nobody even has to be lazy, because the rules do the harm and hand out a receipt. The interview behind this piece is worth your time, and it is a long conversation with a technology executive who ran a health-technology company before joining the Treasury as its chief information officer, where he worked on the IRS and the DOGE-adjacent reform: Inside DOGE, The IRS & How to Scam the US Government. He spent months inside the machine, and what he describes is stranger than a scandal. It is a system producing a bad outcome in full compliance with itself.

The modernization program that never arrives

The conversation opens with the IRS modernization program because it is almost embarrassing as a symbol. Officials kept calling it five years away in 1991, and decades later it was still five years away. A 2025 Tax Notes report, citing Treasury officials, described the modernization effort as roughly fifteen billion dollars over budget and decades behind schedule. The official trail agrees, because GAO said in 2002 the shortfalls traced to weak estimating and that delays and overruns would keep coming until estimating improved. GAO wrote again in 2006 that the Business Systems Modernization had suffered delays and cost overruns with weak requirements and thin management.

People hear modernization and picture a technology story. The real problem sits one layer down in ownership. An old mainframe running batch data processing does a job it is good at, and the IRS does enormous batch data processing. The trouble starts when a system is old enough to carry obligations, new enough to demand integrations, and fragmented enough that nobody can say where one responsibility ends. He says he spent two days inside one IRS compliance facility watching the systems run, and he counted at least sixty systems that did not communicate with one another. He called what he saw the most complicated Rube Goldberg machine he had ever seen. A data source could live in one part of the agency and never reach the place that needed it.

Procurement turns competence into a permission problem

The most useful stretch of the interview sits under the DOGE headlines in the procurement complaint. A technical team needs a tool, the engineer writes the requirement, and the request enters procurement, where it disappears into a black box. The people who understand the problem lose control over the vendor decision and then inherit whatever the process selects.

The rules that govern this all carry a moral story. Competitive bidding protects the public, salary caps keep pay fair, contracting rules block favoritism, and small-business programs open access. Every one of these rules deserves to exist on its own. They also shape what happens when money runs through them, because any threshold creates a behavior around the threshold and any fast lane to a category teaches someone to package a purchase around the category.

The interviewee reviewed contracts clustered just below a review threshold: $24.9 million, $24.9 million, $24.9 million. That pattern comes from the interview rather than an independent audit, so it should be treated as an allegation rather than a settled finding. The mechanism showed up everywhere he went. If nobody technical can challenge the bundle, the paperwork becomes the product. The public believes it bought software, the agency believes it followed process, the vendor believes it won a contract, and the engineer inherits a tool-shaped burden. Every one of them can point straight at a rule.

The interview got blunter on pricing. He described one vendor that took the government for a ride on cost, and a contract that went from about two million dollars a year to about a hundred million dollars a year at renewal, all under a label of pilot pricing. The interview also describes abuse around contracting pathways, including the SBA's 8(a) program and other nonstandard procurement routes. He described buyers staging auctions among resellers to land a license at a price they wanted. Those programs have legitimate policy purposes, and skipping open competition by itself is evidence of a fast lane, not proof of fraud. He says one person investigating procurement abuse estimated losses as high as eighty billion dollars a year, while his own estimate ran closer to thirty billion. Those figures are source claims, not independently established totals.

You cannot fire your way out of this

Here is the part that flattens most reform talk. A federal manager who finds the good people and the bad people has one problem, because he cannot fire the bad ones. The civil-service protection that stops him goes back to the Pendleton Civil Service Reform Act of 1883, and it exists for reasons that made sense at the time. The interview argues the protection may have outlived its reasons, and enforcing it means a staff that can slow a decision to a crawl and absorb any pressure to move.

The whole game inside the building is cover your ass, and every decision goes through a committee so that nobody can be blamed. The interviewee describes one move that actually worked. He put a group of IRS IT leaders on administrative leave because they lacked the technical skill the role demanded, and a week of panic followed. Then things kept arriving, and work that had been stuck for years started getting delivered in weeks, because the people making the calls were the technical people who understood the calls. His best engineer told him, I do not know if this is good, and it is definitely the most different it has ever been here. That veteran had been in the building for more than a decade.

The mechanical fix comes down to three checkable things. A working institution needs an artifact that proves the work improved, an owner who can change the system, and a stop rule that kills the project when the evidence says it should. A project missing an artifact is a report of theater, a project missing an owner drifts, and a project missing a stop rule becomes a subscription to failure. The reform fights skip past these to argue about spending, staffing, and ideology, and those arguments matter. The operational test still comes first, because it is the only one that catches the man who points at a rule.

The better version of the news

A waste story usually gets told as a villain story. The shape this interview keeps pointing at is calmer and much harder to stop. It is a large system producing a bad outcome from ordinary people doing compliant steps with stale incentives and nobody close enough to the result to be embarrassed. A scam that breaks the law is at least visible, and a scam that follows the process hides in plain sight. It arrives wearing a badge, a procurement vehicle, and a steering committee.

The fix is to shorten the distance between a decision and the evidence it claims to obey. Let technical people judge technical claims, let users expose broken services, and let payment systems publish audit surfaces. Let procurement include the engineer who has to live with the tool, and let old systems survive when they are the right tool. Let new systems die when they become monuments to sunk cost.

A country gets modern through a short feedback loop, and the loop gets short when a decision and its evidence live close enough that somebody feels it when the decision is wrong. When the loop runs long, a bad outcome comes with a fully compliant paper trail, and that is the scam. It just runs on paperwork.
