The Easiest Way to Scam the Government Is to Follow the Rules

the CEO’s IRS story turns DOGE noise into a colder systems lesson: when procurement, technical leadership, and accountability fail, waste stops needing a villain. (Inside DOGE, The IRS & How to Scam the US Government - the CEO)

Government waste often survives because the process itself launders responsibility. A bad system can make every individual step look compliant while the final result is absurd: a modernization program that keeps receding, a contract that dodges scrutiny, or engineers separated by management layers.

The cleanest scam in a bureaucracy wears a badge and a workflow.

A form moves to the next desk. A request enters a competitive bid. A modernization program gets another budget line. A technical decision becomes a procurement decision. The person who knows the system lacks purchasing power. The person with purchasing power lacks technical judgment. The vendor that wins the process becomes the answer before the problem is understood.

Nobody has to feel corrupt. That is the dangerous part.

The source that sparked this piece is the host’s long interview with the CEO, the Levels CEO who describes being brought into Treasury work around IRS modernization and DOGE-adjacent reform. The headline sounds like a political episode. The stronger story is stranger and more useful: a country can lose money while following its own rules.

That frame excuses nothing. It is the reason fraud, waste, and stupidity can share the same hallway.

The machine can be broken while every step is approved

the CEO opens with the IRS modernization problem because it is almost too perfect as a symbol. He describes a program that was already “five years away” in 1991 and still somehow five years away decades later. He calls it roughly $15 billion over budget in the interview, which is the kind of number that should make every sentence after it slower and more careful.

The official audit trail supports the shape of the problem even when exact live totals need current verification. GAO warned in 2002 that IRS modernization shortfalls were tied to weak estimating practices and that, until estimating improved, project delays and cost overruns were likely to continue. GAO was still writing in 2006 that IRS Business Systems Modernization had suffered delays and cost overruns, including inadequate requirements development and management.

That matters because “modernization” sounds like a technology story.

It is usually an ownership story.

Old systems can be perfectly rational. the CEO makes a useful distinction later in the source: some mainframes are excellent at batch data processing, and the IRS does a lot of batch data processing. Age by itself is the wrong test. The dangerous system is old enough to carry obligations, new enough to demand integrations, and fragmented enough that nobody can say where one responsibility ends and another begins.

A toaster can be old and still make toast.

A toaster connected to 108 other toaster-shaped compliance systems becomes infrastructure archaeology.

Procurement turns competence into a permission problem

The most useful section of the source sits beneath the headline DOGE material. It is the procurement complaint.

A technical team needs a specific tool. The engineer writes up the requirement. The request enters procurement. From there, the CEO describes it as a black box. The people who understand the technical problem often lose control over the vendor decision, then inherit whatever the process selected.

This is how a rule becomes a maze.

Competitive bidding is supposed to protect the public. Salary caps are supposed to keep compensation fair. Contracting rules are supposed to prevent favoritism. Small-business and disadvantaged-business programs are supposed to open access.

Each rule has a moral story.

The system fails when the moral story becomes a bypass.

the CEO describes reviewing contracts clustered just below a threshold: $24.9 million, $24.9 million, $24.9 million. His specific claim belongs to the source record, but the mechanism is familiar beyond this one interview. Any threshold creates behavior around the threshold. If a process gives a fast lane to a category, eventually someone learns to package around the category. If nobody technical can challenge the package, the paperwork becomes the product.

The line worth keeping is simple.

The paperwork becomes the product.

The public thinks it bought software. The agency thinks it followed process. The vendor thinks it won a contract. The engineer gets a tool-shaped burden. Everyone can point at a rule.

waste can look polite.

A budget can be huge and still feel starved

There is an apparent contradiction in the interview that explains more than the political argument does.

Government budgets are enormous. Teams inside government still feel unable to buy the useful thing.

That sounds impossible until you have worked inside any large organization. Money exists at the system level. Permission is scarce at the working level. A person can spend months navigating a process for a small operational need while a much larger line item rolls forward because it already has the correct channel.

the CEO gives a concrete IRS example: eliminating paper processing. He estimates the work around paper processing at roughly half a billion dollars a year, then describes the difficulty of using external vendors to attack it. Treat the number as a source claim that needs audit context. The structural point is more durable: the system can spend heavily on the old path while making the better path hard to buy.

the bureaucratic version of poverty.

A person inside the system says, “We are boxed in.”

The ledger says, “You have plenty of money.”

Both are describing different layers of the same machine.

PaymentAccuracy.gov, the official federal improper-payments portal, exists because the government itself recognizes that payment accuracy has to be tracked across agencies and programs. The site’s framing is boring in the best way: it compiles agency-reported information so taxpayers and oversight bodies can identify risks across programs. The useful move is an audit surface.

Strip away the audit surface and the story collapses into vibes.

With the audit surface, the question changes from “who sounds wasteful?” to “where does the process create money outside the feedback loop?”

Nontechnical leadership is not neutral

One of the colder details in the source is the job-title history.

the CEO describes the government CIO role as descending from an information-management role, almost a librarian function, then evolving into the technical leadership role while the standards lagged behind. His claim is blunt: many government CIOs have historically been nontechnical because the role could be filled without deep technical competence.

This is a systems bug, not a personality insult.

A nontechnical leader can be honest, hard-working, and public-spirited. They can also be unable to smell nonsense in an architecture diagram, a delivery estimate, a vendor promise, or a staffing plan. Technical leadership goes beyond writing code personally. It is about knowing which questions reveal reality.

Where is the source of truth?

Who owns the interface?

What fails if this vendor leaves?

Which metric proves the migration is reducing risk rather than multiplying systems?

Can the engineer who knows the problem talk to the engineer who owns the adjacent system?

The last question matters most. In one modernization sprint, the CEO describes engineers discovering unknown data sources and resolving stuck coordination problems because the people who understood the work could finally talk directly. Before that, he says, a conversation could travel through eight layers of filtering by people outside the technical substance.

institutional packet loss before the packet reaches the system.

The practical test is ownership before ideology

DOGE makes the story louder. The operational frame makes it clearer.

A working institution needs three things before it deserves another round of modernization money:

  • a named artifact that proves the work improved
  • a named owner who can change the system
  • a stop rule that kills the project when the evidence fails

Most reform fights skip straight to ideology. Cut spending. Increase funding. Fire people. Protect workers. Break bureaucracy. Defend institutions.

Those arguments matter, and the operational test still comes first.

A project missing an artifact is theater. A project missing an owner is drift. A project missing a stop rule is a subscription to failure.

This test also protects against fake reform. A politician can promise disruption. A consultant can promise transformation. A vendor can promise modernization. A founder can promise AI. A department can promise efficiency. The words differ. The audit is the same.

Show the artifact.

Name the owner.

Define the stop rule.

If those three things are missing, the system is asking to be trusted by narrative.

The better scandal is less cinematic

The easy version of this story is that government is full of villains.

Sometimes it is.

The harder version is more unsettling: a large system can produce villain-shaped outcomes with ordinary people, compliant steps, stale incentives, and nobody close enough to the artifact to be embarrassed.

The procurement story matters more than the politics. A scam that breaks the law is at least legible. A scam that follows the process is harder to see. It arrives wearing a badge, a category, a threshold, a procurement vehicle, a modernization plan, a steering committee, and a sentence about best practices.

The fix begins beyond speed worship. Fast systems can be stupid faster.

The fix is to shorten the distance between decision and evidence.

Let technical people judge technical claims. Let users expose broken services. Let payment systems publish audit surfaces. Let procurement include the engineer who has to live with the tool. Let old systems survive when they are the right tool. Kill new systems when they become monuments to sunk cost.

A country becomes modern through feedback, not through software purchases alone.

It becomes modern when the feedback loop is shorter than the excuse loop.
