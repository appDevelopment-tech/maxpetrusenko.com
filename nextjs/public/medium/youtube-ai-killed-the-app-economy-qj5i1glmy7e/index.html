600,000 New Apps. You're Fighting for Three Percent of the Money.

AI made building software nearly free. Being noticed stayed expensive. The newest apps are record supply and a rounding error of the money.

The App Store just took its biggest wave of new apps in a decade, and it changed nothing about where the money goes. Nearly 70 cents of every subscription dollar still lands on apps that existed before 2020, while the newest cohort splits about three cents. Building got cheap, attention stayed the bottleneck, and the gap between them is now the entire business.

In the summer of 2008, Apple opened the App Store with about 500 apps, and people downloaded 10 million copies in the first weekend. A developer named Steve Demeter shipped a $5 puzzle game called Trism and made a quarter of a million dollars in two months, then quit his job writing software for a bank. A year later, an engineer built a tank game in his spare time, watched it hit number one, and cleared more than half a million dollars in a month. One small app really could change a life.

Eighteen years later, the store has the opposite problem. Roughly 600,000 new apps arrived last year, the biggest wave in a decade, and the apps launched into that wave are fighting over roughly three percent of the money. Downloads grew 3 percent last year and 2 percent in the first half of 2026, even as supply nearly doubled. App Store submissions are now at their highest level in more than ten years, with new releases up more than 60 percent year over year and doubling again by April.

AI collapsed the cost of building an app, but it did nothing to the cost of getting anyone to care. The money pooled exactly where it already was, behind attention and trust.

The cheapest it has ever been to build

One mobile engineer kept a running ledger of what the tools changed for him. Replacing a $5-a-month fitness app on his own took about 150 hours in early 2024. In mid-2026, he rebuilt two standalone features and refactored 20,000 lines of code in a single evening, with a handful of prompts. His own summary: he could always have done the work. He just never could have justified the weekends.

That collapse is happening to millions of people at once, and most of them have never shipped software before. The barrier that protected a generation of programmers has evaporated. What used to take a team and a budget now takes a weekend and a subscription.

The review queue feels the flood first. App reviews that once resolved in a day or two now stretch from about a week to a month, and Apple has started pushing back on apps whose features change after review. In one corner of Reddit, an original indie app was rejected five times under the spam guideline, the rule meant to catch clones and mass submissions. Originality now has to survive a spam filter trained on the flood.

The money didn't flood with the apps

Here is the strangest part of this moment. People are spending more on apps than ever. Global consumer spending crossed $167 billion last year, up about 10 percent, and for the first time non-game apps out-earned games. Apple's ecosystem billings reached $1.4 trillion, with billings for AI apps more than quadrupling. Usage is up too. People spend 3.6 hours a day in mobile apps and touch more than 30 of them a month.

The new apps barely touch that money. Subscription analytics put it in stark terms: apps released before 2020 collect nearly 70 cents of every subscription dollar, while apps launched in the newest wave split about three cents. Subscription app launches have exploded more than sevenfold since 2023, and nearly all of those launches are converging on the same tiny slice of the pie.

The pattern repeats inside the new cohort itself. Benchmark data making the rounds among app marketers this year shows AI apps holding roughly 6 percent of their monthly subscribers, against 9.5 percent for everything else. Novelty gets the download. Habit is where money lives, and habit takes years to build.

Why does the money stay put? The apps that existed before 2020 aren't winning because their code is better. In plenty of cases it is a mess. They win because they spent a decade accumulating the only things that matter to a stranger handing over a credit card: a name they have seen before, thousands of reviews, and the dull comfort of familiarity. Credibility is the one asset these tools cannot generate in an afternoon.

What actually broke

Look at what the App Store used to do for a small developer between 2008 and 2012. Building was scarce, so being found was nearly free. The shelves were close to empty, the users were starving, and a quirky puzzle game could reach millions without a marketing budget. That combination existed for maybe four years. It has never come back.

AI recreated the excitement of that era without recreating the empty store. It multiplied the number of apps competing for the same eyeballs. One developer's launch log tells the story better than the stats: a working app built in five weeks with AI, then three months of everything else. Those months went to app store prep, compliance reviews, and support systems. The launch that followed brought 141 users and 312 booked sessions, and a first-month retention problem he is still trying to fix. The build was the fast part. The business was the slow part.

The old ladder, where shipping led to features and features led to growth, is gone. One founder's post said it flatly: more apps than ever, less sales than ever. Building became cheap for everyone at the same time, which means the pricing power moved to the only thing still scarce.

Distribution is the new product

The practitioners who saw this coming spent years earning attention the slow way. The folk hero of the indie hacker scene launched 12 startups in 12 months back in 2014, and one of them became a business that would eventually make him millions. The compressed part of the story is the decade he spent building distribution: more than 150,000 posts on X. In his own words, a third of his life went into building the audience before the audience paid for anything. Another developer posted a video about his build journey every single day for more than 400 days while holding a full-time job, and then launched an app about doomscrolling.

The lesson sounds simple and is not. Posting about your product for a year means hundreds of ignored posts, no certainty you are reaching customers, and the discipline to keep experimenting while nothing works. And even that playbook has a hole in it: much of a build-in-public audience turns out to be other founders building their own audiences, who may admire the process without ever needing the product.

There is no universal playbook, which is exactly what makes distribution a moat. In every indie community, the question under every success story is the same: how did you get users? The engineers getting the most out of these tools draw a line between fast, loose vibe coding and disciplined engineering with the agent as a tool, and plenty of veterans are quietly returning to writing code by hand, because taste and ownership turn out to be the part they were selling all along.

The practical rule

If you are building an app in 2026, the order of operations most builders learn is now backwards. Distribution belongs to the product, the same way the backend does, and it starts before launch. The build stopped being the hard part; Choosing who will care, and earning their trust one tedious touch at a time, is the hard part.

That reframes the whole game. You pick a slice of people small enough to recognize you. You ship to them before you ship to the store. You treat the first hundred users as an engineering problem, not a launch problem, and you count retention before you count downloads. And you use the one asset these tools cannot copy: your judgment about what deserves to exist.

Building software has become universal. Finding an audience is still the part that separates a hobby from a business. The gold rush never ended. It moved toward attention, which was never cheap and is not getting cheaper.
