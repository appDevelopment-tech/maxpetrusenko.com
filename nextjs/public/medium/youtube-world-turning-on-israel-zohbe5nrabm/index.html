The World Is Turning on Israel. Watch the Paperwork.

Sixty-six governments withheld a yes when UNESCO voted on Palestine in 2011. By the 2024 General Assembly vote, the count was 34. Since October 2023, the defections have gone procedural: suspended agreements, closed airspace, licenses sliding to zero.

Israel's Western support is draining through specific, dated instruments: a defense memorandum suspended in Rome, an airspace closed in Paris, arms licenses that fell to zero in Berlin and a Senate vote that failed while setting a record. The change is small, procedural, and countable, and the paperwork points one way.

In October 2011, UNESCO's conference voted on admitting Palestine: 107 governments said yes, 14 said no, and 52 abstained (UNESCO vote). The noes and abstentions formed a bloc that looked like the West and the small states that follow it. On May 10, 2024, a related question reached the UN General Assembly as a status upgrade, and the coalition of the unwilling had shrunk to 9 no votes and 25 abstentions (UN coverage).

But a vote is a snapshot, and snapshots overstate. The change that matters since October 2023 has been quieter and more procedural: governments stepping back from the old assumption that supporting Israel costs nothing. There is a defense agreement suspended in Rome, ports shut in Madrid, an arms fair where Israeli firms are no longer welcome, a protest note from Kyiv over stolen grain. Each step is small on its own, and together they are a direction that can be measured.

So the way to read the turn early is the paperwork. National governments move first, and the unions they belong to move last, because unanimity rewards whichever member wants to stand still. The signals to track are procedural: which renewals lapse, which licenses go quiet, which votes get closer. Speeches lag behind the instruments by a year or two.

The turn looks like paperwork

Start with Rome. On April 14, Giorgia Meloni told reporters that Italy would suspend the automatic renewal of its defense agreement with Israel, days after Israeli forces fired warning shots at a UNIFIL convoy that Italy's government said was clearly marked and included an Italian contingent (statement; Meloni's remarks). The memorandum is a narrow instrument, and the practical effect on trade is small. Its weight sits in the precedent, the first hold of its kind by a major Western European government since October 2023 (analysis).

France went further on March 31, refusing to let American aircraft move weapons through French airspace for the Iran campaign, as Reuters reported; within hours Israel's defense ministry said it would halt all procurement from France and honor only existing contracts (The Hill). Paris had shown its direction two years earlier, when Israeli companies were barred from Eurosatory, one of the largest weapons exhibitions in the world (Politico).

Spain had pushed past symbolism well before either, reinforcing its embargo in September 2025 to cover all defense equipment and dual-use technology and barring ships and aircraft carrying military-use cargo from Spanish ports and airspace (government readout). Even Ukraine, fighting its own war, handed Israel's ambassador a note of protest in April over purchases of grain stripped from occupied Ukrainian land (Ukrinform).

Europe is where the arithmetic lives

The steps would matter less if Europe were small, and Europe is Israel's largest market. The European Union took 31.7 percent of Israel's goods trade in 2025, about 43.3 billion euros, while Israel ranked 27th among the bloc's own trading partners (European Commission). On defense, the licensing data is blunter: EU governments authorized 819.3 million euros of arms exports to Israel in 2024, and France signed 367.6 million of that, 44.9 percent of the European total. Germany came second at 163.8 million (Council of the EU data). Berlin's number has since gone to zero: after a partial freeze in August 2025, the government stopped approving new licenses (DW). Licensing counts permission rather than delivery, and Germany's switch can flip back. Still, the two largest European suppliers moved the same way inside a single year.

Brussels has been slower than its members: on April 21, EU foreign ministers rejected a proposal to partially suspend the EU-Israel Association Agreement, the framework that anchors Israel's access to the single market (Guardian). A legal review under the agreement's human-rights clause had found indications of a breach, and the finding produced no measures. Full suspension requires unanimity, and unanimity hands any single friendly government a veto (briefing). The members keep moving while the union waits for all 27 to agree.

The fourth vote

Washington stays the course, and its instruments carry more weight than Europe's. The Senate still funds the arsenal, though on April 15 it voted on two joint resolutions to block specific shipments: Caterpillar bulldozers worth 295 million dollars and one-thousand-pound bombs worth 151.8 million. The first failed 40 to 59, and the second 36 to 63 (roll call; context).

Both lost, and both set the high-water mark for such a vote since October 2023, in the fourth attempt.

Public opinion crossed its own lines earlier, with Pew finding 60 percent of Americans holding an unfavorable view of Israel in April, seven points higher than a year before (Pew). Gallup recorded the first tilt toward the Palestinians, 41 to 36 (Gallup). NBC's March poll put Americans under 35 at 13 percent favorable and 63 percent unfavorable, roughly five to one the other way (NBC). A coalition whose youngest voters lean that hard will keep producing closer votes, because the calendar does the work.

The other friends stopped answering

Israel saw the turn coming and built a hedge. After a suggestion from Washington that the United States was Israel's only dependable ally, Benjamin Netanyahu pushed back: "We have some other friends, like India" (report). Israeli analysts had a name for the approach, the "other friends" policy, a phrase attributed to the analyst Dahlia Scheindlin (background). The bet was that illiberal governments would prove steadier than liberal ones, and it has aged badly on both fronts. Brazil's Jair Bolsonaro, the strategy's loudest champion, was convicted by Brazil's Supreme Court in September 2025 and sentenced to 27 years and three months for plotting a coup (BBC). Hungary's Viktor Orban, the European anchor of the approach, lost his April 2026 election. The incoming prime minister, Peter Magyar, has said Hungary would arrest Netanyahu on the International Criminal Court's warrant and rejoin the court's work (Politico). That warrant remains live, and the court's deputy prosecutor said in September that no steps had been taken to withdraw it (Middle East Eye).

What has not happened

None of this adds up to a rupture, and the counters deserve their place at the table. No EU-wide arms embargo exists, and no Western government has cut relations (counter-view). Israel's defense exports reached a record 19.2 billion dollars in 2025, up about 30 percent, while Europe's share of its sales fell from 54 percent to 36 (Globes). The US House armed services committee has advanced a provision that would push American and Israeli defense industries into deeper integration (Al Jazeera). Israel's defense establishment spent years preparing for a version of this moment, building domestic substitutes and treating foreign restrictions as a cost to absorb (defense trade press). Analysts at RAND call the isolation real and deepening, and warn that its cushions are dissipating, which leaves much of the outcome resting on choices Washington and Jerusalem still control (RAND). The sharpest counter-argument, that the break has not come, remains standing (counter-view).

All of this is unfolding during an active war: the conflict that opened on February 28 with American and Israeli strikes on Iran is in its seventh month, the Strait of Hormuz is effectively closed, with roughly one transit a day against a peacetime 85, and oil trades near 98 dollars (shipping data; gCaptain). Wars in this region have a history of consolidating support around the strongest military power in the room. This one keeps producing exits while the shooting continues, and exits get written into agreements.

The direction of travel

There is a temptation to wait for the rupture, for the moment a coalition visibly changes sides. The record of the past three years suggests the rupture has a different shape: a memorandum left to lapse, a license count sliding to zero, a vote getting closer. No single one of those events leads a newscast. The series is the evidence.

In The Sun Also Rises, a character is asked how he went bankrupt. "Two ways," he says. "Gradually, then suddenly." The gradual part is measurable now, in dates and figures that did not exist a year ago. The sudden part, if it comes, will be reported as a single event. The evidence for it has been accumulating for three years, in a paper trail anyone can read. Watch the instruments. They are already moving.
