The CIA Didn't Create Pokémon Go. It Funded the Map.

An intelligence-funded check from 2003, a game that collected street footage, and where it all ended up. The paper trail is public.

Pokémon Go was not created by the CIA, and the documented story does not need the claim to be true. Intelligence-community money funded the mapping company whose software became Google Earth, a game built by that company's founder collected opt-in scans from its players, and the models trained on those scans now sit inside a business selling navigation to defense customers.

The check

In February 2003, a mapping startup called Keyhole took an investment from a venture fund called In-Q-Tel. Its software rendered the planet as a 3D globe you could spin with a mouse, but the money behind the fund came from the Central Intelligence Agency.

A release dated June 25, 2003 described In-Q-Tel as "a private nonprofit venture funded by the Central Intelligence Agency," and announced a strategic investment in Keyhole made that February. The release called the deal In-Q-Tel's first engagement on behalf of the National Imagery and Mapping Agency, and it quotes a NIMA director describing the technology reaching the Pentagon within two weeks of the investment, after the agency used it to support United States troops in Iraq.

That check is the first link in a chain of twenty-two years. But the chain ran in the open, and the end of it is startling.

What the money bought

Start with what In-Q-Tel actually is, because the viral version inflates it into something it is not. In-Q-Tel is a nonprofit venture fund that exists to find commercial technology the intelligence community wants to exist and buy it into existence. In 2003, that meant a nonprofit "funded by the Central Intelligence Agency." Its description today is more careful: an independent nonprofit, separate from the government, with government partners rather than government ownership. The important verb, then as now, is buy. The intelligence community wrote checks for products it wanted, and one of the products was a better globe.

No cited document ties In-Q-Tel to the game itself. In-Q-Tel's own archive shows the Keyhole investment; no cited public evidence shows an investment in Niantic. The two are linked by lineage, and the line runs through an acquisition, a spinout, and a founder who stayed. The literal claim, that the CIA created Pokémon Go, is false as written. The pattern underneath it is real, and every link of it is documented.

The map gets a market

Keyhole's usefulness did not stay unusual for long, because its usefulness to everyone else arrived fast. Google bought the company in 2004, folded its globe into what became Google Earth, and put the founder, John Hanke, in charge of its geospatial products. Imagery that had supported a war became a tool for finding hotels, checking on hometowns, and watching hurricanes. Intelligence money had helped pay for the early map. After 2004, the map paid for itself.

Inside Google, the next generation arrived in 2010 as a startup-within-a-company called Niantic Labs. First came Field Trip, a guide that surfaced local history; then Ingress, launched in 2012, which sent players hunting for portals at real landmarks. The design lesson was legible early: give people a reason to walk somewhere and they will go anywhere, phone up, tapping. By 2016 the machinery was ready, with a world-class map, a studio that knew how to move crowds through streets, and a proven hunger for games that happen outdoors.

When the world played

Pokémon Go launched on July 6, 2016, built under a partnership with Nintendo and The Pokémon Company. The scale was immediate. The company reported more than 500 million downloads within about two months, and an early estimate put U.S. daily players at just under 21 million, more than Twitter's daily users, with more time spent in the app than in Facebook. Millions of people walked around with cameras pointed at their neighborhoods, getting rewarded for wherever the game sent them.

The mess scaled with the crowds. Players walked into traffic, trespassed after rare spawns, and showed up in such numbers at the U.S. Holocaust Memorial Museum and Arlington National Cemetery that both sites asked visitors to stop playing. A game had spilled into the physical world, and the physical world had opinions.

Then came the data scare. In July 2016, iPhone users discovered that signing into the game through Google appeared to hand it full access to their entire Google account, and the reaction was immediate and enormous, because the worst reading was the obvious one. Google's investigation found that the permission request itself had asked for too much, that the account access actually used was limited to a user ID and email address, and the companies narrowed the scope within days. The scare was wrong in its details and right in its instinct. Millions of people had proven they would tap through a permission screen faster than they could read it, and the next ask would be bigger.

The ask

In 2020, the game started asking players to do the scanning themselves. AR mapping tasks offered in-game rewards to players who visited certain PokéStops and filmed the area around them for about twenty to thirty seconds. The footage flowed back to Niantic as training data, which the company said would improve its understanding of the physical world. The tasks were open to players above a certain level, unavailable to child accounts at launch, and described by outside researchers as a dangerous precedent while they were still new.

The company's position, then and later, was that scanning was completely optional, which is true and also the point. The famous version of this story suspects a secret directive. The documented version shows a feature that paid people in low-cost virtual rewards to film the real world, at scale. And the footage had a destination. It fed a visual positioning system that lets a device figure out where it is by matching its camera against a stored model of a place, and then a "large geospatial model" announced in late 2024 that can make educated guesses about locations it has only partially seen, inferring the back of one church from the fronts of thousands of others.

By the time the model was announced in 2024, Niantic had spent years collecting training data from players who chose to scan public places for in-game rewards. The company's framing was straightforward: a feature, optional, rewarding. The part it could not frame away was what the scans were for. Privacy advocates kept making the same point in different words: consent collected for a game is thin consent for whatever comes next.

The defense market

In 2025, the company split in two, and the split shows which half mattered. Scopely bought the games business for $3.5 billion; the geospatial technology, the models, and the maps stayed behind in a spinout, Niantic Spatial, funded with $250 million of capital: $200 million from Niantic's own balance sheet and $50 million from Scopely. The games had supplied the training data. The new company owned the models built on it.

What it sells is geospatial AI for defense and enterprise customers, and in December 2025 it announced a partnership with Vantor, an Earth-intelligence firm formerly known as Maxar Intelligence. The stated goal is navigation that works where GPS does not: a drone looks down, matches the ground against a map, and knows where it is without satellites. Niantic Spatial brought the ground-level model; Vantor brought the market for it. The two firms say they aim to link air and ground platforms using the same visual positioning technology.

The federal path is public. By spring 2026, the visual positioning system was listed as "awardable" through Tradewinds Solutions Marketplace, a federal marketplace that assessed it through competitive procedures and, in the company's words, allows agencies to "directly engage, negotiate, and procure" the technology, and the company's site documents separate simulator training work for the United States Coast Guard. Reporting on the defense turn adds a hard number: Vantor holds a United States Army agreement worth up to $217 million. The agreement is Vantor's; no cited public evidence ties it to the models built from game scans. Awardable status is a pathway rather than a contract, and a case study is marketing. Both are still exactly how pipelines like this one move.

Then comes the line the companies draw, and it is worth stating precisely. Both Niantic and Vantor told reporters the game's scans were never handed to Vantor; the data trained Niantic's internal models, and the models are what the business sells. The distance between a model and the files that trained it is the exact boundary this story turns on, and no cited public evidence shows an outside audit of where it holds. The reporting's careful verbs reflect that gap. What is documented is a navigation system being built for defense customers, described as something that could assist drones in war zones. The strongest claim the record supports is the conditional one.

What the record shows

The loud claim fails on its own details. In-Q-Tel's investment went to a mapping company years before the game existed, its own current description places it outside government, and no record shows it ever held a stake in the game studio. An irony runs through the record: for years, security agencies worried aloud that these games were collection risks aimed at them. The spies spent the era as the nervous party, which is not how the viral version casts them.

What survives is the sequence, and every step in it was announced. In 2003, intelligence-community money invested in a mapping company. In 2004, Google bought that company and gave the world its map. In 2016, the founder's game put a camera and a location in millions of hands. In 2020, the game paid players to point those cameras at landmarks. By 2025, the models built from that footage belonged to a company courting defense customers, and by 2026 they were awardable to federal agencies.

Nobody had to lie, and none of it was secret. The steps were public years before their consequences arrived, and the price of seeing it coming was attention, paid to documents that almost everyone skipped.

The rule

Read the opt-in as a contract. When an app asks to see through your camera, to know where you are, or to have you film a place for a small reward, the real terms are longer than the moment of the tap. The tap is a signature; the terms land later, priced by someone you will never meet.

The players who scanned a PokéStop for thirty seconds did nothing wrong, and nothing in the record shows the company broke a law. The accounting simply worked out, one tap at a time, until a game had a map and the map had a market.

Free features have supply chains. The map was always a product. What the game added was a workforce: players who filmed the real world in exchange for game rewards and a genuinely good time. The bill arrives years later, addressed to whoever is holding the phone.
