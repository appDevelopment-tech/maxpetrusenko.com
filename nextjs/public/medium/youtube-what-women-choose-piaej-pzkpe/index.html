She Doesn't Need Your Money Anymore. She's Grading Your Calendar.

Sixty years of mate-preference data and a 2,477-person study show the repricing up close: money lost its monopoly on the decision, and the hours you can't fake are what replaced it.

Six decades of data on mate preferences tell one story in two halves. As women's economic power rose, what both sexes said they wanted converged. The competition kept running, and it moved to quieter instruments: display aimed at rivals, the mirror, and the calendar. The signal gaining the most value now is the hardest one to buy, and the repricing is not finished.

The budget is where the difference shows

In 2002, a team of psychologists handed people a budget and asked them to build a partner. Every desirable trait carried a price, and the instruction was simple: spend the money on what matters most. At a comfortable budget, everyone ordered the same package, warmth and humor and looks and earning power, a catalog with nothing crossed off. Then the researchers shrank the budget until choosing got painful.

The squeeze reordered the lists, with men protecting physical attractiveness and women protecting earning prospects and status. That 2002 study turned mate choice into a laboratory of trade-offs, and a cross-cultural follow-up with 2,477 people in the East and the West tested the same priority model against the same budget logic (Journal of Personality). The finding underneath both runs is stubborn: under scarcity, the sexes protect different assets, and the pattern tracks what each side expects to run short of.

What makes this more than an academic argument is that the scarcity underneath it has been changing for sixty years. Women's earnings and options multiplied, marriage shifted from economic necessity toward choice for most of them, and the share of women who depend on a man's income to get by fell sharply. If priorities really track shortage, the largest natural experiment in the history of mating has been running in plain sight the whole time.

The wish lists converge

The clearest window into that experiment is stated preference. In six surveys from 1939 to 1996, researchers asked men and women to rank the qualities they wanted in a partner, and the 1996 wave produced the most similar rankings the series ever recorded, converging toward a shared ideal. Both sexes raised the importance they attached to a partner's financial prospects, and men moved especially far. The one quality men traditionally prized most in a wife, domestic skill, fell down the list hard. Mutual attraction and love climbed for everyone (Buss, 2001).

Survey answers are soft material, and the most useful recent work treats them like prices. A preregistered 2025 experiment placed 602 people from five countries inside 45 simulated economies, then varied how rich each person was and how unequal their world was. Resource preferences moved with the money: the poorer a person was, and the poorer their sex ranked in that economy, the more they prioritized a partner's resources, across all three measures the study used. Physical attractiveness preferences barely budged, which is a quiet bombshell of its own, because it suggests the resource calculation is the one wired to circumstance.

The convergence story has serious critics, and the data gives them room. A 45-country replication with 14,399 participants found the classic sex differences holding on average and staying modest, with huge overlap between the two groups, and it found that gender equality itself did not reliably shrink the gaps across countries. A meta-synthesis of 106 meta-analyses put the average absolute difference between men and women at roughly two-tenths of a standard deviation. And a speed-dating study of 1,145 people found that what people say they want and who they actually chase often barely correspond, which caps how much any preference survey can prove. The critiques show the convergence was uneven. They do not establish the competition stood still.

That cap is the reason to stop reading statements and start reading spending. If the surveys can only tell us so much, the market's real ledger lives in what people actually buy with their money, their hours, and their skin. The spending tells a cleaner story than the discourse around it.

The loud signal kept working

Money kept mattering while women earned their own; its job changed. A set of five 2014 experiments found women's luxury purchases functioning as a signaling system aimed at other women: activate a mate-guarding motive and women reach for the expensive bag, and those displays are effective at deterring romantic rivals, which makes the handbag a defensive instrument as much as a decorative one. Men's expensive tastes, studied years earlier, ran the other direction: courtship motives made men flash conspicuous goods with no practical use, advertising status directly.

The loudest evidence that beauty remains a live currency is the clock. A survey of 93,158 people across 93 countries found women averaging almost four hours a day on their appearance and men about three and a half, a gap of roughly twenty minutes that sounds small until you multiply it across a lifetime. The domains split by sex, cosmetics against exercise. For explaining the minutes, a single predictor beat income, religion, and national wealth: social media use. The practice is not a Western idiosyncrasy. The most appearance-focused countries in the survey, Tunisia, Thailand and Ghana among them, had already made room in the day for it.

The mirror joined the market

Men's side of the ledger shows the same migration. Market researchers put American men's grooming at about $7.1 billion a year and growing faster than the broader category, with online sales climbing at multiples of that rate. On top of that sits looksmaxxing, an ecosystem of apps, tutorials and rating scores covered in reporting on the trend. It teaches teenage boys to treat a jawline the way earlier generations treated a resume.

The quiet instrument pays better

Then there is the signal that compounds slowly, time with children. American fathers have tripled their child-care hours since the mid-1960s, from about two and a half hours a week to nearly eight, one of the fastest shifts in men's domestic behavior ever recorded (Pew Research Center). The scale still deserves a second look: even after the tripling, an eight-hour father logs fewer hours than the average mother logged in 1965, and she was doing it inside an economy that offered her few alternatives.

The tripling also hides the split that matters most. Fathers with college degrees now spend around ten hours a week on child care, while fathers without one spend under six, down slightly since the early 2000s even as the overall average kept climbing (Institute for Family Studies). That gradient is one of the clearest class signals in American family life, and it keeps widening.

There is a mechanism behind the father turn, and it shows up in the data. Across four samples of Chinese women, priorities shifted toward men who display parenting qualities as income and development rose, with good-father traits gaining ground on good genes and good providers (Evolutionary Behavioral Sciences, 2015). A small priming experiment moved the same needle. That preference only pays if a man's hours are real, and hours are visible long before commitment is. A schedule is a signal you cannot fake in a photograph.

What the market charges now

Put the pieces together and the market looks less like a war between the sexes and more like a repricing. When one sex held most of the money and the other needed it, resources were the rational screen and beauty was the liquid alternative. As women's earnings and options multiplied, resources stopped being the default screen. Competition moved toward traits that are slower to fake: a body kept in condition, a schedule with room in it, hands that show up for the boring shifts.

The practical rule underneath all of it is to watch what a person spends their scarcest resource on. Where someone controls their own budget, that spending is the preference, whatever the survey says. A man who clears Saturday for the park is spending billable hours. A woman choosing which bag walks into which room is spending on an audience. Neither move requires anyone to have read a study, and both leave receipts.

The harder part is distributional. Time and patience and bandwidth are the traits gaining value, the things stable money buys in bulk: coaching, childcare, a functioning week. So the repricing hands its biggest advantage to households that already had one. The paternal-care gradient shows the tilt: the biggest moves come from fathers with degrees, and the smallest from those without. The shift is real, and it is arriving unevenly.

The direction of the change is not a story anyone planned, and it does not resolve into a winner. Care was the unpaid skill this market spent a century underpricing, mostly in women's accounts, and the repricing now values it in hours that are visible and scheduled and hard to counterfeit. A market that once measured men by their wallets and women by their waists is learning to count something both sexes have to deliver in person.
