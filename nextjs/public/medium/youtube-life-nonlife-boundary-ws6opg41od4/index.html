The Boundary Between Life and Matter Is Breaking

A Russian science story starts with impossible organisms and ends with a harder rule: life is a process stack, and some of its pieces now run outside cells. (Нереальная граница между ЖИВОЙ и НЕ ЖИВОЙ материей | Безумные научные идеи) (Mirror-image cells could transform science or kill us all)

Direct answer: The boundary between living and non-living matter is breaking because the traits we use to recognize life, metabolism, movement, replication, evolution, self-repair, and autonomy, now appear separately in systems outside the comfortable category of life. The useful question is becoming less "is it alive?" and more "which life process is running, and what can it become?"

A cell is the compromise we inherited.

For most of everyday life, the border is easy. A dog is alive. A stone sits outside the category. Mold grows. Plastic persists. A dead leaf once belonged to biology. A phone talks with eerie confidence while remaining machinery. The old map works because ordinary life stays polite enough to fit our categories.

The research frontier is impolite.

It keeps producing things that carry one piece of life while leaving the full costume behind. A drop can chase another drop. A molecule can copy its shape into descendants. A naked ring of RNA can colonize human microbiomes while refusing the usual virus categories. A robot can maintain itself by replacing broken parts. An AI system can rewrite its own code and select better descendants. A mirror bacterium, if built, might be made of the same chemistry as life and still fall outside the ecological police system that keeps normal organisms in check.

That is the colder lesson from the SciOne source that sparked this piece: the living/non-living line behaves less like a wall. It is a bundle of processes that evolution happened to tie together in Earth biology. Synthetic biology is now learning how to pull the threads apart.

The first warning sign is scale

Life already cheats our intuition before the lab gets involved.

Placozoans look almost offensively simple. They lack organs, mouth, gut, and a familiar nervous system. They still move, react, digest externally, repair damage, and coordinate different cell types. They are animals because their genome and body organization put them there, even though the image feels wrong.

Scale plays the same trick. The seagrass Posidonia australis in Shark Bay, Western Australia, turned out to be a massive clonal organism spread across an enormous area, identified by genetic markers rather than by what looks like one body to the eye. The Proceedings of the Royal Society B study turned a meadow into a single organism problem.

Fungal networks make the border even stranger. Mycorrhizal fungi thread through soil and plant roots, moving nutrients, water, and carbon through underground infrastructure. A recent Science paper on mycorrhizal networks maps this hidden system as planetary machinery, as infrastructure rather than decorative dirt biology.

A forest can exceed a crowd of trees. A seagrass meadow can be one genetic body. A microbial mat can act like a surface. A single cell can build a structure that looks like a tiny architecture project. The eye keeps asking for a bounded object. Biology keeps answering with systems.

The practical rule begins here: start somewhere deeper than shape. Start with the process.

Hunting can happen before the hunter

Movement is one of the oldest traps in this subject.

A thing moves toward something. It reacts. It follows. It captures. The nervous system in us wants to assign desire, hunger, intention, and strategy.

Chemistry runs before those words arrive.

In the source sequence, oil droplets chase one another in a predator-prey pattern. The underlying paper, "Predator-prey interactions between droplets driven by non-reciprocal oil exchange", describes non-living droplets whose motion arises from nonequilibrium chemistry. They look like they are hunting. They are running gradients.

The uncomfortable part is the comparison with cells. A macrophage chasing bacteria also looks intentional from the outside. It is more complex, more regulated, and biologically meaningful. Still, the behavior is built from physical and chemical processes. Pseudopods extend. Membranes reorganize. Receptors bind. Molecules meet because shapes, charges, flows, and concentrations make some futures more likely than others.

The droplet remains chemistry. The lazy shortcut dies. Motion alone carries too little. Pursuit carries too little. Swallowing carries too little. A behavior that feels alive to the observer can be produced by matter before the matter becomes an organism.

That matters because the path from chemistry to life may be less like flipping a switch and more like adding constraints until matter starts maintaining a pattern.

A definition is a tool with a job

NASA's working astrobiology definition calls life "a self-sustaining chemical system capable of Darwinian evolution." The NASA life detection page also makes the deeper problem plain: every definition is based on the one life we already know, Terran life.

That is a dangerous sample size.

Earth life uses cells, carbon chemistry, inherited molecular information, thermodynamic disequilibrium, and water. Those features are the winner's architecture. A winner is still only one explored corner of the design space.

Obelisks show why the definition keeps slipping. Researchers described Obelisks as a previously unrecognized class of viroid-like RNA elements first identified in human gut metatranscriptomic data. The preprint version says they have apparently circular RNA genomes, rod-like secondary structures, and open reading frames for novel proteins called Oblins; the later Cell work places them among strange RNA colonists of microbiomes. The PMC-hosted preprint reports Obelisks in human stool and oral datasets, plus wider ecological searches.

Obelisks lack cells, the normal viral costume, and the familiar shell. They seem to depend on hosts. Their life cycle remains unclear. Yet they vary, persist, encode something, and inhabit microbial worlds inside us.

A classroom definition wants to sort them.

Reality shrugs.

Digital evolution has escaped metaphor

The open-evolution definition cuts even harder. If life is an autonomous system capable of open-ended evolution, cells stop being mandatory. Carbon stops being mandatory. The category can reach toward machines, code, and systems that maintain themselves across generations.

Early evolutionary simulations already made virtual bodies that learned to swim, walk, jump, or follow light. The catch was the plateau. Once the target was solved, the pressure ran out. That is optimization before open-ended life.

Newer work pushes closer to the edge. Modular robots can replace failed parts by absorbing other modules. A recent Science Robotics / Science Advances line of work on robotic self-maintenance is less interesting as a gadget than as a category violation: a machine starts to look less like a tool and more like a body with repair logic.

AI adds another route. The "Gödel Darwin machine" line of work describes systems that modify their own source code, test descendants on tasks, and archive improved versions. The 2025 arXiv paper frames this as open-ended self-improvement machinery.

Your laptop stays a laptop. The category shift is narrower and more useful: life-like process can be instantiated outside flesh. Autonomy, energy access, variation, inheritance, selection, repair, and open-ended novelty can migrate into substrates that make biologists uncomfortable and engineers excited.

A better question than "is AI alive?" is narrower: which life-process has been recreated, which process is missing, and who controls the stop rule?

The lab is learning to start below the cell

Synthetic biology is moving beyond edits to existing life. It is learning how far below the cell the life-process can begin.

One route starts with self-replicating molecules. In the SciOne source materials, the key thread is a set of peptide-based molecules that assembled into rings and fibers. When fibers broke, the pieces continued to grow. Shape became inheritance. Later work added competition between different replicators, where shared building blocks created exclusion and different building blocks allowed coexistence, a primitive echo of ecological niches.

This is why the MiniLife idea matters. The University of Groningen describes the ambition as asking whether life can be built from entirely different building blocks, rather than merely replaying the ancient Earth script. If a chemical system can replicate, maintain a boundary, consume resources, change under selection, and generate descendants, the refusal to call it life becomes less scientific than emotional.

The strongest line here is engineering discipline: if the classical checklist can be assembled from non-biological materials, the checklist stops belonging exclusively to biology.

That would make life more general.

Mirror life is the safety test

The most useful test of a definition is whether it changes what you do.

Mirror life changes what you do.

Every known organism on Earth uses left-handed amino acids in proteins and right-handed sugars in DNA and RNA. Molecules have handedness, like gloves. The atoms can be the same while the three-dimensional shape refuses to overlay with its mirror image.

For medicine, that asymmetry is powerful. Mirror fragments of nucleic acids can persist where ordinary molecules are degraded, and an expanded genetic alphabet can let engineered organisms store and retrieve more information than natural DNA normally carries. The Nature paper on a semi-synthetic organism is one example of biology's alphabet becoming an engineering surface.

For ecology, mirror life is frightening.

A group of scientists recently warned in Science that mirror bacteria and related organisms should stay uncreated until convincing evidence shows mirror life is safe from extreme danger. The Science warning was backed by a much longer technical report. The concern is brutally simple: a mirror organism might evade ordinary immune recognition, ordinary enzymes, ordinary viruses, ordinary predators, and ordinary ecological controls.

Normal life is dangerous enough, but it is embedded in enemies. Bacteria have phages. Prey have predators. Dead matter has decomposers. A mirror organism might enter the world with fewer natural accountants.

That is the article's real stake.

The boundary between living and non-living matter has outgrown philosophy alone. It is a governance problem. Once we can build systems that copy, evolve, repair, evade, and persist, the naming debate becomes too slow. The process matters before the label settles.

The useful rule is to score the life-process

The binary question is attractive because it feels clean.

Alive or outside life.

Safe or dangerous.

Natural or artificial.

Tool or organism.

The frontier keeps making those words leak.

A stronger rule is to score the process:

  • Boundary: does the system separate itself from the environment?
  • Metabolism: does it maintain itself by transforming matter or energy?
  • Inheritance: does structure or information pass into descendants?
  • Variation: can descendants differ in ways that matter?
  • Selection: do some variants persist better than others?
  • Autonomy: does the system keep operating beyond direct external choreography?
  • Open evolution: can novelty keep expanding beyond the original task?
  • Ecological accountability: what limits it, eats it, breaks it, or absorbs it?

That last question is the one most definitions forget.

A thing can be fascinating in the lab and still be a bad citizen of the biosphere. A thing can be technically non-living and still run a dangerous life-process. A thing can be alive by one definition and inert by another. The label is less important than the trajectory.

The old comfort was that life belonged to organisms.

The new reality is stranger: organisms are one successful packaging format for processes that may be more general than biology.

Cells won here. They built us. They built trees, fungi, animals, bacteria, parasites, flowers, cancers, brains, and every hand that now reaches into the lab to make new versions of the game.

Cells may be one way matter learns to continue.

The boundary was a scorecard waiting to be written properly.
