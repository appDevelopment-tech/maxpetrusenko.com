How Putin’s War Pay Is Creating New Wealth in Russia

In Buryatia, battlefield bonuses are rewriting the local economy

Russia’s war economy is moving money into some of its poorest regions through enlistment bonuses, salaries, and death compensation. In Buryatia, that cash is buying homes, cars, and small businesses while lifting prices, dividing families, and creating a powerful local dependence on continued fighting. The money is real. So is the bill that follows.

In Ulan-Ude, the clearest sign of Russia’s war economy may be a new coffee shop rather than a tank. The city has welcomed Stars Coffee, Lenta, and its first Burger King as military money reaches veterans and families of soldiers killed in Ukraine. At the same time, the city’s cemetery has expanded, visible in satellite imagery.

The mechanism is straightforward: Moscow and regional governments transfer war compensation into households, those households spend it locally, and businesses raise prices to meet the new demand. The result is a narrow wartime boom funded by recruitment and casualties. It redistributes income toward military households, raises costs for everyone else, and makes an end to the war economically dangerous for places that have come to depend on the flow.

That pattern matters beyond Buryatia. Russia’s federal budget has shifted heavily toward defense, and the Stockholm International Peace Research Institute documents the broader global rise in military spending. Russia’s own budget documents show the same priority: military and security spending reached an extraordinary share of planned expenditure. The war is being financed as an industrial program, but also as a labor market.

The price of a recruit

Russia’s recruitment system has three cash channels. A new volunteer can receive a regional signing bonus, a monthly military salary, and what Russians often call “coffin money”: compensation paid to relatives after a service member’s death. The video transcript reports bonuses above three million rubles in some regions, monthly payments of at least 200,000 rubles, and death compensation near 14 million rubles.

Those figures vary by region, contract, exchange rate, and eligibility. The Russian government’s recruitment portal describes contract service and benefits, while Radio Free Europe/Radio Liberty has tracked the escalation of regional bonuses. The Moscow Times has reported regional payments reaching millions of rubles. The important fact is the direction: regions have competed by making enlistment an unusually large financial decision.

Three examples show how the mechanism works.

1. Buryatia’s signing bonus. According to economist Janis Kluge, cited in the source reporting, the regional payment rose from 200,000 rubles in early 2024 to 500,000, then one million, and later roughly 1.5 to 1.6 million rubles. That is a large one-time transfer into households in a comparatively poor region. Kluge’s analysis of Russia’s war economy places these payments in the wider fiscal system.

2. The monthly wage. A payment above 200,000 rubles a month can exceed ordinary local earnings by several multiples. Russia’s official statistics agency reports regional differences in wages and household income; those differences help explain why a military contract can look attractive even when the risks are understood.

3. Death compensation. A family payment near 14 million rubles can resemble an inheritance or lottery win in a low-income region. BBC reporting on Russian military recruitment has described the financial incentives and their effect on enlistment. Meduza’s reporting on compensation disputes shows how eligibility and family relationships can turn the payment into a source of conflict.

The money does not arrive in an abstract macroeconomic model. It arrives as a down payment, a used car, a shop lease, or a family argument. That is why the same transfer can feel like opportunity to one household and inflation to its neighbor.

Why poorer regions feel the shock first

Buryatia has long been poorer than Russia’s largest metropolitan centers. It has also supplied a disproportionate share of recruits and casualties. Mediazona and BBC’s open-source casualty project has counted Russian military deaths by region, while [People of Baikal](https://baikal people.example.org) has reported heavily on losses in Buryatia. The exact total changes as names are confirmed, but the regional pattern is clear enough to shape public life.

The redistribution has two sides. Military households receive cash, while labor leaves the civilian economy. Fighting-age men depart for the front or leave Russia to avoid mobilization. That can tighten labor markets, reduce household production, and increase the bargaining power of workers who remain. It can also make local businesses more dependent on customers whose income comes from the state.

Research on Russia’s wartime economy reaches a similar conclusion. The Bank of Finland Institute for Emerging Economies has tracked defense-driven growth and labor shortages. The Bank of Russia’s regional economy reports describe demand pressure, constrained supply, and rising costs. The International Monetary Fund’s Russia data provides a useful outside baseline for growth and inflation. These sources do not prove that every price increase comes from military compensation. They establish the broader pressure in which local war cash operates.

Real estate is a particularly visible channel. A household with a large bonus can bid for an apartment or renovate a home. Landlords and sellers see the new purchasing power and adjust prices. People without military income face the same market with fewer resources. The source video’s residents describe a city where the cost of living rises faster than the benefits available to civilians.

Consumption, status, and the politics of payout

New retailers are symbols of this transition. Stars Coffee, the Russian brand that replaced Starbucks, and Lenta are ordinary commercial businesses. Their arrival does not prove that a city is prosperous in a durable sense. It shows that companies see enough concentrated spending to justify expansion.

Veterans also turn compensation into small enterprises. The source describes a wounded veteran building a farm, another veteran opening a nail salon, and a third opening a pastry shop. These are real forms of reinvestment. They can create jobs and services. They can also conceal how dependent the local multiplier is on continued military transfers.

Death payments carry a different political function. A widow or child may suddenly control more money than neighboring households could accumulate in decades. The Carnegie Endowment has examined how authoritarian systems use social spending and patronage, while Chatham House has analyzed the domestic pressures created by Russia’s war. In Buryatia, the payout can reduce anger by making the state the source of a family’s new security. It can also create jealousy, disputes over who counts as a relative, and a public reminder that the money is attached to death.

That combination gives the payment unusual power. It compensates a household while normalizing the system that produced the loss. The United Nations has documented the human and economic costs of the invasion, but the local political economy is easier to miss: compensation can make war simultaneously more destructive and more institutionally entrenched.

The dependence problem

A wartime boom can continue while the state keeps injecting money. Russia’s defense factories receive contracts, soldiers receive wages, and regions compete for recruits. The European Bank for Reconstruction and Development’s transition reports offer a comparative framework for understanding investment and productivity shocks. The OECD’s economic outlook explains why demand supported by government spending can coexist with supply constraints and inflation.

The exit is harder. If the war ends and recruitment bonuses stop, households lose an exceptional income stream. If veterans return in large numbers, local labor markets receive workers with injuries and uncertain civilian prospects. If compensation falls while apartment prices remain elevated, the earlier boom leaves debt and unaffordable housing behind. The World Bank’s work on conflict and development describes this familiar post-conflict problem: transfers can sustain consumption during war without building a diversified economy for peace.

Ulan-Ude therefore offers a compact view of a much larger bargain. Russia can buy recruitment with money, and the money can create visible prosperity in places that were previously left behind. Yet every new shop, apartment purchase, and family payout also records the cost of keeping the system running.

The war economy has created wealth in Russia, but it has concentrated that wealth around military service and made parts of the civilian economy dependent on the war continuing.
