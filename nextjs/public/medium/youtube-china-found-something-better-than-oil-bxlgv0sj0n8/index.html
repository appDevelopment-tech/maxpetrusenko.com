The F-35 Needs Magnets Beijing Can Refuse to Ship

Rare earths were never rare. The scarce part was the processing, and thirty years of quiet handovers put a licensing office between America's fighter jets and the magnets they need.

The scarce thing was never the rock. Rare earths are abundant and America runs a working mine, yet the West quit the unglamorous middle of the chain, the near-identical chemistry of separating lookalike elements. In April 2025, that abandoned middle became a licensing office, and the motors, turbines, and missile programs downstream began paying for thirty years of outsourcing.

In 1995, General Motors signed away the plant that made the strongest permanent magnets on earth. The Magnequench factory sat in Anderson, Indiana, where it turned rare-earth alloys into magnets small enough for a hard drive and strong enough to steer a cruise missile. The buyers formed a mixed consortium of American financiers and two Chinese firms, one of them owned by the Chinese Academy of Sciences (NBC News, Heritage Foundation).

At the time, it looked like housekeeping. The business was small, the margins were thin, and American industry had other priorities. Within a decade, the equipment and processes were running in China, and the plants around Valparaiso had gone quiet. A history of the sale describes the dismantling as the last act of a longer handover that almost nobody watched (CounterPunch, The China Project). The sale moved the magnet end of the chain. The separation end, the chemistry that feeds it, unraveled on its own slower timeline.

But the machines were the small part of what changed hands. Rare earths are not rare, and the magnets were never the scarce thing either. What was scarce, and what the West was quietly walking away from, was the unglamorous middle of the chain: the chemistry that separates seventeen lookalike elements, and the willingness to handle what that separation leaves behind. Thirty years later, that middle became a lever on the entire electric economy.

The magnets kept mattering the whole time. They went into speakers and disk drives, then into hybrid drivetrains, wind turbines, missile fins, and MRI scanners. A plant sale in a small Indiana city did not look like the opening of a strategic problem, because strategic problems look like paperwork.

The rocks are everywhere

The category name is misleading. Rare earths are not rare, and the United States Geological Survey groups them as a relatively abundant set of seventeen elements, more common in the crust than plenty of metals nobody worries about (USGS). They sit beside each other on the periodic table with nearly identical chemistry. Separating them is less like filtering and more like untangling two languages that share an alphabet (Chemistry Stack Exchange). The digging was the easy stage.

China solved the hard part first, and kept solving it. It now mines roughly two-thirds of the world's rare earths, separates about 90 percent of them, and manufactures about 94 percent of the permanent magnets that come out the far end (CSIS, Benchmark Mineral Intelligence, CRS). The three numbers are not the same number, and the gaps between them are where the power sits. A country with a mine but no separation plant owns a pile of dirt with an impressive label. A congressional record put the practical version bluntly: even if the United States sourced every raw material domestically, it would still depend on China for refining (House Energy and Commerce).

The middle nobody wanted to run

The middle of the chain is unpleasant work. It is low-margin, slow to repay, and attached to a waste problem. Private markets walked away long before anyone called it strategic.

Separating rare earths at scale produces waste that towns notice. One engineering estimate of the standard acid route puts the waste at roughly 75 cubic meters of acidic wastewater, a tonne of thorium-bearing residue, and thousands of cubic meters of waste gas for every tonne of separated oxide (r/mining discussion). Exact figures vary by process, and the shape does not: the chemistry comes attached to a disposal problem. When Western plants closed, the environmental cost did not vanish; it relocated. The same discussion makes the sharper point, that the pollution was offshored through Western choices as much as it was pushed from Beijing.

The same waste problem runs at a different scale on the Chinese side. The Baogang tailings dam near Baotou, where waste from the refining process collects, has grown since the late 1950s to the point where it has been dubbed the "world's dystopian lake," and the cancer mortality rate in the Bayan Obo mining district rose 50 percent from 1990 to 1997 (The China Project).

Economics finished what the paperwork started. Quotas, a boom, a bust, and a wave of consolidation turned China's rare-earth sector from several hundred companies into a handful of conglomerates, and the price collapse of the early 2010s wiped out almost every international competitor (Fastmarkets). Washington noticed eventually. By 2020, the Pentagon was issuing its first awards to rebuild domestic processing, including for the California mine that had once been the free world's main source (DoD). The mine had already been through bankruptcy; a new owner picked it up for about $20.5 million in 2017, a rounding error against the billions a full chain would take, per a market note on the deal (market note).

The first squeeze

The squeeze showed up first in 2010. After a collision at sea strained its relations with Japan, China blocked shipments of rare earths to Japanese ports for weeks, and then the quiet halt reached some orders bound for the United States and Europe (The New York Times). Beijing also announced a deep cut to the following year's export quota, and prices for several elements multiplied.

The West did what the system had trained it to do. It filed a case. In 2014, a World Trade Organization panel ruled that China's export duties and quotas broke its commitments, rejecting the attempt to justify them under general exceptions (WTO). It was a clean legal victory that changed almost nothing. Losing a case does not build a factory. China retired the explicit quotas, replaced them with licensing and permits, folded the industry into fewer state-linked groups, and kept the architecture of control intact. The quotas went. The control stayed.

A market too small to protect itself

Most famous dependencies are industries large enough to have careers: oil, semiconductors, automobiles. The sharpest choke point works the other way.

Samarium is the element that made the April list sting. Samarium-cobalt magnets keep working at temperatures that would demagnetize their cheaper neodymium cousins, several hundred degrees Celsius, and they sit in radar, missile fins, and the actuators that move a fighter jet's control surfaces (The New York Times, metallurgy analysis). Each F-35 carries about 50 pounds of samarium magnets (The New York Times). By one defense analyst's accounting, samarium-cobalt is about half a percent of the permanent-magnet market by volume, and the metal's annual market runs in the tens of millions of dollars (defense analyst, critical minerals research).

That is the trap in its purest form. A market too small to fund a Western supply chain, and too important to lose. Civilian demand alone was rarely enough to pay for a second source, so the day a licensing office asked defense contractors for paperwork, there was nowhere else to send it.

On April 4, 2025, China's Ministry of Commerce placed samarium, gadolinium, terbium, dysprosium, lutetium, scandium, and yttrium items under export controls, covering samarium metal, alloys, magnet materials, and oxides (MOFCOM). Announced beside retaliatory tariffs, the controls landed on a specific set of industries, with Lockheed Martin and Tesla among the companies affected (Reuters). Ford and Suzuki briefly paused vehicle lines, and by June, with no samarium export licenses approved, the constraint had reached the F-35 program itself (CSIS, The New York Times).

The licensing office

The April controls were built as a valve. A valve does its work without ever firing.

The announcement created a licensing regime rather than an embargo, and China's commerce ministry has said so in as many words: the measures are not export bans, and licenses will be granted for eligible applications (MOFCOM). The throttle shows in the paperwork. Shipments of magnets containing controlled elements sat while exporters worked to prove their cargo was clean, and one trade analyst tracking the controls reported that some shipments declared free of a controlled element tested positive anyway (trade analysis). Nothing was technically banned, so little could be planned around. Chinese strategists have argued the restraint is structural: restrictions work precisely while they stay temporary, because a permanent cut would damage the dominance they use (RFF, Adamas Intelligence).

Then the valve turned further. In October 2025, the controls widened to more elements and reached foreign products made with Chinese rare-earth inputs, an extraterritorial move studied from the American playbook (CSIS, CFR). Companies tied to foreign militaries were steered toward denial (CSIS analysis). For a month, the world priced a rupture. Then, ahead of a leaders' meeting, the package was suspended for a year and folded into a truce (trade reporting). Washington had been escalating on its own track the whole time, with tariffs ahead of the April controls and a Section 232 investigation into processed critical minerals (Federal Register). Two lever-pullers agreed to pause where their pressure overlapped. The controls did not disappear. They went onto a shelf, labeled. A year of the regime still left China's exports down and buyers edgy (Nikkei Asia).

The bill comes due

The clearest sign of how seriously Washington took April was the contract it signed in July. Under a ten-year agreement, the Defense Department guaranteed a floor price of $110 per kilogram for neodymium-praseodymium products from MP Materials and committed to buy the output of a magnet factory that is still under construction, even when cheaper magnets are available elsewhere (FAS, company release). The package included $400 million in preferred equity, a $150 million loan aimed specifically at heavy rare-earth separation at the California mine, and warrants; the billion-dollar construction financing came from JPMorgan and Goldman Sachs, a detail most retellings flatten into a bank commitment (SEC filing, DoD). The design is deliberate. A floor lets private capital treat, as bankable, an industry it avoided for thirty years. A take-or-pay offtake gives the buyer a reason to keep a plant alive while the market matures, and prices above the floor send 30 percent of the excess back to the government.

The rest of the policy wave is thinner than the headlines. The American frameworks with Australia and Japan promise cooperation and financing measures, not committed tonnage or disbursed dollars (White House, White House). And the floor, which is real, is already being tested from below: with protected prices trading under $110, the government is topping up the difference, while dysprosium and terbium, the heaviest and least replaceable elements, remain the true bottleneck no contract can conjure (market analysis).

There is a quieter force at work. Every use of the lever recruits engineers to the exit. Renault and others now ship traction motors built without rare-earth magnets; labs keep chasing iron-based compounds that could replace the hardest magnets entirely; and China's own share of raw mining has been sliding for years as other countries dig (Hacker News, research thread, The China Project). The CFR's advice to Washington is to stop trying to out-mine China and leapfrog the whole layer instead (CFR). None of it is a finished answer. All of it is what a squeeze buys.

What the lever teaches

Strip the arc down and it is an insurance story. A strategic dependency is a risk that looks hypothetical while the monopoly stays cheap and quiet, and insurance against hypothetical risks is the least popular line in any budget. A monopoly can last for decades that way, right up to the day the pressure moves from the balance sheet to the licensing office. After that, the premium gets paid by everyone downstream: the automaker pausing a line, the defense program queuing for a permit, the treasury topping up a price floor.

The fix, in most serious versions, is the same. Someone has to be paid to keep a second supply alive before anyone needs it. A price floor, a take-or-pay contract, a customer willing to pay above market for a domestic line, all of it wasteful right up until the week it is obvious. The Western repair is now buying back, at government prices, the same commitments it declined at market prices for thirty years.

The rocks were never rare. The scarce things were nerve and patience: the nerve to run unglamorous chemistry at home, and the patience to pay for it years before the paperwork arrives. In 1995 the West sold what it no longer wanted to run. It is now learning what the buyer already knew.
