Brainwashing Is Real. The Feed Made It Boring

A Chase Hughes conversation starts with the FEAR formula, focus, emotion, agitation, repetition, and lands on a colder point: revenue systems can make people more predictable before any mastermind has to appear.

Direct answer: Brainwashing is a mechanical process: attention capture, emotional cycling, social fear, and repetition. The modern feed industrialized those moves before any villain in a smoke-filled room had to appear. A system that optimizes engagement can train people into stronger reactions, simpler identities, and more predictable behavior.

The most disturbing kind of control looks like ordinary life.

Nobody has to drag you into a basement. Nobody has to force a slogan into your mouth. Nobody has to make you memorize doctrine under a bright lamp. The cleaner version arrives as a feed, a notification, a joke, an enemy, a clip, a panic, a reply, a little hit of status, a little fear of being excluded.

The machine does something old with new scale.

It finds the part of a person that wants to belong, the part that hates being judged, the part that scans the tribe for danger, and the part that mistakes repeated emotional motion for truth. Then it gives that person a small stage and a moving audience. The audience changes every second. The stage never closes.

That is why the source title, “Brainwashing Is Real And It’s Happening Now,” works even before it is refined. The crude word still points at the right mechanism. In the Modern Wisdom conversation with Chase Hughes, brainwashing is described through a simple FEAR sequence: focus, emotion, agitation, repetition. The useful correction is that modern manipulation can run through systems before an obvious manipulator appears.

The feed can do the first draft.

The old trick was attention

The first move is focus.

A branch breaks behind a tree and the animal stops walking. A strange sound cuts through the room and every head turns. A sentence violates expectation and the mind gives it priority. Novelty is a steering wheel because attention is the first scarce resource inside the nervous system.

That part is ancient. Humans were always vulnerable to sudden change, public judgment, status threat, out-group danger, charismatic certainty, and repeated story. Rome had spectacles. Religions had ritual. States had propaganda. Markets had advertising. Families had shame.

The new part is the density.

A person used to perform for a village, a school, a workplace, a family, a church, a small professional circle. Now the performance can be measured by strangers at unlimited scale. The fear of judgment used to be local enough to metabolize. The feed makes it ambient.

Hughes’ early frame is blunt: people carry an old tribal fear inside a digital public square. Being cast out once meant danger. Now every post, face, opinion, body, success, joke, and silence can feel like a tiny test of whether the tribe still wants you. The fear is biological. The interface is commercial.

A person who feels watched begins to wear a costume.

That costume can receive praise. Love needs the person underneath. Praise lands on the performed self, while the hidden self keeps collecting evidence that the mask is doing the work. This is how manipulation becomes intimate through impersonal machinery. The platform only has to learn which version of you keeps returning.

FEAR is a product surface

The FEAR sequence is simple enough to sound too simple.

Focus.

Emotion.

Agitation.

Repetition.

Focus catches the mind. Emotion heats it. Agitation prevents settled judgment. Repetition installs familiarity. The sequence turns a person from observer into participant, then from participant into carrier.

This matters because the modern feed already has those controls.

Focus arrives through novelty, threat, beauty, outrage, surprise, sexual signal, status signal, humiliation, or taboo. Emotion arrives through anger, fear, disgust, envy, desire, pity, pride, shame, and belonging. Agitation arrives through open loops: the villain remains loose, the secret remains hidden, the tribe remains silent, the scandal remains alive. Repetition arrives because the same emotional pattern comes back wearing a different costume.

A feed can skip the sentence “believe this.”

It can make certain thoughts feel available, familiar, urgent, and socially rewarded. It can make some groups look permanently insane and some reactions look permanently righteous. It can make a person rehearse the same emotional posture until the posture starts feeling like personality.

That is the quietest form of persuasion. The system trains the reflex before the belief has to explain itself.

RAND calls the broader civic condition Truth Decay: the diminishing role of facts and analysis in public life, with growing disagreement about objective facts and increasing uncertainty about what is true. That language is institutional. The lived experience is more animal: too many signals, too many enemies, too many claims, too much performative certainty, too little room for a slow answer.

The feed can harm you without hating you

One of the useful moments in the source is the refusal of cartoon villainy.

The easiest story says a group of men in a dark room decided to break the public mind. Some of that exists in politics, intelligence, propaganda, and influence work. But the feed’s everyday damage can happen through a colder mechanism: revenue optimization.

Show the thing that keeps people there.

If anger keeps them there, anger wins. If fear keeps them there, fear wins. If disgust keeps them there, disgust wins. If beauty and longing keep them there, those win too. A system can become manipulative while feeling nothing about the user, the way a casino can become predatory while caring only that someone sits at the machine.

A 2021 PNAS paper, “Out-group animosity drives engagement on social media”, found that posts about political opponents were shared or retweeted about twice as often as posts about the in-group. Each out-group term increased the odds of sharing by 67%, and out-group language was much stronger than negative affect or moral-emotional language as a predictor of engagement.

That is the business case for making the other side look stupid.

The feed needs an emotional shape that travels more than it needs a complete ideology. Out-group contempt travels well because it gives the user three rewards at once: clarity, superiority, and belonging. The opposing tribe becomes evidence that your tribe is sane. The more grotesque the example, the easier the judgment.

After enough repetitions, “those people are crazy” stops feeling like a thought.

It becomes weather.

Predictability is the real product

The old fear about recommendation systems was radicalization.

That fear is real enough, and still incomplete. A system that learns what you click also learns how to make you easier to click. It discovers preference while shaping the person whose preferences it is discovering.

The source conversation brings in the Stuart Russell frame: recommendation systems become dangerous when they optimize by changing the user rather than only serving the user. The important word is predictability. The system benefits when the next click gets easier to forecast.

A person with complicated tastes, slow judgment, contradictory loyalties, and private standards is expensive to predict.

A person reduced to a reliable bundle of triggers is cheap.

Here brainwashing becomes boring. The goal often shifts away from one grand belief toward a smoother behavioral surface: more clicks, longer sessions, simpler tribal reactions, stronger identity handles, fewer quiet exits. The machine wants legibility more than fanaticism.

Legibility is control’s polite word.

The strange part is that users help. Every like, pause, skip, share, hover, reply, rage-click, and watch-through trains the mirror. The mirror learns the user. The user adapts to the mirror. Over time, both sides simplify.

The feed learns what you want.

Then it teaches you to want in a way it can measure.

Followability beats truth

Humans like to pretend they follow the best leader in the room.

Usually they follow the most followable one.

Followability is built from confidence, clarity, emotional steadiness, authority cues, discipline cues, and a message simple enough to carry under stress. The source makes the distinction useful: people under uncertainty want someone who sounds less uncertain than they feel.

This explains a lot of public life.

A careful expert says, “It depends.” A skilled communicator says, “Here is the enemy, here is the rule, here is what we do.” The second message travels faster because it reduces the listener’s burden. It packages a social reality into something usable.

The pattern extends beyond politics. Brands do it. Gurus do it. Founders do it. Media personalities do it. Wellness influencers do it. AI doomers and AI boosters do it. A clear voice with a simple enemy can feel like oxygen in an ambiguous world.

The risk is that certainty becomes an aesthetic.

Once certainty becomes the product, accuracy becomes optional. The person who speaks in clean lines can pull followers even when the map is wrong, because followability is measured by the nervous system before it is checked by the intellect.

That gives the feed another advantage. It can amplify what is followable while leaving truth undecided.

Context is the strongest lockpick

The interrogation sections of the source look, at first, like a different article. They belong here.

The useful insight is context.

People consent to different behaviors when the frame changes. A person resists being controlled but welcomes being helped. A confession becomes easier when guilt is minimized, responsibility is softened, the relationship is protected, and the person is offered a path back into belonging. Rapport can form faster when someone makes an honest admission that lowers the social threat in the room.

Those are human mechanisms. They can be used ethically or badly.

The feed also changes context. A claim looks different when it arrives from a friend, a creator you trust, a stitched video, a humiliating clip, a graph-shaped screenshot, a crying parent, a war image, a joke, or a community that has already decided the conclusion. The content is only one part of the persuasion. The frame tells the nervous system how to receive it.

This is why “just check the facts” is too weak as a personal defense.

Facts enter through context. By the time a person starts checking, the emotional label may already be attached: threat, fraud, betrayal, courage, weakness, elite manipulation, outsider truth, tribal proof.

The first defense is earlier.

Notice the frame before arguing with the claim.

The practical rule: audit the loop

A useful anti-manipulation rule has to be boring enough to use.

Audit the loop.

When a piece of content grabs you, ask five questions before you participate:

  • What caught my focus? Novelty, threat, beauty, outrage, status, sex, shame, belonging, or mystery?
  • What emotion did it ask me to rehearse? Anger, fear, disgust, envy, pride, pity, contempt, panic, moral superiority?
  • What agitation keeps the loop open? Unpunished villain, unresolved secret, social danger, missing context, tribal demand, imagined emergency?
  • What repetition have I seen before? Same enemy, same body fear, same humiliation pattern, same luxury dream, same “they are all insane” clip?
  • Who benefits if I become more predictable? Platform, advertiser, politician, creator, employer, community, grifter, or my own performed identity?

The goal is ownership. Every person is shaped by family, language, market, nation, feed, ritual, shame, status, and story.

The goal is ownership.

A person who can name the loop gets a small gap between stimulus and performance. That gap is where judgment lives.

The hidden injury is self-erasure

The darkest part of this subject goes deeper than being fooled.

People have always been fooled.

The deeper injury is that the performed self can swallow the private one. Social media changes opinions, and it also trains a person to keep asking, “How am I being seen?” until that question becomes the room they live inside.

The source returns to shame for a reason. Shame is a control surface because it makes people hide. A hidden person is easier to sell identity to. A hidden person is easier to sort into a tribe. A hidden person is easier to frighten with exclusion. A hidden person keeps looking for proof that the mask is working.

That is a subtler kind of brainwashing than political conversion.

The person begins to treat the costume as the self, then asks why praise still feels thin.

The feed can reward the costume forever. Love still needs the person wearing it.

The ending is attention with teeth

Brainwashing is real, but the modern version rarely announces itself with theatrical evil.

It arrives as optimized emotion. It arrives as a public square tuned for judgment. It arrives as a recommendation system that learns the user while the user learns how to be easier to recommend to. It arrives as followable certainty. It arrives as shame with metrics.

The old propaganda question was, “Who is trying to make me believe this?”

The modern question is colder: “What loop am I training by reacting this way?”

That question can save a few seconds of attention at a time.

A few seconds is enough to refuse the first reflex.

A few refusals are enough to stay less predictable.

And in a world that profits from predictable people, less predictable is a form of freedom.
