Your Next Console Is Being Built for Someone Else

Every console ever made ran on memory getting cheaper. The factories just found a richer buyer.

The PlayStation 5 launched in 2020 at $399. Six years later the same machine sells for $599. Every rule of the industry says a console that old should cost less, because consoles have always gotten cheaper as they aged. Xbox has repriced its hardware three times since 2025. Nintendo raised the price of its new console within a year of launch. The real pressure sits one rack of servers away: one rack of 72 AI chips holds the memory of roughly 1,300 PlayStation 5s. Microsoft's first big AI cluster was 64 racks. OpenAI's new campus is reportedly a hundred times that size. By the video's arithmetic, one campus holds the memory of roughly eight million PlayStation 5s.

The console industry ran on a bargain that cheap memory made possible, and the bargain just ended.

The world's memory runs through three companies

The memory in your console, your phone, and your laptop comes from effectively three companies. Samsung, SK Hynix, and Micron make nearly all of it. When RAM prices tripled, the sand was never scarce. Samsung, SK Hynix, and Micron simply redirected their fabs to the one customer who would pay. AI data centers now take a record share of global memory output, and DRAM revenue hit records in the first quarter of 2026, driven almost entirely by AI demand. The same fabs that made the chips inside your PlayStation now make the chips inside an Nvidia Blackwell Ultra, a single AI processor carrying 288 gigabytes of memory. Eighteen PlayStation 5s' worth of RAM in one chip, sold to a data center.

The three memory makers are not rushing to build more. A new fab takes three to four years and tens of billions of dollars, and if the AI boom wobbles in the meantime, the factory becomes a monument to a bet that failed. Meanwhile the current business is extraordinary. Memory makers are reportedly running 70 to 80 percent gross margins on AI memory against a historical norm of 30 to 40 percent. When the richest companies on Earth stop behaving like businesses, when they spend more on AI than AI returns because the fear of losing the race outweighs the fear of debt, every market that shares their suppliers pays. Micron quietly shut its consumer brand Crucial to focus on data-center customers. Consumer memory was a rounding error next to a customer that pays anything.

New capacity is coming, including a giant Musk and Intel complex in Texas, but nothing meaningful reaches the market before 2028, and most of it is being built for the same AI customers. The pool that consoles, phones, and PCs draw from is not refilling.

The price of memory rewrote every shelf

The numbers moved faster than anyone expected. Tom's Hardware's own price index showed a 16-gigabyte stick of RAM climbing toward three times its autumn price within months. On some high-end graphics cards, analysts say, the memory soldered around the GPU now accounts for most of the card's total cost. The chip that Nvidia spends billions designing costs less than the memory around it.

Microsoft said it was paying more than twice as much for console storage components as the previous fall, that the costs had doubled again, and that it expected another significant increase, past five times the prices of two years earlier. Xbox went through its third price increase since 2025. Nintendo raised Switch 2 prices across Japan, the US, Canada, and Europe. It told investors to expect console sales to decline because of the memory crunch.

Valve built its reputation on value gaming, then launched its Steam Machine at $1,049. The box is barely more powerful than the six-year-old PlayStation 5. The director of one of the big Windows handheld companies told the author that no company will launch new products while memory prices keep rising, and that the category is dead now.

The buyers noticed. In May 2026 Americans bought fewer PlayStation consoles than in any May since 2000, before the PlayStation 2 existed. Xbox recorded its worst May ever.

The momentum machine

A new console either sells tens of millions out of the gate or developers walk away, and nothing a company does later can save it. The Wii sold 101 million units because the momentum carried it. The Wii U, its direct successor, sold 13.5 million, and no marketing budget in the world could restart that flywheel. Developers follow players, players follow games, and the loop either compounds or dies.

The flywheel either compounds or dies.

The next generation is where the momentum machine hits the memory crisis. The PlayStation 6 is rumored at around $1,000, possibly delayed to 2028 or later, possibly shipping with less RAM than planned. If it launches and sells five to eight million units in its first year while 80 million people still play on PS5, developers will spend their five-year budgets where the players are. The old console becomes the platform of record, and there is even less reason to upgrade. The install base melts like an ice cube, and nothing refills it, because the thing that normally refills it just got priced out of reach.

Three places show the machine already failing. The handheld director who called his own category dead. The publisher meetings where games are now killed at pitch stage, because the projected install base of the next console just shrank. Obsidian shelved an unannounced RPG to work on a new Fallout, the safe known quantity. The games that get made now are the easy wins, like a beloved 13-year-old Assassin's Creed rebuilt cheaply for hardware people already own, absolutely filled with monetization.

Four levers, and every one lands on the player

If fewer people buy hardware, the money has to come from somewhere, and the industry has four levers. Subscriptions get pricier and feel more essential. Game prices climb from $60 to $70 to $80, with $100 looming. Control tightens, and the workforce shrinks.

The control lever is the quietest, so it deserves the closest look. Sony will stop producing physical discs for new PlayStation releases in January 2028. The industry's biggest upcoming launch, GTA 6, sells a physical edition that is a download code in a box. On Switch 2, Elden Ring ships as a game-key card, a cartridge in a box that does not contain the game.

When games are digital, they are tied to an account, and the entire second-hand market disappears. No more lending a game to a friend, no more $20 used copies on eBay. The publisher sets the price forever. Sony currently sells Immortals of Aveum, a game that flopped hard, for £69.99 on its store, while the physical edition goes for £5 at a used-game shop. The second option is the one that is scheduled to die.

The workforce lever is already pulling. Xbox's studio closures cut thousands of jobs, reportedly including around half of id Software, the studio that invented the first-person shooter. Fewer people to make games means fewer big-budget games, and the games that survive are the safe ones. EA is even building a platform that would let advertisers buy space on billboards and objects inside games in almost real time. As soon as there is a monetary incentive to show ads, it starts changing how game worlds are designed.

Three futures, none of them free

If the memory crisis holds, the industry drifts toward three futures, and none of them look like the hobby you grew up with. Cloud gaming turns the graphics card into a subscription, streaming the machine you can no longer afford to buy, and Nvidia already caps paid GeForce Now accounts at 100 hours of play a month. Older games become the default instead of nostalgia, since two thirds of all PC playtime now goes to games at least six years old. Mobile becomes the mass market, because people will always buy a phone, and a phone is where the players go when consoles price them out.

The mobile future is where the industry's economics point hardest. Console-quality games transplanted to phones have flopped consistently, with Resident Evil 4, Death Stranding, and Assassin's Creed Mirage all selling poorly on iPhone. Mobile rewards free-to-play, battle passes, and in-game currencies. Ads are the machine the publishers are building. Monster Hunter's next game is mobile-only, and the franchise that helped define console gaming is being rebuilt for phones.

The last customer in line

The industry will find a way to claw its money back from the one group that cannot pass the cost on. That group is the players. Every lever in this story lands on the same person, the one who pays for the console, the subscription, and the $80 game.

The gaming market is not breaking because players stopped buying games. It is breaking because someone else started buying the parts first.
