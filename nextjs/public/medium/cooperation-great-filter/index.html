The Tournament That Made Cooperation Look Like a Cosmic Filter

This article follows the evidence behind a familiar story, then turns it into a practical way to read the system, its limits, and the decision a careful reader can make next.

From Axelrod's prisoner's dilemma to bacteria, AI labs, and Fermi's silent sky, the most profitable strategy in life may be the one civilizations fail to keep.

The classroom version of the prisoner's dilemma makes selfishness look mathematically clean.

Two suspects. Two choices. Betray or stay silent. The rational move is to betray, even though mutual trust would leave both people better off.

Then the game repeats.

At that point, the question changes: how much future do I believe in?

the engine inside Homo Deus's video Самая выгодная стратегия жизни. On the surface, it is a video about game theory and cooperation. Underneath, it is darker. It is asking whether life, intelligence, and civilization all face the same exam: can a system learn to restrain short-term advantage before it destroys the environment that made advantage possible?

The difficult part: morality is only the surface. Under it sits an engineering problem.

The Wrong Argument

The weak version of the argument is familiar: cooperation is good, selfishness is bad, so we should all be nicer.

too small.

The stronger argument is colder. Cooperation is the technology that makes the future real.

Easy reading vs Harder reading

  • Be nice: Make yourself safe to cooperate with
  • Refuse betrayal: Retaliate enough to make betrayal expensive
  • Forgive: Restore cooperation before revenge becomes the whole system
  • Trust people: Build conditions where trust is rational
  • Think long term: Keep the shadow of the future long enough to discipline the present

the prisoner's dilemma keeps escaping the seminar room.

The video walks it through nuclear competition, antibiotics, climate, AI labs, trench warfare, vampire bats, cleaner fish, bacteria, viruses, symbiosis, multicellular life, and finally the silence of the sky. That sounds like a wild list until the same pattern keeps reappearing.

A local actor can gain by defecting.

The shared system loses if enough actors defect.

The future decides whether the short-term gain was actually a gain.

The Tournament

In 1981, Robert Axelrod and W. D. Hamilton published "The Evolution of Cooperation" in Science. Axelrod later expanded the work into the 1984 book of the same name. The University of Michigan describes Axelrod's work as asking how patterns of social behavior emerge across biology, psychology, and computer science.

The famous part is the tournament.

Axelrod invited researchers to submit strategies for a repeated prisoner's dilemma. The winner was a simple reciprocal strategy associated with Anatol Rapoport: start by cooperating, then copy what the other player did last time.

The video presents the lesson in four qualities:

  • begin cooperative
  • answer betrayal
  • forgive quickly
  • be readable

There is nothing sentimental about that. A strategy that never punishes becomes prey. A strategy that never forgives becomes trapped in feud. A strategy nobody understands cannot create stable expectations.

The winning pattern was closer to: be good news to meet, bad news to exploit, and easy news to understand.

a much more useful rule.

The Mechanism

Martin Nowak's "Five Rules for the Evolution of Cooperation" gives the larger biological frame. Cooperation can be supported by kin selection, direct reciprocity, indirect reciprocity, network reciprocity, and group selection.

In ordinary language:

Help can survive when relatives share genes.

Help can survive when I may meet you again.

Help can survive when reputation travels.

Help can survive when cooperators cluster.

Help can survive when groups with better internal cooperation outcompete other groups.

The video uses this ladder well. It pushes cooperation out of human morality and down into animals, bacteria, viruses, symbiosis, and multicellular life.

Bacteria have no speeches about ethics. They still coordinate. In quorum sensing, bacterial cells use chemical signals to regulate behavior based on population density. A 2023 Nature Communications paper frames quorum sensing as a way for bacteria to pool information and make more reliable group decisions. Princeton's publication page for Bonnie Bassler's quorum sensing work describes it as gene regulation in response to cell population density.

Here the evidence shows worse than "nature can be nice." Cooperation is older than our stories about cooperation.

Life keeps rediscovering coordination because isolated extraction hits a ceiling. At some point, the individual unit wins too much and loses the system. A cell that exits the contract of multicellularity can become cancer. It takes resources as if the body has no future, then kills the body, then dies with it.

That image is impossible to ignore.

One Concrete Scenario

Thought experiment.

Imagine two AI labs racing toward a system neither fully understands.

Each lab can slow down, share evaluations, support external audits, and accept weaker short-term market position. Or each can push ahead because it fears the other lab will push ahead.

If both coordinate, the world gets a better chance at safety.

If one coordinates and the other defects, the defector may gain money, talent, prestige, and strategic advantage.

If both defect, capability rises faster than governance.

the video's AI chapter belongs next to nuclear weapons and climate. Frontier AI has the shape of a repeated cooperation problem under extreme pressure, even while researchers still argue about the exact probability and mechanism of extreme risk.

The Center for AI Safety statement put AI extinction risk in the same public category as pandemics and nuclear war. Reasonable people disagree about probabilities, mechanisms, and timelines. But the coordination problem is visible even before the risk model is settled.

Every actor can say the same thing:

If we slow down alone, we lose.

If everyone speeds up, the system may lose.

That sentence is the prisoner's dilemma wearing a lab badge.

The Reversal

The midpoint of the argument is sharper than "cooperation works."

Cooperation works only when the future has enough weight.

one-shot interactions rot so quickly. A tourist taxi ride, a collapsing business, a last term in office, a dying empire, a platform trying to juice one more quarter: all share the same pathology. The actor no longer expects to pay the future cost of present extraction.

The video's phrase "shadow of the future" is doing real work here. When the future is long, retaliation, reputation, and repair matter. When the future is short, cheating becomes cheaper.

This is also why institutions exist.

Good institutions lengthen the future. They make promises durable, punishment predictable, reputation portable, and cooperation legible beyond face-to-face trust. A market, a court, a treaty, a protocol, a standard, a source-control history, a public benchmark: all are attempts to make tomorrow show up in today's payoff table.

Without that, the clever move becomes parasitic.

And parasitic intelligence can be extremely impressive right until it runs out of host.

What We Actually Know

We know Axelrod and Hamilton gave the repeated prisoner's dilemma a powerful evolutionary frame.

We know Nowak identifies several mechanisms that can support cooperation under natural selection.

We know quorum sensing gives bacteria a real communication mechanism for coordinated behavior.

The Great Filter remains a speculative lens, rather than a measured object. Robin Hanson's original essay asks why dead matter so rarely appears to become expanding, lasting life. It gives us a question, not a verdict.

Freeman Dyson's 1960 paper proposed looking for artificial infrared signatures from civilizations using stellar-scale energy. No clear evidence of such civilizations has become part of settled science.

The article's core claim sits between those facts:

If intelligence commonly reaches dangerous power before it reaches stable cooperation, cooperation becomes a plausible candidate filter.

Call it a useful suspicion.

The Strongest Critique

The strongest critique is that "cooperation" is too broad to explain anything.

Cancer, climate, AI races, trench truces, bacteria, Dyson spheres, and the Fermi paradox are different problems. They operate at different scales, with different mechanisms, evidence, and incentives. A good reciprocal strategy in an iterated game cannot automatically solve geopolitics. A bacterial signaling system cannot tell us how to regulate AI. The Great Filter may sit behind us, ahead of us, or nowhere in this frame at all.

That critique is right.

The cooperation lens becomes sloppy if it pretends one mechanism explains every domain.

But the lens survives if we use it more carefully. Treat it as a diagnostic question:

Where does short-term local advantage create long-term system failure, and what makes the future matter again?

That question travels well.

It travels from antibiotic overuse to climate policy. It travels from open-source maintenance to frontier model races. It travels from cells to states because the exact mechanism changes, but the failure mode rhymes.

Extraction without a future is a timer disguised as strength.

The Cosmic Version

The video's final turn is the most powerful part: it takes a familiar scientific idea and makes the human self-image smaller.

The Kardashev scale classifies civilizations by energy use. Dyson imagined that sufficiently advanced civilizations might leave detectable infrared signatures as they harvest stellar energy. The Fermi paradox asks why, if the galaxy is old and large, we do not see obvious signs of others.

The usual explanations are astronomical, technological, or probabilistic.

Maybe life is rare.

Maybe intelligence is rare.

Maybe interstellar travel is harder than it looks.

Maybe advanced civilizations hide.

Maybe we are early.

The cooperation filter adds a different possibility: maybe many civilizations learn to split atoms, edit genomes, heat planets, and build artificial minds before they learn to keep the shadow of the future long enough.

They fail when intelligence increases the prize for defection faster than it builds the architecture for cooperation.

That would make the universe quiet for a humiliating reason.

Maybe mind is possible, but unstable.

How To Lengthen The Future

The practical version of the argument is simple: make defection expensive, make repair possible, and make the next round visible.

For an AI lab, that means shared evaluations before public capability races, incident disclosure norms, model cards that state real deployment limits, and third-party audits with consequences. If every lab can privately defect while publicly praising safety, the payoff table is already broken.

For a company, it means incentives that survive the quarter. Pay people for maintenance, not only launches. Keep decision logs. Make rollback cheaper than denial. Reward the person who catches the hidden systemic risk before it becomes a heroic cleanup story.

For a team, it means clarity. Say what counts as cooperation, what counts as betrayal, how retaliation works, and when forgiveness resets the game. Vague trust is fragile. Legible trust can compound.

For a country, it means institutions that make tomorrow politically real: independent measurement, treaty verification, durable courts, procurement rules, and public records that outlast the current winner.

The shared pattern is boring because it has to be. Cooperation does not survive on inspiration. It survives on memory, penalties, exit costs, reputation, and repeated contact.

What Changed My Mind

I used to think of cooperation as an ethical preference.

The better frame is that cooperation is a way for a system to have a future.

This changes how the video lands. The best strategy in life is to become the kind of agent others can safely build the future around. Cooperative first. Retaliatory enough. Forgiving enough. Legible enough.

At human scale, that sounds like character.

At institutional scale, it sounds like governance.

At planetary scale, it sounds like survival.

At cosmic scale, it may be the difference between a civilization that turns a planet into a launchpad and one that turns it into a tumor.

the image I cannot shake.

Earth as a body. Intelligence as a new tissue. Cooperation as the contract that keeps the tissue from becoming cancer.

If we keep the contract, the planet becomes the first rung of a ladder.

If we break it, the sky stays quiet for a worse reason.

Something like us kept happening, briefly, brilliantly, and then no further.
