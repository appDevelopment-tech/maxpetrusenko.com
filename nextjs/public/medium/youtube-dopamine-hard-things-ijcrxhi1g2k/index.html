Dopamine Sets the Price of Effort. Your Phone Keeps Undercutting It.

Dopamine prices effort against payoff, and cheap rewards move the number. What the crash evidence covers, and which price changes actually move the comparison.

A brain keeps a running comparison between what an action costs and what it pays, and cheap rewards move that comparison. The comparison explains why hard things stop feeling worth doing, and why friction and a nearer payoff change the calculation.

I build agents for a living, and one of them drafted this. The opinions and the edits are mine.

In the mid-1990s, 34 people in treatment for heroin dependence were handed a short story. It opened with a sentence about a man named Bill, who wakes and begins to think about his future. The question was how far ahead Bill was looking. A comparison group of 59 people without a drug habit placed him about 4.7 years out. The group in treatment placed him nine days out (Petry, Bickel and Arnett, 1998; the averages are reported in a later review from Bickel's group).

Nine days is short enough to change what a person starts. Effort is priced against payoff, and that price moves whenever a reward sits closer or arrives cheaper, but the popular account of why it moves points at a tank that does not behave the way the advice assumes.

The price usually arrives as a mood. At 9pm the day feels wasted, the reason is hard to name, and the phone is already in your hand.

The pricing shows up in three places that matter for anyone trying to do difficult work. It sets how far ahead effort seems worth extending, it responds to friction, and it can be defeated by a goal that was never worth its price in the first place.

What dopamine is measuring

Dopamine carries a signal about pursuit. Kent Berridge's work on incentive salience separates wanting a reward from liking it, and concludes that dopamine is "neither necessary nor sufficient" for hedonic pleasure (Berridge, 2007). The wanting and liking circuits can be damaged independently, which is hard to reconcile with the idea of a single pleasure chemical (Berridge and Robinson, 2016).

Wolfram Schultz's recordings of dopamine neurons in monkeys describe something narrower and more useful: the cells fire when a reward arrives unexpectedly, and then shift their firing to whatever predicts it (Schultz, 1998). A reward that is fully predicted produces little response. The signal tracks surprise and learning rather than volume. The same chemical can appear in movement, in learning, and in motivation without contradiction.

The part that matters for a Tuesday morning is how dopamine enters effort-based choice. John Salamone and Merce Correa's review of that literature describes dopamine as modulating how much work an organism will perform for a reward, including how it trades a larger, harder payoff against a smaller, easier one (Salamone and Correa, 2018). A system like that recalculates after every scroll session.

What the crash evidence actually covers

Something real does happen to the reward system in addiction. George Koob and Michel Le Moal described the process as allostasis: repeated drug exposure drives counteradaptive changes that fail to return within the normal range, so the baseline itself moves (Koob and Le Moal, 2001). Nora Volkow's imaging work found dopamine D2 receptor availability lower in methamphetamine users than in comparison subjects, by 16 percent in the caudate and 10 percent in the putamen (Volkow and colleagues, 2001). The National Institute on Drug Abuse states the consequence plainly: in addiction, the ability to feel pleasure from ordinary rewards is reduced (NIDA).

Those findings come from drug exposure at pharmacological doses. The tank metaphor borrows its evidence from that pharmacology and then changes the dose. Extending it to a phone habit goes beyond what those studies measured. Peter Grinspoon's review of the fasting trend at Harvard Health makes the narrower point that dopamine "doesn't actually decrease when you avoid overstimulating activities, so a dopamine 'fast' doesn't actually lower your dopamine levels" (Harvard Health, 2020). Reporting in The Scientist describes the same category error, with Stephanie Borgland noting that abstinence stops reinforcing a behaviour while leaving the habit in place (The Scientist, 2024).

The behavioural-addiction literature also keeps its claims modest. A 2010 review found only gambling and internet use had enough evidence to consider classification, and warned that there was "insufficient data to justify any classification of other proposed behavioral addictions" (Grant and colleagues, 2010). People describe the pattern in those terms: cheap rewards crowd out expensive ones, and the result feels like depletion. The chemistry being invoked to explain it is doing decorative work.

The price of waiting

Behavioural economics has a cleaner account of the same experience. Delay discounting describes how a reward loses value as the wait to receive it grows (Bickel and Marsch, 2001). A smaller reward available now can beat a larger one available later without anyone behaving irrationally, because the later reward is worth less at the moment of choice.

A meta-analysis of 46 studies found steeper discounting among people with addictive disorders, with a pooled effect of 0.15 across all comparisons and 0.58 across the methodologically comparable subset (MacKillop and colleagues, 2011). The paper reports high heterogeneity and evidence of small-study bias, so the association looks consistent and its size looks uncertain. It is a correlate of addiction, and the causal direction is not settled.

What the curve describes well is the stretch of a project where effort has been paid and the payoff is still invisible. Training for four weeks changes little that a mirror can confirm. A business in its second year has consumed savings and still cannot show a customer. The value is there in the model and absent in the feedback, and the decision to continue is being made by a system that weights what it can feel.

You have probably heard that 80 percent of New Year's resolutions are dead by the second week of February. The two-year follow-up study usually attached to that figure found 77 percent still going after one week and 19 percent after two years (Norcross and Vangarelli, 1989). A larger 2020 study of 1,062 resolvers found that 80.9 percent of the 670 people who responded were succeeding in February (Oscarsson and colleagues, 2020). The real numbers describe a slow decay with a long tail. That shape matters, because slow decay responds to maintenance while a cliff invites fatalism.

Friction moves the price

A reward that requires a password, a walk, or a separate device costs more to reach, and a reward that sits on the home screen costs almost nothing. The arithmetic runs in both directions: friction added to the cheap option and removed from the wanted one changes the comparison before any decision gets made.

Concretely, that looks like a rule that non-grocery purchases wait 24 hours, a phone set to grayscale and left in another room during work, and a standing block on the hardest task before the day's first notification. Each one is a price change, and it works by removing a decision instead of winning one.

For some people the cleanest price change is a blanket rule. Moderation keeps the negotiation open every day, and a rule with no exceptions closes it. Anyone who has tried to drink less by measuring pours recognises the difference in attention cost between a limit and a month of none.

There is a second effect that makes this more than damage control. Effort can add value to an outcome rather than only subtracting from it. Michael Inzlicht and his colleagues review evidence that the same result is experienced as more rewarding when more effort produced it, including the effort justification effect, the IKEA effect, and learned industriousness (Inzlicht, Shenhav and Olivola, 2018). A thing you built carries value the identical thing bought does not. That is how difficult work becomes self-sustaining, and it only opens when the work is aimed somewhere the payoff can actually land.

The wall you are climbing

Stephen Covey told a story about a patrol dropped behind enemy lines. The soldiers start cutting their way through the jungle, and after a while one of them climbs a tree, looks around, and shouts that they are going the wrong direction. Nobody had checked the heading before starting to swing.

A misaligned goal raises the price of every step. A job you dread, a relationship that has run its course, and a training programme for a body or a sport you do not actually want all share a structure: the effort is real, the payoff never arrives, and the person concludes that their discipline is broken. The same effort aimed at a goal they want would clear the same bar with less friction, because the payoff is closer to the work.

The check that costs least is asking why three times. Wanting to start a business resolves to wanting uncapped income, which resolves to wanting freedom over your calendar. A sales role with uncapped commission might reach the third answer in a year instead of the five to ten the business needs. Wanting a leaner body resolves to wanting attention, which may be available without the part that makes you miserable. Each answer moves the target closer to the reward, and closer targets are cheaper to pursue.

What the pricing model cannot fix

A short horizon is not always caused by cheap rewards. Sendhil Mullainathan and Eldar Shafir's work on scarcity found that financial worry consumes bandwidth directly, with induced money concerns costing roughly 13 IQ points in their measurements, and produces tunnelling toward the immediate problem at the expense of longer-term goals (Scarcity, 2013). When rent is due in nine days, a nine-day horizon is an accurate reading of the environment.

The attention side is structural as well. Shoshana Zuboff argues that the extraction of behavioural data happens below awareness, which makes the advice to control your attention a poor defence against it (Zuboff, 2022). Jelle Bruineberg locates the problem in the continuous availability of easily available possibilities for action, which is a property of the environment rather than a flaw in the person (Bruineberg, 2023). Friction is what an individual can do about a design problem, and it is damage limitation rather than a cure.

Burnout deserves the same honesty. A clinical commentary on burnout argues that the syndrome reflects poor work design, and that individual resilience training lets organisations avoid fixing the job (Douros, 2020). Angela Duckworth's own recent position, as reported in The New Yorker, moves toward situation design and away from willpower, after a meta-analysis found grit's correlation with academic performance around 0.18 (The New Yorker, 2026; NPR, 2016). Finding hard things hard is sometimes an accurate report about a badly designed week.

The smallest condition you control

The nine days and the 4.7 years describe two forecasts produced by two environments. Forecasts move when conditions move, and the conditions available to most people are small: the phone in another room, two hours before the notifications start, a 24-hour rule on the purchase, a third why before the next commitment.

Pick one this week and watch whether the next hour pays better than the last one did. If it does, that is one lever you can reuse on the next condition. If it does not, the constraint is probably the wall or the rent, and neither of those is fixed by a timer. Knowing which one is binding decides whether the next move is a new goal or a new month.
