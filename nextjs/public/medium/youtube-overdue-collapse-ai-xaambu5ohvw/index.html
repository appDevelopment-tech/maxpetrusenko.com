The Overdue Collapse of Artificial Intelligence

Ford rehired the engineers it replaced, Amazon tore down its leaderboard, and a $725 billion wager is still priced against your wages.

The first audit of the AI mandate did not come from a lab or a regulator. It came from payroll, warranty claims, and a leaderboard nobody could stop gaming. The verdict says less about the machines than about the scoreboard their owners chose.

Ford calls them gray beards, and this June the company confirmed that roughly 350 of them are back on the payroll. They are veteran quality engineers who were shown the door as the carmaker pushed automated systems into inspection work, part of a salaried workforce that has shrunk by roughly 5,300 people since 2020. The systems kept missing things. The veterans kept being right.

Ford's vice president of vehicle hardware engineering described the lesson in one sentence: "Artificial intelligence is a fantastic tool, but it's only as good as the information you use to train it" (BBC). The information in question had walked out the door with the inspectors. This spring, Ford finished first among mainstream brands in the J.D. Power initial quality study, a rank it had not held since 2010 (TNW).

Reading the reversal as a verdict on AI is tempting, and mostly wrong. What unites the rehires, the leaderboards, and the recall bills is a measurement failure: the industry spent three years tracking whether people used the tools and almost no time tracking whether the tools worked at all. Yet the bill for that distinction is now arriving, and it is denominated in the most embarrassing currency available to a company, which is hiring back the people it was sure it no longer needed.

The mandate era

The memo that set the tone was published by a chief executive who knew it would leak. In April 2025, Shopify's Tobi Lutke told his staff that "reflexive AI usage is now a baseline expectation at Shopify" (Shopify memo). It read as enthusiasm, but it worked as policy, and within a year the pattern had reached the blunt end of the org chart. Coinbase cut about 14 percent of its staff, and its CEO Brian Armstrong described the destination without flinching: "rebuilding Coinbase as an intelligence, with humans around the edge aligning it" (Coinbase email).

Amazon turned usage into a game. An employee-made leaderboard called KiroRank ranked engineers by how much they used the company's AI tool, and staff answered the incentive exactly as you would expect, assigning agents pointless tasks to climb the rankings. Amazon deprecated the board in May, and the company's statement on the reversal became the most revealing sentence the industry produced all year: "Don't use AI just to use AI" (Business Insider). The same dynamic was reported at Microsoft and Meta, where token counts bought standing the way lines of code once did (Fortune).

What the scoreboard paid for

If you want to know what a scoreboard pays for, watch what it rewards. Allbirds, a shoe company that went public at a $4 billion valuation, sold its brand and footwear assets for $39 million in March. By April it had renamed itself NewBird AI and raised a $50 million convertible facility to buy computer chips it plans to rent out (TechCrunch). The stock jumped about 400 percent on the announcement, to roughly $13 (Ars Technica). The business changed nothing except the sentence describing it, and the sentence was worth more than the shoes.

Chegg shows the other side of the same accounting. The homework-help company just finished a quarter with revenue down 48 percent from a year earlier, at $63.3 million, and it guided the next quarter to as little as $49 million (Chegg Q1 2026). In a world where the answer to most homework questions lives in a chat window, the tools that halved its business were the same tools every remaining company was being graded on using.

The bill arrives

By spring, the bill was arriving faster than the results. Uber's chief operating officer said publicly that it was getting harder to justify what the company spent on AI tokens (Business Insider). Engineers on the receiving end of the mandates were less diplomatic: "Measuring token usage as a productivity metric is like measuring keystrokes," one wrote, in a thread full of people who had watched their employers confuse a receipt with a result (Hacker News).

The pattern became a headline in July, when CNBC reported that employers who cut staff citing AI were already walking the decisions back. Ford, IBM, and Commonwealth Bank of Australia are among those refocusing on human capital after the automated versions of the same jobs struggled (CNBC). The savings had been visible in the quarter they were booked. The costs took a little longer to show up, the way costs usually do.

The $725 billion question

The retreat is not because the wager got smaller. Google, Microsoft, Meta, and Amazon plan to spend as much as $725 billion on capital expenditures this year, up 77 percent from last year's $410 billion, most of it aimed at AI infrastructure (Yahoo Finance). There is a serious case that the spending is beginning to work: a July report found generative AI revenue climbing and argued the buildout is starting to justify itself (Los Angeles Times). That case may even be right.

The problem is the timetable the money implies. A return measured in hundreds of billions of dollars per year has to come out of the only pool on the planet that size, and the pool is payroll. Every dollar of the wager is positioned against the cost of a human being, which is why the audits happening in quality bays and on leaderboards matter more than the demos.

What the veterans were rehired to do

Ford's rehires are not the defeat of automation, and reading them that way misses the more interesting transaction. The gray beards were brought back to do two jobs: catch what the systems miss, and teach the systems what took decades to learn. The quality study the company now tops measures the first job. The recall record measures how much remains, since Ford still led American automakers with 51 recalls covering more than 11 million vehicles this year, more than double the next-closest manufacturer (TNW), and it remains the most-recalled automaker in the country (Independent). The turnaround is real and incomplete at the same time.

The pattern extends past Ford. Banks that replaced service desks with voice bots rebuilt the human version of the same capacity. Companies that automated their HR lines found a minority of requests that judgment alone could carry, and went back to hiring for it (CNBC). Whether the returning work comes back at the same price, with the same ladder attached, is the open question, and it is the question the next decade will answer.

The incentive is the plot

None of this happened because the executives were stupid. It happened because the math rewarded it, and the math is worth walking through. Imagine a town with ten shop owners, each employing workers who spend their paychecks across the other nine stores. One owner replaces half his staff with machines and keeps every dollar of the savings. His former employees now have less to spend, and nine-tenths of that lost spending lands on the other owners rather than on him. He keeps the gain. The town absorbs the damage.

In a market, the other nine owners eventually run the same calculation and arrive at the same answer. Being the last shop in town with a full payroll does not make you principled; it makes you expensive. The trap is structural, and nothing about it changed when Ford's quality scores came back. The savings from cutting people are private and immediate. The damage is shared and slow. The next time someone works out how to swap a person for a system, the arithmetic will say what it said the first time, and whoever waits will be told they are gambling with the company.

What a person prevents

The number that AI promises to measure is cost. The numbers it keeps failing to measure are what a person prevents and what a person creates. A quality inspector walking a Ford line prevents a recall, and the recall that never happens appears on no dashboard celebrating adoption. A veteran engineer creates the next process, and training data contains only what already happened. Machines compress the past with astonishing skill, faster than any human alive, but the next thing the world has never seen is a different kind of object. It is still made by people with ideas and the agency to spend them.

The discipline underneath all of it is old and unglamorous: score the thing you actually want. Usage is a proxy, proxies become games, and games get gamed, which is what a leaderboard of tokens was always going to teach. What survives an audit is the outcome, the defect caught, the customer retained, the system that did not fail.

Ford's veterans walked back through the same doors they were shown out of, which makes for a satisfying story at a moment when satisfying stories are scarce. The satisfaction is premature. The money that replaced them has not gone home, and the bet is still open. What changed is that the question has finally been asked out loud. It concerns whether anyone was keeping score of the right thing. The first results came back in favor of the humans. The scorekeepers now have to decide what to do with the receipt.
