You Should Be Worried About Sam Altman

The OpenAI board built a kill switch for one man. When it finally pulled it, money, employees, Microsoft, and charisma broke the switch in 72 hours. ("You should, unfortunately, be worried about Sam Altman" by AI In Context and 80,000 Hours) (Emergency stop button (red on yellow background) on a control board)

At noon on November 17, 2023, Sam Altman opened his laptop in a Las Vegas hotel room expecting a routine video call. He was in town for the Formula 1 race, a well-earned break after a year of press tours and meetings with heads of state. On the other end was OpenAI's board, and Ilya Sutskever read from a prepared statement: the board had lost confidence in Altman's ability to lead the company, his employment ended effective immediately, and a public announcement would follow shortly.

Altman was too stunned to speak. Twenty minutes later he was locked out of his company laptop and could not reach his own Gmail. His phone took so many texts per second that the Messages app crashed. Three hundred miles away, hundreds of employees stood in the San Francisco office refreshing the same four tabs, because nobody could work and nobody could say why. The board's public statement explained the firing with a phrase that did more damage than clarity: Altman had been "not consistently candid in his communications with the board." (OpenAI, November 17, 2023)

OpenAI had been built around a promise that no one person should control artificial intelligence. The mechanism that enforced that promise sat directly above the company: a nonprofit board with the legal power to fire the CEO. It had never been pulled before.

OpenAI built a kill switch for one man. The first time anyone pulled it, the company broke the switch instead.

The brake sits inside the machine

This matters beyond one company because the same design appears everywhere. A safety officer who can halt a launch, an auditor who can flag a deal, an ethics committee that can veto a product: all of them sit on the payroll of the thing they are supposed to check. The OpenAI weekend was a public stress test of that arrangement, and the arrangement failed in public. If the best-funded, most mission-driven version of private control can be overruled in a weekend, every smaller version carries the same weakness.

People underweight this because governance diagrams are clean and companies are not. A diagram has no scared employees, no tender offer, no Slack panic, and no partner with billions committed. It also has no founder-CEO who knows how to keep a room from accepting his absence. The legal chain of command said the board could fire Altman. The living chain said something else: employees who trusted him, investors who wanted him back, Microsoft's need for stability, and a press cycle that understood his side faster than it understood the board's.

A switch built on paper

OpenAI did not start as a company. It started in 2015 as a nonprofit research lab funded by donations from Elon Musk, Peter Thiel, and Altman himself, with a mission written in the language of existential risk. That year Altman wrote on his personal blog that "development of superhuman machine intelligence is probably the greatest threat to the continued existence of humanity."

Then training frontier AI turned out to cost billions, and in 2019 Altman designed the compromise that made him CEO. A nonprofit board would own and control a for-profit subsidiary. The for-profit could raise money and compete with Google, while the nonprofit upstairs held two guardrails: investor profits capped at 100x, and the power to fire the CEO. That second guardrail was the kill switch, and Altman talked about it constantly. His script never changed: "I don't have super voting shares," he said. "I don't want them. The board can fire me. I think that's important."

By November 2023, ChatGPT had become the fastest-growing tech product in history and OpenAI was about to be valued at $86 billion. The board that could fire its CEO was four people: a chief scientist, a think-tank researcher, a robotics founder, and the CEO of Quora.

The house became a government in exile

The Saturday after the firing, Altman's home in San Francisco turned into a war room. Dozens of people lined up inside, including OpenAI's senior executives. Lawyers set up an office outside his bedroom. Mira Murati was there, which was strange because she had just replaced him as interim CEO. Altman worked the phones for twelve hours a day. At 6 p.m., with the board ignoring a deadline to reinstate him and resign, he walked out to the living room and handed out Negronis. "You need to relax," he told people. "Whatever's going to happen is going to happen."

That night he sent one tweet: "I love the OpenAI team so much." Within minutes dozens of employees retweeted it with heart emojis. His advisers included Chris Lehane, a political operative whose motto is "everyone has a plan until you punch them in the mouth." Lehane wanted scorched earth. Altman played it differently, stepping back while allies fed sympathetic journalists just enough to shape the story. The board stayed silent because it could not say why it had fired him. Within twenty-four hours the press narrative had flipped from "Sam Altman was fired" to "what the hell was the board thinking?"

The memo they could never show

The board had a case, and the case was serious. A few weeks before the firing, a terrified Ilya Sutskever sent the other three directors a disappearing email containing a 70-page memo. The screenshots inside had been compiled covertly over months, with Slack messages photographed on personal phones to avoid company records. The first line read: "Sam exhibits a consistent pattern of lying, undermining his execs, and pitting his execs against one another." (The Verge, November 2025)

The memo documented a board that could not trust its main source of information about the company. Changes to GPT-4 had been described as safety-approved when no approval existed. A company lawyer was quoted as waiving review of a release, and the lawyer said he never said that. A $175 million venture fund deployed in OpenAI's name turned out to be personally owned by Altman, a fact that took months to clarify. When one director published an academic paper with mild criticism of OpenAI, Altman told Sutskever that another director agreed she should be removed. She had said no such thing. "I don't think Sam is the guy who should have his finger on the button," Ilya told a fellow board member.

The board could not show any of this. Revealing the evidence would expose Murati, the source of many screenshots, and put the directors at legal risk. So they fired the most famous CEO in tech with one vague phrase and total silence, and then discovered that silence is a weapon only the other side knows how to use. OpenAI's own review, conducted by the law firm WilmerHale, later found a "breakdown in trust" between the board and Altman. It said the board acted within its broad discretion and that Altman's conduct did not mandate removal. (OpenAI)

The company broke the switch in 72 hours

The blast radius was mostly money. OpenAI employees were days away from a tender offer at the $86 billion valuation, and for many of them those shares were the majority of their net worth. Some had already put deposits down on houses. Microsoft had been warned only minutes before the public announcement, and Satya Nadella was furious. (The Verge, November 2023)

Sunday night the board announced Emmett Shear, co-founder of Twitch, as the new CEO. Altman called it bad-faith negotiation and walked out. He had another move already lined up. Seven minutes before midnight, Nadella tweeted that Altman and Greg Brockman would join Microsoft to lead a new advanced AI research team, with a guaranteed job for any OpenAI employee who wanted to follow. (Microsoft newsroom, November 2023) One post was enough: staying with the board meant chaos and a dead tender offer, while following Altman meant continuity under a richer partner.

At 1 a.m. Monday, a letter began circulating on Signal. It accused the board of lacking the competence to oversee OpenAI, quoted a director saying that allowing the company to be destroyed "would be consistent with the mission," and demanded Altman and Brockman back. The first signature was Mira Murati's, the same woman whose screenshots had built the board's case. More than 700 of roughly 770 employees signed, and "OpenAI is nothing without its people" spread across the company's public feeds. (The New York Times, November 2023) One employee put it plainly: "Sam Altman is not the best CEO, but millions and millions of dollars and equity are at stake."

Ilya Sutskever signed too. Greg Brockman's wife Anna confronted him in tears in the office lobby that Sunday night, and Ilya had officiated her wedding. He blinked. The board conceded on Tuesday, and Sutskever, Toner, and McCauley were out. Bret Taylor and Larry Summers joined the board, and Altman was back as CEO. At the office party afterward, a smoke machine set off the fire alarm, and everyone kept partying. "If you come at the king, you best not miss," goes the line from The Wire. The board came at the king, missed, and the king was back in four days.

The weekend test

A brake only works if the person who pulls it can survive the weekend.

That is the rule the OpenAI weekend proved. The board had the legal authority, and its members had no payroll, no equity, and no army inside the company. They survived zero nights. Ilya Sutskever had spent a decade seeing AI before anyone else, pulled the switch himself, and then signed the letter demanding the man he fired come back. Even the puller broke. The current OpenAI structure has a safety committee that can delay a release until safety concerns are addressed. Its authority to delay a major release against intense internal pressure has not been publicly stress-tested in anything resembling the Altman weekend, and after 2023 everyone inside and outside the company knows what a test would cost.

Before you trust any brake, ask who survives pulling it. The compliance officer who flags the deal, the engineer who halts the launch, the regulator who revokes the license: if the answer is that nobody survives, the brake exists on paper only. Altman said it himself: "Although the board had the legal ability to fire me, in practice it didn't quite work. And that is its own kind of governance failure."

The machine rebuilt itself

In the two and a half years since, the structure has drifted toward what the board was supposed to prevent. The restructuring did not remove nonprofit control. On paper, the Foundation still appoints and can replace the OpenAI Group board. What changed is the economic structure: the old capped-profit model disappeared in favor of conventional equity. The deeper lesson from 2023 therefore survived the recapitalization: legal authority can remain intact while the cost of exercising it becomes enormous. Internally the whole episode is called "the blip." Several central figures in the 2023 governance conflict, including Sutskever and Murati, have since left. The company is now worth $852 billion and is functionally a normal tech company, except that a normal tech company was never built on the belief that it might be building a civilization-ending technology.

Altman told the New Yorker his "vibes don't really match a lot of the traditional AI safety stuff." Experts who once said AGI was decades away now say it could arrive within five years, and the people who tried to brake the company closest to it are gone. The 80,000 Hours researchers behind the source video draw the same conclusion: private self-regulation failed, and what replaces it has to live outside the companies. (80,000 Hours action page)

The fix is not a better board. The audit trail has to live outside the companies being audited: independent evaluations with teeth, incident reporting that survives executive discomfort, whistleblower protection, and public institutions that understand the technology before the next emergency. The OpenAI board was the best-case version of private control, and it did not survive the weekend. The next brake will face the same blast radius, and the people who pull it will still be inside the company. The worrying part of this story is not that the kill switch failed. It is that the switch worked, and the company around it refused the result.
