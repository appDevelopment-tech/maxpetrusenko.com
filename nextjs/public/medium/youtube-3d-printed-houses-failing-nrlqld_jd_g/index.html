The House Printer Arrived. The Homes Never Did.

Cairo, Illinois set out to print 30 duplexes. It got one unfinished shell, a $579,134 dispute, and an FBI investigation that has produced no charges.

Printing walls is the part of 3D-printed housing that demos best. What fails is everything a home needs after the walls go up: the money, the codes, the repairs, the insurance, and a buyer for the thirtieth copy. One unfinished duplex in Cairo, Illinois shows where that gap opens, and who holds the bill.

The first new house in 30 years

Outside a repair shop in Galatia, Illinois, the parts of a massive construction printer sat on a flatbed trailer for more than a year, weeds climbing the wheels. Two men from Harrisburg had borrowed $1.1 million from a small local bank to buy that machine, promising a housing revival in a corner of southern Illinois that had lost most of its jobs.

Their first stop was Cairo, a river town with fewer than 2,000 residents, most of them Black, and no new home built in at least 30 years. In August 2024, more than 100 people gathered at 17th Street and Dr. Martin Luther King Jr. Avenue to watch the printer lay the base of a promised duplex. Children clutched cotton candy and popcorn. Two American flags were clipped to the printer's steel legs while it hummed behind a chain-link fence. Kaneesha Mallory, who had grown up in town and come back, pressed against the fence to watch. She stayed in the August heat long enough to faint and leave by ambulance.

Connie Williams, a city council member, said leaders had warned the newcomers against overpromising. "We kept saying to them, 'Look, we've had enough people come through Cairo talking all this crazy stuff and then back out,'" she said. "They were just like, 'No, no, oh no, that's not us. We are here. God sent us.'"

Within a month, the walls went up, and then the work stopped. Dozens of cracks opened in the concrete, the owners said. The printer was hauled away, and a year later nobody had moved in. The machine printed the walls. But a home is everything after the walls, and everything after the walls is where projects like this keep falling down.

What the printer builds

The promise keeps arriving in places that cannot wait. More than 90 percent of Americans live in counties where median rents and house prices grew faster than median incomes from 2000 to 2020, and in 2023, 19.2 percent of adults aged 25 to 34 still lived with a parent.

A 3D construction printer forms walls. A nozzle on a gantry squeezes a cement mix in layers, following a digital plan, and the result is a shell with cavities for insulation and steel reinforcement. Walls can rise in days, which is where the biggest claims come from.

The catch is what the printer never touches. Windows, plumbing, electrical work, the roof, the foundation, and the finishes stay conventional work. The Texas Real Estate Research Center puts this conventional remainder at nearly 80 percent of total construction cost. Engineers on Eng-Tips put structural work under 30 percent of a project's cost. A veteran of the modular housing business put the printer's job bluntly: walls only.

The economics point the same way. Construction printers sell for $500,000 to $800,000, and the mix runs $800 to $900 a yard. Henrik Lund-Nielsen, founder and general manager of the Danish printer maker COBOD, says printer-based construction costs "equal to or a bit higher than conventional construction methods." "We run into problems basically every day," the chief technology officer of the printer company SQ4D said.

Roughly 140 printed homes stand worldwide, about 41 in North America, and one estimate counted fewer than 100 built in the United States in a year out of 1.5 million homes. Capital keeps arriving anyway: the most visible American name in the field, ICON, raised $56 million in 2025 about a month after laying off 114 people.

The wire

In October 2023, Grand Rivers Community Bank, in a town of 450 called Karnak, approved the $1.1 million printer loan, nearly double its single-customer limit, so a second regional bank joined the deal. That month the bank sent half the printer's price, $591,458.22, to PERI 3D Construction, and the money moved faster than the machine. Delivery was due six months out "at the earliest," and PERI commissioned the build from COBOD in Denmark. By spring 2024, the buyers, Jamie Hayes and Erik Burtis, tried to cancel. Their lawyer proposed PERI keep about $60,000 and return the rest. The April reply said none of the deposit would come back.

Prestige sued in Saline County, arguing that the document it signed was a "mock document" and never a real contract, the same document PERI later said made the deposit nonrefundable. Court records show a judge ruled for Prestige by default in August 2025 after PERI 3D did not respond, a ruling that settled no question of fault. The judgment is real, and collecting it is not. "I guess good luck trying to collect it," the judge said. In a county where poverty runs near 20 percent, the $579,134.25 that PERI kept was a lot of money.

The printer itself has a stranger ending. Hayes said the machine, the size of a small garage when assembled, was stored on his lot. When a reporter asked to see it, he seemed surprised, then led the way past rows of vehicles to the back lot. There was no printer, just heat shimmering off blacktop and a long chain-link fence. "I don't see it, do you?" he said, and shrugged. It had been there at one point, he said later. He did not realize it was gone.

Prestige owes about $13,000 a month on a ten-year loan, with more than $400,000 of interest over the term, and Hayes has been paying it out of another business account. The other half of the loan bought a replacement printer from Black Buffalo 3D, a New Jersey company that has sold only two printers in the United States since 2020. The cracks have their own trail: Prestige said it waited a year for a crack-remediation plan from its printer supplier that never came, and a promised return trip to fix them collapsed when the supplier's engineer said he could not sign off without city permits, which do not exist because they are not required. That machine sits in pieces too, and in December 2025 Black Buffalo filed for Chapter 11 protection in Delaware.

Cairo's side of the paperwork was thinner than its promises. The city sold the lot for $1, and the agreement for all thirty duplexes tied the buildout to "the City's ability to secure funding" that never materialized.

The official version

State senator Dale Fowler introduced the company to Governor JB Pritzker and to staff for Senator Tammy Duckworth, and his nonprofit secured $40,000 to stage the groundbreaking. Prestige and its sister firms gave Fowler $22,000 between May 2022 and August 2024, including a final $6,500 that arrived five days after the party, which Fowler and Burtis said was for co-sponsoring a golf fundraiser two months earlier. Months before the printer arrived, Fowler had pitched the project to a state commission on poverty: the machine was being "manufactured and assembled in Germany," the first house would be in Cairo, and the duplex would house two of the 230 families displaced when the town's public housing came down.

The praise outlived the construction by months. In December 2024, months into the stoppage and after six of the company's employees had quit, the office of Illinois comptroller Susana Mendoza posted that the dormant duplex was "a testament to resilience" and "a tangible symbol of hope and progress". The posts were deleted after questions. The FBI, meanwhile, opened an investigation led by an agent who handles white-collar and public corruption cases, and subpoenas went to the company, two school districts, and the city of Harrisburg over contracts unrelated to the duplex. No charges have been filed, no arrests made, and the owners deny wrongdoing.

Fowler, for his part, said he has taken unfair heat over the ordeal: "guilty by affiliation, I guess." He believes in his "spiritual soul" that he is the modern-day Daniel, thrown to the lions by his enemies and saved while they were "chomped, mutilated, by the lions." The story puts him "at peace," he said. "I've never told this to anyone," he added. "I've never told it to my wife."

"I've seen a lot of deals fall through," the city attorney, Rick Abell, said. "But we always knew why. Here, we got nothing."

The teardown

Iowa ran the same experiment a year earlier, with state money behind it. In March 2023, the Community Foundation of Greater Muscatine and a startup called Alquist 3D broke ground on a 1,300-square-foot house billed as Iowa's first 3D-printed home. By summer, the concrete was missing its 5,000 psi strength target, and in November 2023 the house was demolished before anyone finished it. Ron Monahan, the foundation's housing lead, later described "vertical cracking and some horizontal cracking, which was less of a concern."

The cause has never been established. "We're still trying to sort out whether it was because of climate," said Monahan. The heat index that summer hit 120 degrees, and the process is fussy in ways the brochure skips. Each new layer has to bond to the one below before it cures too far. Small shifts in temperature or humidity change how the mix sets, and an additive construction specialist named Stephan Mansour has described storing material in air-conditioned containers in Saudi Arabia. "There's a whole panic attack that's happening off camera to make sure this consistency comes in," he said. "Concrete cracks, and ours was cracking," Monahan said.

Iowa had real money in the game. About $3.2 million in state funds went into the Muscatine project and a printer for Iowa State University. Up to 70 printed homes were projected for the state by 2025. The Register found no evidence that any were completed as of its reporting in June 2024. Alquist relocated to Greeley, Colorado on more than $4 million in incentives, and its website never mentions Muscatine.

The rules

Codes and money have frameworks now, and both still treat each printed house as a question. The International Code Council's evaluation arm approved criteria for 3D concrete walls in December 2021, and the first compliance report went to Black Buffalo 3D, the same company that later supplied Cairo's replacement printer and then filed for bankruptcy. The report covers one printer and one ink under an alternative-means code path, so each jurisdiction still decides for itself. The first international quality-assurance requirements for printed construction arrived in December 2023, after Muscatine's walls had already gone up.

Fannie Mae's selling guide says a printed home with a traditional design and conventional materials is not "unique or nontraditional," so normal mortgage rules should apply, though appraisers must flag the house as a "3D Printed Home," and without evidence of market acceptance the property is not acceptable collateral. Practitioners put the chain bluntly: if the house cannot be insured, the mortgage never happens. A house that cannot be appraised, insured, or resold is a demonstration with a deed.

Cement production accounts for about 8 percent of global carbon dioxide emissions, and printed walls inherit concrete's problems, from hard-to-upgrade insulation to repairs that are hard to make. Failures arrive as cracks in a structure that is expensive to patch.

The exceptions

Two projects show the method working, and neither is a neighborhood. In Japan, the startup Kizuki and the builder ONOCOM completed a two-story printed reinforced concrete house in Miyagi Prefecture in November 2025, described by the printer maker as the first in the country to meet the government's seismic design requirements. It is tiny, 537 square feet across two floors, and the announcement and the approval both reach the public through the company's release and the trade press that reprinted it, so treat them accordingly. "Japan has some of the most demanding seismic requirements in the world," said Lund-Nielsen, and a government-approved printed house demonstrates the method is "ready for projects that rely on structural precision and consistent quality."

Denmark pushed on cost, by the printer maker's own numbers. A student housing project of 36 homes met the country's social-housing cost cap of roughly €3.1 million, about €1,880 per square meter, which the company says came in below ordinary construction costs with a profit left for the contractor. Three workers printed each 275-square-meter building in about five working days, using a low-carbon concrete mix. Both cases share the same supports: a demanding code regime and an experienced builder covering the parts the printer cannot. Denmark added a cost-capped public contract; Japan added a seismic standard few builders can meet.

The test

Before believing the next demo, apply a stricter test. Ask who finishes the house, who certifies it, who repairs it, who insures it, and who pays for the thirtieth copy.

In Cairo, the answers never arrived.

The duplex still stands unfinished, its cracks patched with hydraulic cement after questions started. Kaneesha Mallory applied for housing assistance in Chicago. The machine sits in pieces on a trailer, and the industry has moved on to the next groundbreaking.

The machines keep improving, and the promises have not changed at all.
