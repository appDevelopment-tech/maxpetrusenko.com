Ukraine Is Pulling Off Something Impossible in Crimea. Look at the Lights.

147 ships in eleven days, a highway turned into a graveyard, and a peninsula going dark: the campaign that is changing what Crimea costs Russia.

Ukraine cannot retake Crimea, and its commanders say so in public. So its drones are doing something else: closing the peninsula's three supply routes, sinking its coastal shipping, and darkening its power grid, until keeping Crimea costs more than it returns.

On February 9, Crimea glowed from orbit like a chandelier, a bright ring of cities around a dark sea. By July 19, the same satellite view was a sputtering, patchy dimness, as if someone were switching the peninsula off one district at a time (satellite analysis).

Ukraine cannot retake Crimea, and its commanders say so in public. But the campaign that darkened the peninsula runs on a different logic, and once you see it, the whole southern war rearranges itself: Ukraine is closing the three routes that keep Crimea alive, and it is doing that to make the peninsula unaffordable for the country that took it.

The catch is that this does not end the war by itself. Russia can absorb pain for a long time, Ukraine still cannot stage a landing, and the campaign's real test sits somewhere quieter than the front: in what Moscow decides the peninsula is worth paying. Whether pain converts into peace is the open question this summer began to answer.

The three roads in

Geography rules this war, and Crimea hangs off southern Ukraine on a thin neck of land, where everything Russia needs arrives by one of three paths. The Kerch Strait bridge carries cars and trains; heavy freight was ruled out after 2022, when a truck bomb dropped a span and the crossing reopened under weight limits that banned fuel trucks and armor. So the tonnage moved to the sea, where ferries, cargo ships, and tankers run from Rostov-on-Don across the Sea of Azov. And it moved to the road, the highway Moscow calls Novorossiya, which translates to New Russia and runs roughly 390 miles from Rostov to the peninsula (NBC News).

By this spring the corridor felt solved. The three routes were stitched together with air defense: missile batteries and radar nets, including small modular radars bolted to towers and roofs, the kind that are cheap and hard to replace. Anything Ukrainian trying to cross flew into a web that stretched from the coast to the peninsula's spine. When Ukraine's drone commander said in June that the push in Crimea was just getting started, it sounded like a boast (PBS NewsHour). By midsummer it read like a schedule.

Punch a hole

The way through the network was to hunt its eyes. A radar net only works while the whole mesh reports; knock out every set covering one corridor, and for a few days that corridor is a door. Ukrainian units began prioritizing radars over launchers and striking in waves, flooding a segment with decoys and strike drones, blinding it, then running payloads through the gap before replacements arrived. When Ukrainian drones swarmed a frontline airfield and destroyed the Russian drones parked there, Moscow refilled the losses within a week. The damage that lasted was the window that followed: with the area's small radars hunted down, the local air defense went dark for days (strike imagery). Through June and July the hunts concentrated on two corridors, the northern approaches that let longer-range drones reach Moscow, and the long stretch of road from Rostov down to Crimea.

Tactics iterate here because the machine behind them iterates. Ukraine's drone units buy equipment themselves through an online marketplace, spending a points budget that scales with their results; engineers sit with the crews; a fix requested in the morning can be on a drone by the weekend. More than 400 units order through the system, which pushed through more than half a million drones in its first year (analysis), and the defense ministry describes the whole arrangement as a market where the fighting units are the customers (ministry). Armies are not supposed to work this way; procurement is normally a decade-long argument. Ukraine turned it into a shopping app, and the drone war moves faster for it.

None of this is one-sided. Russia has centralized its drone effort under a single command and fielded fiber-optic-guided machines that fly past jamming to hunt Ukrainian supply trucks the same way (analysis).

The highway

Route one to close was the road, and through June Ukrainian drones and missiles worked down the Novorossiya highway, burning trucks and tankers and pulverizing the spans where the road meets Crimea. The commander of Ukraine's drone forces told Reuters his units were striking as many as twenty vehicles a day, and that traffic on the highway had fallen by more than two thirds (Reuters). Western coverage nicknamed it the highway of death (Guardian); the occupation administration closed it to civilian traffic (closure order); and pilots began parking their machines on the shoulder to ambush convoys. A road under that threat does not stay busy for long.

Rebuild, strike, rebuild: repair crews and drone crews are locked in the same loop, and every repaired crossing comes back smaller than the one it replaced, a pontoon here and an earth embankment there, nothing that can carry the tonnage the corridor needs.

147 ships

Route two was the sea, and it took less than two weeks to close. For most of the war the Azov corridor ran behind the Russian fleet, ferrying cargo far from the front. Hundreds of ferries, cargo ships, and tankers turned a truck artery into a sea bridge, a reverse Dunkirk in which the emergency convoy's job was to keep the garrison in place. Then the drone units that had been hunting trucks turned on the ships. In eleven days in July, the drone forces say they hit 147 vessels, tankers among them, a claim maritime trade coverage relays while noting it cannot be independently verified (gCaptain, drone forces).

Independent tracking fills in the rest. On June 30, the Sea of Azov held 267 vessels broadcasting their positions; by July 11 the number was 120, and the survivors were clustered in port, unwilling to run the gauntlet (ISW). Russia suspended shipping in the basin entirely (Guardian). A sea that had been a supply line became a parking lot, and a parking lot is a list of targets.

Lights out

Route three was the peninsula itself, and here the campaign stopped looking like a military operation. Ukraine began hitting what Crimea runs on: oil depots, substations, the power plants at the end of the wires. On June 21, the occupation government stopped selling gasoline and diesel to private buyers outright (ISW); fuel moved to rationing by coupon, roughly twenty liters a week per car, and blackouts rolled through districts that had gone four years of war without them, while the summer tourist season that sustains the peninsula's economy shrank toward nothing (Moscow Times). Satellite imagery through July showed illumination falling toward a fifth of normal, and by mid-July families were queueing to leave across the one Kerch span that still worked (Meduza).

Ukraine let them. The drone forces deliberately left the big bridge mostly intact so that Russians could leave through it (Nasha Niva), a call that matched military intelligence's conclusion that the crossing no longer carried its old weight (Kyiv Post). A former deputy head of Ukraine's security service described the purpose plainly: the goal is to make Russia's hold "as difficult and expensive as possible," to grind down the logistics of occupation until the peninsula's military usefulness runs out (Odesa Journal).

What it is for

It helps to see what Crimea is for Russia. Vladimir Putin has called the peninsula an unsinkable aircraft carrier whose acquisition would help secure Russia's other lost territory (speech); in practice it is the depot, airbase, and rail hub behind Russia's southern front, wrapped in twelve years of propaganda about a conquest made permanent. The drone campaign has one large military aim: to make those bases unusable, and to sever the staging ground from the war it feeds.

The first payoff is local: the rail yards and depots in Crimea are part of the supply chain behind Russia's grinding pushes in the Donbas, where the siege of the fortress belt has stalled (Foreign Policy). Ukraine's drone commander describes the goal as depriving Moscow of Crimea as a springboard, and says the isolation is coming "in the near future" (Reuters).

The larger aim is about the end of the war, whenever it comes. Most wars finish at a table, and both sides are fighting now to decide what sits on the map when they get there. Russia spent a decade making Crimea irreversible, and much of Western diplomacy priced in that permanence; a peninsula that can be darkened, emptied, and left without fuel reads differently. It becomes contested again, in the oldest sense, a territory whose ownership is an open question. Volodymyr Zelensky put the bet in one line this summer: creating the conditions that force Russia to choose peace (speech).

The limits deserve their place. Ukraine has no force that could storm the peninsula and no plan to try (NPR); Russia has absorbed worse than fuel coupons and kept fighting (Soufan Center); and Moscow's answer to the drone campaign has been more drones, including strikes on Ukraine's own ports and a warning about opening a Pandora's box (Reuters). A campaign like this is real pressure, and pressure spends slowly.

What the summer settled is narrow, and still worth marking. Ukraine has not retaken a kilometer of the peninsula. It has changed what the peninsula is worth, from a prize that was Russia's permanently to a property that has to be paid for every month, in fuel, in troops, in the price of pretending that nothing is wrong at a Black Sea resort whose lights are going out.

The bidding is not over, and pain alone has never signed a treaty. But a country that can reach across the water and switch a region dark knows something its opponent spent a decade hoping nobody would notice. A place that can be switched off was never quite owned at all.
