The Simple Question Socialism Couldn't Answer

Two Berlin grocery stores, twenty minutes apart, ran the century's clearest experiment. The shelves kept the score.

Two grocery stores stood twenty minutes apart in the same city for four decades. One carried about 3,000 items; the other carried 7,000, and the biggest markets climbed toward 15,000. That distance settled more of the twentieth century than any army did. It measured which system could hear that it was wrong, and how fast the shelves could answer back.

One account of the period puts the state grocery on the east side of Berlin at about 3,000 items, with mustard in exactly one variety. On the west side, a market in a chain of a hundred and twenty carried close to 7,000, with competing chains chasing the same shoppers, and the largest stores in town pushed toward 15,000.

Most of what the twentieth century fought over reduces to one practical question: who decides what gets made, and for whom. Armies, treaties, and walls were downstream of it. A second question hides inside the first. Who finds out when the answer was wrong, and how fast the news travels back. The clearest answers came from shelves.

The question sounds small, yet answering it from a single office means knowing what a million households will want next Tuesday; a market discovers that one purchase at a time, and the till carries the news back. The century ran both approaches side by side in one city, and the verdict had little to do with armies.

The question underneath the century

Every economy starts with the same raw material: scarcity. For most of history, the fight was over land, and the winners handed it down. Feudalism worked because a lord could defend a border, and it kept working until trade made land worth more as an asset than as a fort. Enclosure fences followed, the commons disappeared, and the people who had lived off that land walked to town with only their hours to sell.

Factory life in the nineteenth century was brutal enough that a moral revolt was inevitable. The complaint at the center of it was simple and correct: the people who made the wealth should share in it.

A middle-class kid from what is now Germany gave the complaint its most durable book. Marx never stood at a factory gate; he spent decades in poverty, pawning his coat some winters, burying three children young. His friend and coauthor Friedrich Engels, whose family owned cotton mills, kept money coming. The work that would arm revolutions against factory owners was funded by factory profits. When Marx published Capital in 1867, almost nobody noticed; when he died in 1883, eleven people came to the funeral.

Capital is a diagnosis, and its core formula fit the misery: profit is unpaid labor. Workers create more value than their wage, and the difference flows upward, which Marx called "an exact expression for the degree of exploitation of labour-power by capital" (Capital, Vol. I, ch. 9). Where the book goes quiet is production. It never says how the morning shift at a bakery should decide how many loaves to bake. What to make was left to the people who came after him, and that open question is where their systems broke.

A vote you cast with five dollars

Thousands of people walk through a grocery aisle each day, and each one, on paying, tells every producer in the chain something precise: against everything else those five dollars could buy, I want this.

Add up millions of those verdicts and you get prices, and prices move the whole system without a central instruction. Friedrich Hayek's 1945 essay argued that the knowledge required for an economy never exists in concentrated form; it exists as dispersed, incomplete, contradictory bits, and the price system coordinates it "without an order being issued" (The Use of Knowledge in Society). Ludwig von Mises had pushed the claim further in 1920: without exchange and prices, no objective basis for calculation exists at all (Economic Calculation in the Socialist Commonwealth).

Both claims sound like ideology until you restate them as engineering. One design keeps a live signal running between the edge of the system and the center, cheaply and in both directions. The other runs it one way.

The plan, from Moscow to the bread truck

The replacement was designed as a single pipe. At the top, a committee of eleven men set priorities for a five-year span. Those priorities became a plan of hundreds of pages, and a state commission of about 3,000 people broke it into some 2,000 commodities balanced in a ledger: so much grain becomes so much flour becomes so many loaves.

People still needed wages, so the planners built a scale of eight pay bands. Then came the Office of Prices, whose job was to make the arithmetic close. A loaf of bread should cost a family no more than about one percent of the lowest wage, and prices were stamped on the goods before they left the factory. From there, quotas went out to fifteen districts, flour rode a truck to a bakery, and the bread rode another truck to the store.

The manager of the store unloads the truck with whatever arrives.

Every step in that chain moves information downward. None of it moves up. There is no channel for the message that nobody bought the chocolate bars, because the chocolate bars were the plan.

The shelf could never say no

A plan does not fail loudly. It freezes. The east side of Berlin hovered at about 3,000 items while the west side ran to 10,000 and the biggest markets approached 15,000. Materials that went into unpopular goods stayed committed to them, because no price existed to bid them away. A factory that missed its quota made more of the thing it was stuck making.

The car told the story in steel. One model, the Trabant, barely changed across three decades, and buyers waited up to a decade or more for the privilege; a used one cost more than a new one. A country of about sixteen million people made roughly three million of them in the same decades the west filled its roads with dozens of models available the day you walked in (DW).

People answered in the only currency left to them: the exit. When the Soviets blockaded West Berlin in 1948, the West kept the city alive from the air for eleven months, roughly a quarter of a million flights feeding two and a half million people (U.S. Department of State). After that, the seam stayed open, and millions streamed through it. More than 2.6 million left for the west before the wall (DDR Museum); through Berlin alone, the outflow ran 200,000 to 300,000 per year, passing a million since 1949, from a country of fewer than sixteen million people. The exits skewed young, and one account of the exodus puts the physician loss near a quarter. As one official history of the crisis put it, East Germany was depopulating itself, and the wall's construction was "an admission of defeat" by the leadership that built it (CIA, Bitter Measures).

Two months before the wall went up, Walter Ulbricht told reporters that no one had any intention of building a wall. The wall went up in one night in August 1961, first as barbed wire, then concrete. Every guard tower and dog run along its length faced inward. A barrier that faces inward is a system admitting, in concrete, that its shelves could not compete. And the split showed up in bodies as well as shopping: east German life expectancy ran two and a half to three years behind the west's, a gap that collapsed for women after reunification but, for men, narrowed and persisted (Eur J Popul, Demography).

The second failure is power

Bad information is half the story. The other half is what the system did to anyone who might have fixed it.

Work the incentives through the chain and the behavior stops looking like incompetence. The store manager loses nothing when the store runs dry by ten in the morning; fewer customers means less work. A bakery asked to hit a bread quota in dense loaves has no reason to attempt croissants, which take about five times as long to make. A truck driver paid by weight hauls bricks before bread, because bricks fill the quota faster. The commission that wrote the quotas wants them reported as met.

Upward, every incentive inverts. Bad news threatens whoever carries it, since the person most likely to deliver it is also the person most likely to be blamed for it. The official at the center hears that everything is going well, and has spent a career learning to treat contrary reports as disloyalty. By the top of the pipe, the plan and the country are describing different places.

Stalin ruled for twenty-nine years, Mao for twenty-seven, Castro for forty-nine. A center that owns everything ends up holding both the guns and the factories, and one that cannot be voted out hears mostly good news.

The deeper problem with a command economy is that geometry. Better arithmetic does not touch it, and the periodic revival of the idea keeps hitting the same wall. Even socialists who defend planning as a project describe the calculation and incentive problems as hardwired into it; in one recent accounting, "even supercomputers won't solve the problems inherent to central planning" (Jacobin).

What the plan actually built

The best case for the planned economy is real. The shelf question is what it does not answer. Starting from a peasant economy wrecked by war, the command system built steel, power, and literacy at a pace that markets had not managed in Russia, and one economic historian argues that without the five-year plans, the country's fate "would have been somewhere between India's and Argentina's" (EH.NET). Consumption per head rose by about 30 percent between 1928 and 1937, and the book makes a larger claim, that the USSR was one of the twentieth century's most successful developing economies (Princeton University Press).

That record is contested, and it measures a climb from a low base with immense human cost. It also points at why the same system starved the shelves. Steel and power were targets the planners could name a decade ahead. What belongs on a grocery shelf changes every week, and only the shoppers know the answer. Command economies could industrialize a country, and they could not learn what to stock a store with.

Part of the argument is about the word itself. The Leninist state stands in what Noam Chomsky called "the relation of contradiction" to socialism (The Soviet Union Versus Socialism). The eastern bloc's own framing was kinder: socialism as a waypoint, communism as the next stage. That stage never arrived. Ask ten people today what the word means and you will get ten answers, which is how the policy dispute hides inside a definition.

Denmark is the case everyone reaches for. It collects about 45 percent of GDP in taxes, the highest share among the OECD's thirty-eight members (OECD Revenue Statistics). That figure covers the whole economy; for the average single worker, the total wedge withheld from a paycheck runs about 36 percent (OECD Taxing Wages). Its grocery stores are private chains setting their own prices, and its employers hire and fire at will. The taxes are high and the markets are ordinary, and calling it socialism keeps collapsing into a fight about definitions (Foreign Policy).

The test for every machine that decides

Strip the century down and the shelf question becomes a reusable audit for any system that decides something: a company, a product, a feed. Three questions survive contact with the history.

  • Count the items. The variety a system offers is a receipt from its feedback loop; a shelf with one kind of mustard has stopped listening.
  • Find the loop. Name the person who learns that something failed, and name what happens to that person next. If the news dies with them, the loop is decoration.
  • Keep the decision near the consequence. Distance is the variable that kills systems slowly: every degree of separation between the chooser and the affected adds delay, and delays compound.

Cheap computation is real, and it will keep making planning better at logistics. It cannot make reporting bad news safe, or make a central office want to hear that its plan was wrong. Those are power problems wearing math as a costume, and they do not shrink when the spreadsheet gets faster.

The wall came down in 1989, and the two grocery stores became one city's ordinary story. The question they were built around did not go away. Every day, in every market, someone answers it with a cart, and the systems that last are the ones still able to read the answer. The machine that decides what gets made can be an office in Moscow or an algorithm in a data center; the test is the same, and it is being graded this morning, one aisle at a time.
