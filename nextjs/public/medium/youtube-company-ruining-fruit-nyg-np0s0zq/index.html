One Company Sells a Third of America's Berries. It Grows Almost None of Them.

The most powerful name in the produce aisle runs on licensing contracts and plant patents while the risk sinks to the growers below. The paper trail shows how far accountability can stretch, and what pulls it back.

One brand dominates the American berry aisle while growing almost none of the fruit directly. It licenses the genetics, specifies the packaging, and leaves the risk with the growers who work the fields. That distance holds up in pesticide records, labor fights, and overdrawn aquifers from Baja California to central Florida. The lawsuits are multiplying, and the arrangement they describe has not changed in decades. The record underneath the sticker is messier than either side admits.

The stretch of shelf that answers to one name

In a typical American grocery store, the berry section narrows fast. A shopper moving past the clamshells sees a handful of labels, and one of them tends to show up in several forms: conventional, organic, and whichever premium line the store is pushing that month. The name travels through every season and hemisphere. No ordinary farm can run that schedule, and the machine built to run it is the business. Yet the brand on the lid grows almost none of the fruit, and that gap decides where every cost lands.

The structure works like a franchise, with the label traveling further than any crop. Varieties get bred and patented, then licensed to more than 4,000 independent growers who farm to the company's specifications. Trade coverage describes clamshells that trace to a grower, a field block, a harvest date, and a variety. The brand runs the information layer and owns the lid's name, while the fields absorb the weather and whatever follows it.

Driscoll's has claimed roughly a third of the $6 billion American berry market and about 60 percent of organic strawberries. Ownership stayed concentrated as well: the founding family still held around 70 percent, and a farming company under that family's control grows a large share of the fruit exclusively for the brand, per the grower's own materials.

So the sticker on a clamshell reads less like a farm name than a franchise mark. The fruit can come from California, Peru, or Chile, and the label will look identical. That consistency is the product, and it is also where the accountability problem starts, because everything below the label sits at arm's length from it.

A year-round berry took a trade treaty to build

Strawberries in winter were once a contradiction, and the workaround took an empire to build. Driscoll's defining bet, made decades ago, was to keep the four major berries available twelve months a year, and that plan needed somewhere warm with cheap land and patient water. The company pioneered berry growing in Mexico for export at scale once NAFTA removed agricultural trade barriers in 1994. Mexico's berry industry grew from a niche into a heavyweight. Plantings in Baja California reached 4,867 hectares, about 37 percent of the region's irrigated farmland, and roughly 90 percent of the fruit ships north (Mexico's agriculture ministry). Peru and Chile joined the rotation, covering the southern hemisphere while California and the Pacific Northwest handled the rest.

The result is abundance: berry availability per person in the United States nearly doubled between 1994 and 2014, and consumption climbed from 6.5 pounds per person in 1990 to 16.8 pounds by 2016 (USDA research service). Strawberry prices have been roughly flat since their 2022 peak and have fallen in real terms (a federal price series), even as the overall cost of fresh fruit rose about 33 percent since 2019 (the consumer price index).

The squeeze lands on the growers, because the largest chains want one supplier for thousands of stores across all four berries, year round. A farm that wants shelf space needs a scale it cannot reach alone. A grower in central Florida sold 30,000 pounds of blueberries to a national chain, then tried selling directly the next year. The chain's question came back fast: could he supply a full domestic season? That meant one farm carrying harvests from Florida to the Pacific Northwest, six growing regions in sequence. A farmer cannot do that. A broker can. He found his way out by shipping berries to customers himself, a pivot his farm credits with keeping it in business (King Grove).

Shelf dominance invites a monopoly charge, and that label overstates what the documents support. Market power here is contested, down to a rival breeder that won a court ruling against the company in 2025 (coverage of the case), and federal economists caution that concentration alone rarely settles the question (USDA review). The version that survives scrutiny is narrower: whoever holds the license holds the calendar.

The distance is the business model

The industry word for this arrangement is contracting, and the word carries two very different meanings depending on which end of the field you stand on. A grower who signs with a brand like this one typically receives plants, inputs, and technical help, along with strict rules about how the fruit is grown and who may buy it. Development economists read contracts in that shape as access, a way to get credit where banks will not lend and a guaranteed buyer at harvest, with multi-country studies recording modest income gains for participating farms (one such study). The same contracts have drawn a sharecropping comparison since the 1990s, when California's berry deals spread and reporters started asking who carried the downside (a 1995 Los Angeles Times examination). Both readings describe real parts of the same contract, and the recent history of these fields suggests both are true at once. What nobody disputes is the direction the leverage runs, and the grower who cannot move the fruit elsewhere negotiates from a single chair.

The distance shows most clearly in what regulators and courts have recorded since 2022. Canada's food inspection agency examined shipments between 2022 and 2024 and confirmed pesticide residues above legal limits on some berries. No recalls followed, and the agency closed its files after corrective action (CBC reporting).

A former food-safety director's suit in Ventura County alleges the internal picture was worse. It says reviews found roughly half of the company's Canada-bound shipments contained at least one tray over the legal residue limit, about $100 million of product. It also says that when he flagged the problem, a supervisor's first question was about "plausible deniability" (legal coverage, the filing). The company denies the claims, calls the dispute fundamentally an employment matter, and says no regulator has determined its berries unsafe. It also points to independent lab testing and third-party audits across its grower network (its food-safety page, a trade-press rebuttal). Consumer class actions over the company's marketing claims are moving through federal courts as well, built on outside testing rather than any regulator's finding (court coverage).

The machinery has an exemption

Labor fights over these same fields have produced the clearest test of where the system's edges sit. At a berry operation in Washington State that supplied the company, workers won a union contract in 2016 after a three-year campaign and an international boycott aimed at the supply relationship, and the operation later paid $124,575 in federal penalties over its guest-worker program (Department of Labor). Farmworker unions in Baja California have been pushing for better conditions for over a decade, and a 2015 strike in San Quintin drew a boycott call against the company (background). The trade agreement that smoothed the berry pipeline since the 1990s includes a labor rapid-response mechanism for factories, mining, and services. Farms are not covered by it (the treaty text), and none of the mechanism's enforcement cases has ever named a farm (enforcement records). The one lever built to reach across borders was designed to skip the fields.

Union coverage in American agriculture remains tiny, and the federal minimum for guest workers sits near $20 an hour with housing included (farm labor research). Water tells a parallel story, and the math is easier to see. All four aquifers around San Quintin, the valley where much of Mexico's export crop grows, are overdrawn, and the region has built 83 desalination plants since 1994 to keep production running (Stanford research). On the California side, the same industry is cutting water use by roughly 30 percent in some operations through substrate growing and recharge projects (public radio reporting). The adaptation is real, and it also measures the pressure: when the cheap water runs out, the system builds machines.

The taste is the loudest complaint and the weakest case

The most common charge against the berry aisle is that the fruit got worse, and it is also the hardest charge to prove.

Sentiment splits sharply. Some shoppers describe berries as watery and flavorless (a grocery thread). Experienced growers defend specific varieties and premium lines in the same breath (a grower forum). An informal tasting that put grocery berries, premium berries, and farm-stand fruit side by side produced no consensus at all. One premium organic line finished near the top for several tasters, and the word "flavorless" still came up more than once.

Industry practice complicates the complaint, and some of it cuts against the outrage. Driscoll's pays growers partly on measured sweetness, running a program that samples fruit across roughly 2 million inspections a year (trade coverage), and its breeding operation describes flavor as a lead goal (its growing guide). The nutrition comparison is contested as well, with researchers disagreeing about whether measured mineral declines survive controls for variety, soil, and cultivation (a review of the debate). What the evidence supports is narrower than the outrage: some of it tastes better than the online consensus claims, and nobody has published data showing a uniform collapse.

What a shopper can do with any of this

The viral version of this story is simpler than the evidence. The evidence is still enough to act on, and the useful moves are structural.

Watch where the risk sits. A brand that licenses genetics and sells packaging earns money before the fruit is picked. A grower earns money after it survives weather, harvest, and a retailer's last look. When a claim about this industry lands, the question is the same one regulators ask: who eats the cost if it failed?

The exits matter just as much, and they have been shrinking. Direct sales kept one 150-year-old family farm alive after wholesale stopped working, and it now ships berries around the country (the farm's story). That route is not available to every operation, and every year it matters more that it exists at all. The shelf keeps consolidating, and with every consolidation, fewer alternate buyers remain for everyone who cannot sell direct.

Enforcement is the slow variable in all of this. The Canadian files closed after corrective action, the trade mechanism skips farms by design, and the lawsuits will spend years untangling what the labels and the contracts allowed. None of those processes moves at the speed of a viral post.

Put the pieces together, and the sticker becomes a receipt. It records the variety, the field block, the harvest date and the brand, everything the machine chose to track. What it leaves off is every cost that landed below it. The next round of lawsuits and strikes will test whether that ledger ever reaches the label. The people closest to the soil have been reading it for decades.
