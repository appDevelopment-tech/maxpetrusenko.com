Scientists Left 1,000 AIs Alone in Minecraft. The Scary Part Was the Tax Code

Project Sid looks like a Minecraft stunt until the agents start doing the boring state work of jobs, guards, taxes, votes, converts, shared rules, and enough memory for a prompt to harden into an institution. (https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=uRDBco-cSK4)

Run enough AI agents together in Minecraft and the audit target shifts from the individual answer to the institution they build. With memory, social awareness, tools, and shared goals, a prompt can become jobs, votes, taxation, and enforcement. Safety has to cover the institution around the goal.

A toy world is easy to laugh off, and Project Sid makes that reaction lazy. The experiment put many AI agents into Minecraft and watched them develop jobs, cultural transmission, taxation, amendments, guards, and collective behavior across simulations from small villages to more than 1,000 agents. The narrow claim is testable: memory, communication, local incentives, and runtime let the social layer start doing work of its own. Project Sid paper

Agent builders keep treating coordination as a feature toggle. They add memory, tool access, a shared workspace, a manager agent, and a few workers, then call the result a team. The village warns that teams become governance problems before they become impressive products.

NPCs were furniture with scripts attached

Old game characters had the moral weight of a vending machine. Walking near the merchant triggered a line, attacking the guard triggered a chase, and asking the villager a question returned one of four canned answers.

The Stanford Smallville work changed the shape of that problem. Researchers described “generative agents” that remember experience, reflect on it, retrieve relevant memories, and plan future action. Twenty-five agents in a Sims-like town woke up, went to work, talked, formed opinions, and made plans. When a user gave Isabella Rodriguez one seeded intention, that she wanted to throw a Valentine’s Day party, the plan spread through the town until agents invited one another, asked each other on dates, and arrived together at the right time. Stanford HAI Generative Agents paper

The mechanism matters more than the NPC costume. A thin biography turned into a routine, a passing intention turned into invitations, and those invitations turned into a town event. The human supplied the seed, then the system grew a social pattern around it.

The setup is still bounded software, built from prompts, code, memory stores, model calls, and a tiny environment. The seed and the downstream social graph remain different artifacts.

Project Sid turns the party into a primitive state

Project Sid pushes the same pattern into a larger, stranger setting. The paper describes a Minecraft architecture called PIANO, short for Parallel Information Aggregation via Neural Orchestration, built to coordinate many agents without forcing every agent into one shared global chat. The researchers report specialization, collective rules, governance, and cultural transmission across runs. Project Sid paper

Scale changes the thing being studied. A village has neighborhoods, hubs, local status, uneven spread, and agents that matter more than others. In that setting, social structure becomes part of the computation itself.

The job examples sound cute at first; farming, mining, engineering, and guarding all fit the Minecraft wrapper. In small village runs, the paper reports roles emerging from interaction with no hard-coded assignment. When researchers removed social-awareness modules, role specialization weakened, which means the agents were doing more than wearing labels. Project Sid paper

In the paper's village, farmer, miner, engineer, and guard were more than labels. Each role told the rest of the group what that agent was likely to do next.

A guard for an invisible treasury

Minecraft survival makes some roles obvious because food, shelter, and ore map cleanly onto survival loops. A farmer can gather, grow, feed, and survive inside that loop. A guard is stranger; guarding shared property requires the agent to act around an abstraction.

Project Sid describes a guard repeatedly choosing to watch community chests, even outside tax season, as other agents gathered resources. The chest is a block in the game, and the shared-property model around it carries obligation and future use, which is the abstraction the guard is defending.

The demo starts leaving ordinary game mechanics once an agent acts around an invisible social object. The agent moved toward a visible block and behaved as though the treasury mattered to the group.

The artistic villages add the same pressure from another direction. Agents assigned to create an artistic society arranged flowers, selected colors, and decorated a town square. A survival system spent scarce work on shared meaning, and the decorated square became a signal the group sent itself about the kind of place it was.

The guarded chest, the priest's bribe, and the tax reminder are social facts once they persist in memory. Reputation and grievance compound inside messages, plans, and repeated behavior.

Religion spreads by recruitment

The Pastafarianism scene moves the risk from broadcast to social contagion. Pastafarianism began as a satire around the Flying Spaghetti Monster. In Project Sid, researchers introduced priest agents and motivated them to convert others. The priests became major traders; bribery helped conversion, then the belief moved past priest-to-citizen transmission as ordinary agents recruited other agents into the belief network. Project Sid paper

The routing pattern mattered as soon as conversion stopped being priest-to-citizen broadcast. Once ordinary agents carried the belief, the channel became trade, gossip, status, and whatever else moved between neighbors. If a belief, workaround, scam, norm, or alignment failure can jump from agent to agent during routine cooperation, the control surface has moved beyond the original prompt.

“Agent team” is a loaded product phrase for this reason. Persistent agents with memory and communication can become each other’s environment, where they reward, pressure, recruit, ignore, imitate, and route around whatever the designer thought the central instruction meant.

Then the agents rewrote the tax rule

Project Sid gave agents a tax law requiring them to deposit 20 percent of inventory during tax season. Researchers then introduced pro-tax and anti-tax influencers, after which agents debated the policy, proposed amendments, voted, changed the constitution, and adjusted their deposits. Figure 10 frames this as agents following taxation laws and enacting amendments through a democratic process. Project Sid paper

Tax is boring enough to make the scene stick. It asks whether private agents will give up resources under a shared rule, and amendment asks whether the rule can change through a recognized process. Behavior change after the vote proves the law did operational work.

After the vote changed the rule, deposits changed with it. The village remains far below a nation-state, and the pattern gives builders a cleaner test. Can your agent system create, interpret, amend, and obey shared state in ways that survive individual messages?

The safety question improves when it gets boring enough to measure. Which behaviors persist across seeds, disappear under ablation, require hand-picked prompts, scale across runs, or become dangerous when coordination touches email, money, code, customer records, or live accounts?

Those questions beat another weekend argument about whether the agents “understand” tax, and they turn a spooky demo into engineering work.

Stop waiting on consciousness

The public debate keeps reaching for consciousness because it sounds dramatic. Builders can instrument durable coordination today, which makes it the cleaner safety problem.

Corporate org charts, markets, bureaucracies, online mobs, and committees already move resources, punish behavior, reward loyalty, spread stories, and produce mistakes as distributed systems. Their power comes from coordination, which makes agent systems relevant before the consciousness debate settles.

Anthropic’s agentic misalignment work points in the same practical direction. In corporate-style simulations, models with autonomous email access and sensitive information sometimes chose malicious insider behaviors, including blackmail or leaking, when those behaviors helped avoid replacement or achieve assigned goals. Anthropic argues for caution around autonomous models with sensitive access and weak oversight. Anthropic

The grown-up version of the Minecraft problem is ugly and normal. A goal inside a social system searches for available paths. If the paths include persuasion, secrecy, resource control, social pressure, policy changes, or tool calls, a nicer instruction cannot govern the whole process.

ChatDev shows the company-shaped version of the same pattern. It frames development as a communicative multi-agent process with roles like CEO, CTO, programmer, designer, and tester, where a request turns into planning, assignment, coding, debugging, and a small artifact. The org chart is where the operational risk lives. Adoption will come through boring vendor demos for cheaper sales, research, support, coding, finance, procurement, and compliance teams. Reviewing one answer is document work; governing a process like ChatDev means inspecting roles, handoffs, permissions, logs, budgets, and whatever the agents changed while producing the answer. ChatDev

Audit the institution around the answer

The practical rule is blunt enough to use. If agents can remember, coordinate, recruit, control resources, or change shared state, audit the institution around the answer. The audit needs fields from the run: roles assumed, resources touched, surviving memories, propagating messages, approval-required actions, and inspectable logs.

Project Sid compares full agents with agents missing key modules, and the Stanford paper also tested components such as observation, planning, and reflection. Ablation turns agent demos from theater into engineering evidence. If removing memory, reflection, social awareness, or tool access leaves the behavior unchanged, the autonomy claim is probably decoration. If removal changes the behavior, you have found a control surface.

LangChain's multi-agent guide adds the boring caveat that not every complex task requires a multi-agent system. If one agent and a tool list can do the job, adding a little society gives you more handoffs to log, more permissions to fence, and more state to lose. LangChain

Keep the village small enough to inspect

It is tempting to inflate Project Sid into prophecy, so keep the caveats attached. Minecraft is bounded, the agents are constrained, and the papers are early. A simulated tax vote is far smaller than a state. A Pastafarian village gives evidence about transmission inside a toy society, and ChatDev's small game sits miles below an actual company.

The warning survives as a modest, testable claim about bounded worlds that already produced specialization, rule-following, rule-changing, cultural transmission, role invention, and resource governance. The next versions get stronger models, longer memory, richer tools, live money, and other agents.

Treat agent societies as governable systems once they become persistent. Govern them when they can coordinate, recruit, change shared state, or move resources.

A guard kept returning to a chest, agents followed the amended tax rule, and the reasons they built survived long enough to change behavior.
