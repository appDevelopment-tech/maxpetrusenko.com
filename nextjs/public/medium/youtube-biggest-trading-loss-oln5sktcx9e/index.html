Where an AI Story Becomes Cash Flow

A brilliant thesis, four times leverage, and one margin call.

A 24-year-old former OpenAI researcher grew the hedge fund Situational Awareness LP from about $225 million to a reported $45 billion in under two years on an AI thesis, running roughly four times leverage. In July 2026 an AI rout triggered margin calls, and on July 30 the fund sold nearly its whole public book to Citadel. Being right about AI did not save it. The damage came from how the bet was built.

Leopold Aschenbrenner was 24 and had the kind of résumé that turns into a fund. He had worked at OpenAI, written a widely circulated essay about the future of artificial intelligence, and convinced a group of investors to back a concentrated bet on the technology.

Then the public book disappeared.

In July 2026 the fund, Situational Awareness LP, was forced to unwind nearly all of its public stock positions after steep losses, according to people familiar with the matter. The useful story sits underneath the headline. A persuasive view of the future became dangerous the moment it was mistaken for a risk-management system.

The rise happened fast

The fund launched around 2024 with roughly $225 million, backed by Stripe’s founders, Nat Friedman, Daniel Gross, and the trading firm Jane Street. By early July 2026, CNBC reported it had swelled to as much as $45 billion.

That number needs a label. A private fund’s “assets” can mean client money, gross exposure, or the value of affiliated vehicles, and those are different things. A Form ADV filed by the firm in April 2026 listed roughly $9.3 billion in discretionary assets under management and two clients, while news accounts described the fund as running three to four times leverage. When a fund borrows four dollars for every dollar it manages, its exposure can be several times its investor money.

The thesis was not the problem

Aschenbrenner’s argument was expansive. Compute would grow, models would get more capable, and the companies supplying chips, infrastructure, energy, and software would sit near the center of a historic shift. The public filings showed long exposure to names like CoreWeave, SanDisk, Micron, Nebius, Bloom Energy, and IREN, alongside shorts against software companies he expected AI to disrupt.

That kind of thesis can be correct and still produce bad trades. The Berkshire Hathaway shareholder letters repeat a related warning in a different vocabulary: a good business does not guarantee a good purchase price, and Benjamin Graham’s margin-of-safety principle makes the same point in reverse. The investor needs room for error because the future arrives with errors included.

The trouble was not the direction of the bet. It was the size and the financing of it.

The number that changed everything

In late June 2026, AI-linked stocks began to break. The Philadelphia Semiconductor Index fell roughly 29% from its June 22 peak, and a momentum-heavy technology index dropped far more. For a fund holding four times leverage in the same crowded names, the move did not just hurt returns. It burned through the margin banks had demanded.

By the end of July, the Wall Street Journal reported the fund was down 67% for the month, and on July 30 the firm sold the bulk of its public portfolio to Ken Griffin’s Citadel in a block trade. A July 24 letter cited by the Financial Times told investors simply, “We let you down this month.”

Regulators were paying attention. The Wall Street Journal reported that the SEC had sought information from the banks that financed the AI-centric bets.

Here is the part the easy story misses. The fund did not lose everything. Its private holdings, including a significant stake in Anthropic reported at roughly $5 billion, were not part of the sale, and some accounts note the fund’s returns were helped by Anthropic’s value rising even as the public book collapsed. A liquidation is an action. It is not automatically a loss, and an alarming headline is not an audited statement.

What liquidation really tells you

A rapid sale of public positions tells you that the fund’s previous setup stopped working under the financing it had chosen. It does not tell you the thesis was wrong. It tells you the leverage expired before the idea could.

That is the lesson that transfers. A fund can survive a large decline with patient capital and fail after a smaller one if lenders and counterparties demand cash at the wrong moment. Roger Lowenstein’s account of Long-Term Capital Management remains the classic case: confident capital, enormous leverage, and liquidity that vanished precisely when conviction mattered most.

The mental model: thesis, transmission, failure

A practical way to examine any high-conviction investment story is to separate three layers.

Thesis. What do you believe will happen? In this case, AI demand would keep climbing.

Transmission. How does that belief become a return? Through chip demand, cloud spending, power infrastructure, or a particular company’s margins.

Failure. What breaks the chain? Prices fall, capacity gets overbuilt, rates rise, or the financing you used to hold the position gets called.

Most commentary stops at the thesis. Professional risk work spends far more time on transmission, and on how failure would arrive through the balance sheet rather than the story.

Howard Marks’s memos return repeatedly to second-level thinking: an asset is not attractive merely because its story is attractive. Seth Klarman’s discussion of risk draws the line between the probability of a bad outcome and the consequences when it arrives. Nassim Nicholas Taleb’s work on fragility pushes you to ask how a system behaves under stress, not how persuasive it sounds in calm markets. AQR’s research on factor investing makes a related point: a portfolio can hold many names and still depend on one hidden factor.

The quiet advantage of uncertainty

The fund’s limited public disclosure creates a temptation to fill the space with a clean narrative. That is how financial mythology forms: a young genius sees the future, builds a giant position, loses it, and teaches everyone a lesson.

Reality is less cinematic. Aschenbrenner may have been right about parts of the AI story. The fund still holds private assets that may be worth far more later. The available evidence supports concern about concentration and leverage. It does not support every version of the catastrophe story.

A good investor keeps uncertainty visible. That means labeling what is known, what is inferred, and what would change the conclusion. For this fund: the leverage is documented, the Citadel sale is reported, the scale of the loss depends on which measure you use, and the final outcome is still unwinding.

The bill arrives after the insight

The irony is that “situational awareness” is valuable precisely because it should make a person more sensitive to limits. A forecast needs an exposure map. An exposure map needs an exit plan. An exit plan needs enough liquidity and humility to survive being early, wrong, or both.

The lesson is not to avoid conviction. It is to price the terms under which conviction has to survive. Ask what happens if the market disagrees for two years. Ask whether the lender will still be there at the moment you need it. Count the holdings, then count the assumptions they share.

A thesis earns its keep when it survives contact with time, price, financing, and uncertainty. The fund that looked like intelligence collapsed on one of those four, and it was not the thesis.
