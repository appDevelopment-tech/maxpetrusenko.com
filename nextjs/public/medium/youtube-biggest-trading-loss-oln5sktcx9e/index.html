Where an AI Story Becomes Cash Flow

A brilliant thesis, four times leverage, and one margin call.

A former OpenAI researcher launched a fund with $225 million in 2024 and rode about four times leverage to a reported $45 billion of gross exposure by mid-2026, while regulatory filings showed about $9.3 billion of discretionary assets under management. In six days that July, a rout triggered margin calls and most of the public book went to Citadel. The fund fell 67% that month but stayed up 80% for the year. The reporting shows a financing failure; it does not show that the AI forecast was wrong.

Leopold Aschenbrenner was 24 and had the kind of résumé that turns into a fund. He had worked at OpenAI, written a widely read essay series about AI timelines, and convinced a small group of investors to back a concentrated bet on the technology.

In July 2026 the fund was forced to sell most of its public stock portfolio to Ken Griffin’s Citadel after steep losses, according to the WSJ. The useful story sits underneath the headline: a persuasive view of the future became dangerous the moment it was mistaken for a risk-management system.

The numbers need labels

What gets called "assets" in a fund story is not one number. A private fund has regulatory assets under management, disclosed public-equity holdings, and total exposure after borrowing, and those are different.

The events below are from this July, reported at the time by the WSJ, Reuters, CNBC, and the Financial Times.

The regulatory record is the cleanest. Situational Awareness LP’s Form ADV, filed in June 2026, reported roughly $9.3 billion in discretionary assets under management across two accounts. Its 13F filings showed disclosed long equity of about $552 million at the end of 2025, $13.7 billion in the first quarter of 2026, and $20.2 billion by the second quarter.

The ~$45 billion headline is where leverage shows up. The fund was reported at roughly four times gross leverage, meaning exposure of about $45 billion on top of far less investor money. CNBC described the growth to as much as $45 billion before the sell-off.

A correct thesis, an unsafe construction

Aschenbrenner’s argument was expansive: compute would grow, models would get more capable, and the companies supplying chips, infrastructure, and energy would sit near the center of a historic shift. The fund’s reported longs included Bloom Energy, SanDisk, CoreWeave, Micron, Nebius, IREN, and SK Hynix, with reporting describing shorts against software names such as Adobe.

That thesis can be correct and still produce a dangerous trade. Benjamin Graham’s margin-of-safety principle makes the point directly: the investor needs room for error because the future arrives with errors included. The Berkshire Hathaway letters repeat a related warning: a good business does not guarantee a good purchase price.

The limitation was the size and financing, more than the direction. The fund had reported gains of about 439% through June 30, per the investor letter reviewed by the Financial Times, largely built on leverage.

Six days

The late-July sell-off was brutal for AI infrastructure. Reporting on the unwind described core longs falling 27% to 54% in July against that four-times-levered book. At roughly four times gross leverage, even a modest drawdown in the longs could consume most of the equity; the precise figure depends on netting and financing that are not fully disclosed.

On July 24, with the drawdown already running, Aschenbrenner told investors the fund had “not been immune,” called the selloff one of the best buying opportunities in over a year, and invited fresh capital from August 1; five days later the arithmetic took over. Goldman Sachs, JPMorgan, and Bank of America, the fund’s prime brokers, issued margin calls. On July 29 the fund’s core longs printed their lows of the year. Before the next morning’s open, most of the public book went to Citadel in a single negotiated block, at a discount of more than 10%.

The Financial Times first reported the Citadel purchase; Reuters later described the block. Citadel has since unwound more than 80% of the risk it took on, according to Ken Griffin’s letter, through more than 100 block trades.

Not the collapse the headline suggests

The fund did not vanish. Its reported gross exposure fell from the $45 billion peak, and the investor letter cited by the WSJ said the fund lost about 67% in July while remaining up about 80% for the year. After the sale, CNBC and Reuters described the fund as left with roughly $10 billion in stocks and private investments.

Crucially, the fund kept its private positions. A stake in Anthropic, reported at roughly $5 billion, was not part of the sale. Anthropic was valued around $965 billion in a May 2026 private round, per reporting at the time, and the fund also holds positions in cloud startup Fluidstack and AI chip company MatX. Reuters noted that Situational retained roughly $10 billion in stocks and private investments including Anthropic.

A liquidation is an action, not automatically a verdict, and an alarming headline is not an audited statement.

What the leverage actually tells you

A rapid sale of public positions tells you the setup stopped working under the financing chosen. It does not tell you the thesis was wrong. What the reporting supports is that leverage and liquidity drove the forced sale; it stops short of saying whether the underlying forecast also failed.

That is the lesson that transfers. A fund can survive a large decline with patient capital and fail after a smaller one if lenders and counterparties demand cash at the wrong moment. Roger Lowenstein’s account of Long-Term Capital Management remains the classic case: confident capital, enormous leverage, and liquidity that vanished precisely when conviction mattered most.

The comparison to Archegos is an analogy about mechanics, not an equivalence, as one analysis of the episode put it: a handful of correlated names, leverage spread across banks with only partial visibility into total exposure, and a stall that forced a fire sale instead of an orderly exit.

The mental model: thesis, transmission, failure

A practical way to inspect any high-conviction story is to separate three layers.

Thesis. What do you believe will happen? Here, AI demand would keep climbing.

Transmission. How does that belief become a return? Through chip demand, cloud spend, power infrastructure, or a company’s margins.

Failure. What breaks the chain? Prices fall, capacity gets overbuilt, rates rise, or the financing used to hold the position gets called.

Most commentary stops at the thesis. Professional risk work spends far more time on transmission and on how failure arrives through the balance sheet rather than the story.

Howard Marks’s memos return repeatedly to second-level thinking: an asset is not attractive merely because its story is. Seth Klarman’s letters draw the line between the probability of a bad outcome and the consequences when it arrives. Nassim Taleb’s work on fragility asks how a system behaves under stress, not how persuasive it sounds in calm markets.

One detail is worth keeping: the fund’s leverage had been questioned before the crash. In a March 2025 assessment later reported by the WSJ, the research firm Aksia cautioned clients that overconfidence could create risk-management problems if leverage was large. At the time it was one voice among many.

The quiet advantage of uncertainty

The fund’s limited disclosure invites a clean narrative: a young genius sees the future, builds a giant position, loses it, and teaches everyone a lesson.

Reality is less cinematic. Aschenbrenner may have been right about parts of the AI story, and the fund still holds private assets that may be worth far more later. The WSJ reported that regulators including the SEC had sought information from the banks, which is an investigative step rather than a charge, and investigations often close with no finding.

The honest read is concentrated and precise: the leverage is documented, the Citadel sale is reported, the scale of the loss depends on which measure you use, and the final outcome is still unwinding.

The bill arrives after the insight

The irony is that "situational awareness" should make a person more sensitive to limits. A forecast needs an exposure map. An exposure map needs an exit plan. An exit plan needs enough liquidity and humility to survive being early, wrong, or both.

The lesson is not to avoid conviction. It is to price the terms under which conviction has to survive. Ask what happens if the market disagrees for two years. Ask whether the lender will still be there at the moment you need it. Count the holdings, then count the assumptions they share.

A thesis earns its keep when it survives contact with time, price, financing, and uncertainty. By the reporting available, the part of this fund that ran out of room first was its balance sheet.
