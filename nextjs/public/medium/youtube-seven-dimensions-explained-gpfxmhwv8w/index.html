You Can Be Completely Right and Still Miss the Truth

Seven dimensions reveal what happens when an accurate view is mistaken for a complete one. (Why Your Life Feels Empty Even When You're Never Bored) (Max Petrusenko) (Medium) (X) (Substack)

A two-dimensional person builds a house. The walls close on every side, the door is shut, and for everyone else on the plane the person has vanished. Privacy, solved. Then someone looks down from one dimension higher and sees the roof, the walls, and the person inside, all at once. The fortress was never a fortress. It only worked for everyone who shared the plane.

That is the whole trick of higher dimensions, and it is not really about physics. An accurate view can still be incomplete, and the missing direction is usually ordinary: the one your current frame cannot represent.

A dimension is an independent direction where something can vary. A point has location without room; a line adds one direction; a plane adds another; a body adds depth; time turns the body into a path. The climb from point to body is neat, but the useful part begins where the neatness breaks: a creature reasons from the world it can occupy, so its conclusion is locally sane, and a direction it cannot see is what makes that conclusion fail.

Ridddle's "All 7 Dimensions Explained in Detail (From 0D to Infinity)" uses spectacle to walk this ladder. The thing worth keeping is a diagnostic you can use without pretending pop physics has settled every higher-dimensional claim.

A wall only works inside its own world

The one-dimensional case is the cleanest version of the trap. An obstacle in front of a one-dimensional creature consumes the creature's available future. The creature reasons from the world it can occupy, so its conclusion is locally sane; the missing direction is what makes that conclusion fail from above.

The creature is not stupid; its record is simply incomplete, and the incompleteness only shows up when a new direction arrives.

Two dimensions add the first escape hatch. A flat creature can route around an obstacle, draw a boundary, and build a house that blocks other flat creatures, because everyone inside that conflict shares the same plane. In Edwin Abbott's Flatland, privacy depends on the observer's layer: the resident sees a wall, the higher observer sees the resident, the room, and the hidden interior in one view. A sphere passing through the plane would look absurd from inside it. The flat record shows a point, then a growing circle, then a shrinking circle. Then nothing. Every frame of that record is accurate.

The same distinction matters outside toy geometry. A dashboard, diagnosis, or self-story can be accurate inside its measurement layer while still missing the object that produced the pattern. We are built to reach, stack, and hide things in three dimensions. Comfort from repeated fit between body and layer tells you how well you work in one slice; it does not tell you whether a second slice exists above it.

The frame is the part that is too small for the object it describes.

Your eyes already live on incomplete data

You do not receive a finished 3D room through your eyes. Light hits the retina as a flat signal, and the brain builds depth from disparity, shadow, and expectation.

The cup on the table feels immediate because the reconstruction is fast and usually good enough. You do not see the back of the cup, so you infer a stable object from the slice available to you and act before the model announces itself as a model. Your eyes flatten the room into a signal and the brain rebuilds the depth, which is why a flat image can stand in for a full room.

This is not a hypothetical about Flatlanders. Ordinary seeing already works through compression, and higher-dimensional talk gets less mystical when you remember that.

Time makes the object bigger than the moment

The fourth dimension usually enters as time. A person at one instant is a three-dimensional cross-section; a person across time is a longer structure where birth, life, and death sit as connected segments rather than separate events.

Hermann Minkowski's 1908 lecture "Space and Time" gave relativity a sharper language by joining space and time into spacetime. An event has a spacetime position, and a moving object traces a worldline as it changes location.

The video-editor metaphor is crude in the right way. The viewer watches frame by frame, while the editor can inspect the strip and see how each moment sits inside a larger sequence. Inside experience, a choice feels like a vanishing point once the moment passes; a spacetime picture gives that moment a place inside a larger geometry. You still have to live from the frame you occupy, and breakfast does not become fake because spacetime gives it coordinates. The 4D view adds the rest of the object without letting you occupy it, so it stays useful as a map you cannot live inside.

Eventually the metaphor breaks

The fifth, sixth, and seventh layers keep climbing, but the moves start to repeat: each new layer is another way an accurate view misses a direction. Physics has no single settled public meaning for "the fifth dimension" in the branching-timeline sense, so the honest use is as a visualization of alternate histories, quantum branches, and neighboring possibilities.

Hugh Everett's relative-state formulation is one famous interpretation where branching is taken seriously; other interpretations model quantum results without literal splitting worlds. Branching stays a visualization of possibility, with weaker footing as an established physical claim.

At the sixth layer, universes get different constants or laws; at the seventh, they become too different to compare cleanly with ours. Cosmologists and string theorists sometimes discuss a landscape of possible vacua or universes with different properties. Leonard Susskind popularized that frame in The Cosmic Landscape, and Brian Greene's The Hidden Reality gives a public map of multiverse ideas. A table stays a table because the rules underneath allow wood, screws, and gravity to hold; push the rulebook far enough and the table stops being an object at all.

A lot of higher-dimension content gets sloppy here because it treats awe as understanding. The rougher standard is better: if the language stops cashing out into a clear relation, constraint, or decision, stop pretending the metaphor is doing physics. The seventh layer is most useful as a limit marker: past that point, no measurement, experiment, or decision changes because of the words.

The missing-direction test

The habit transfers cleanly outside physics. When an explanation feels complete, ask one question: what direction can this model not represent?

Then check the three fastest examples:

  • a KPI that misses its time horizon,
  • a diagnosis that misses its context,
  • a personal story that misses the constraint which selected one route over another.

A bad argument may be stuck on one axis; a company can spend a year improving a metric inside the wrong plane; in a personal problem, the current frame can make one route look like fate. Once you name the missing dimension, the job becomes narrower because you can test it instead of arguing inside the same old plane.

When something looks inevitable, ask what variable this frame is incapable of representing.

The room stays useful. The table still holds the cup, and the door stays closed because your body and the door share the same layer. The dangerous mistake is not being wrong. It is being right inside a frame too small to contain the truth.
