They Cloned a 100-Person Studio With One Instruction

One studio spends weeks verifying every script. Three channels rebuild the look in a day, fabricated history included, and the audience can't tell the difference.

Anyone can now buy the machine that makes faceless channels: a $75 course, a $67 school, a $1,499 VIP program, and tutorials that promise $39,500 a month. The copies look like the originals, and the cost is everyone's ability to check.

About 100 people work on the studio's output: journalists, art directors, 2D and 3D animators, producers and a fact-checking desk that reads every script. All of it serves one weekly piece of animated history. Elsewhere on the same platform, three channels in one network run a much cheaper operation. They screenshot a successful thumbnail, paste it into an image model, and type the only instruction the job seems to need: "Regenerate this photo."

The economics are barely a secret. One operator sells a $75 course that teaches the workflow, and the community around it costs another $67 a month. The network sells its own version for $1,499: a blueprint, weekly calls with its founders and a promise to "push you to your first 100K." A tool company hosts a tutorial titled "Recreate a $39,500/Month Faceless YouTube Channel With AI."

The problem is that copying collapsed to nearly free while the cost of being believed did not move at all. Credibility still comes from names, corrections, and sources that exist off-platform, and none of that scales in a Discord server.

The copy machine has a price list

In the course economy the workflow is step by step: find a winning style, screenshot it, and hand the screenshot to an image model for a variant. Scripts get drafted the same way, and the sales pitch for all of it runs in the same register as a late-night ad, disarmingly blunt about what it replaces: "You don't have to be good at literally anything anymore."

The scale is not theoretical: an audit of the most popular channels across countries found 278 channels producing only AI slop, carrying more than 60 billion views between them (Guardian). The first 500 videos shown to a fresh account included 104 slop uploads, plus another third of the sample that the researchers filed under brainrot.

Around it all sit Discord servers where users trade tips and titles. One member described the calculus in the community the studio joined: making videos from scratch is work, while it is "easier to steal people's content" and dub over it with a synthetic voice. The arithmetic works, and nobody in that community seems conflicted about it.

MrBeast posted the receipt in April: a faceless account copied one of his thumbnails, swapped his face for a red stand-in, and the copy pulled 1.8 million views (post). The post he was answering, from an account promoting that exact strategy, laid out the doctrine: taking a top creator's thumbnail format into the faceless space is "by far the strategy with the best track record," and "we never judge the winners."

The wall the fact-checkers hit

Then the copies started shipping history. One network upload about Auschwitz builds to a number: 1,750,000 victims, and the world did not know. The figure has a lineage: it is Rudolf Vrba's estimate from April 1944, recorded by a man who escaped the camp system, and the upload shipped it without the modern count beside it. Historians today estimate that about 1.3 million people were deported to Auschwitz and about 1.1 million were murdered there (Auschwitz museum, USHMM).

The same upload contains a scene of prisoners burning a pile of toys, which the network now concedes "did not happen in that form," and a narrator promising that "no one had ever escaped from Auschwitz to tell the world the truth." That is false, and the network's own corrections page says so: Witold Pilecki escaped in April 1943 and carried reports of the camp to the outside world. One of the network's own channels had already published his story, which makes the line a contradiction of its own catalog.

The studio's fact-checking desk went through the copies line by line (investigation). The hardest finding sits beyond what any single correction can fix. "It is incredibly hard to verify things that never actually existed," its head of fact-checking wrote after working through half of one upload. "Real people usually leave some kind of trace or reference point, but AI can just completely make things up." Plagiarism leaves evidence. The copied thumbnails sit next to their originals with dates attached, and the whole comparison takes an afternoon. A fabricated scene leaves nothing to pull, and its audience has no way to know it happened.

The bill lands on everyone

The damage compounds outward from the fabrications: the studio's own animators now get comments insisting their hand-built work is machine-made. "They think that my voice is AI. They even think that my face is AI generated," one of them says. When an audience cannot tell the difference, some stop trying, and the suspicion lands hardest on the people still doing the expensive version.

Outside the studio, the same tax shows up as a fight. A September post defending an animator who had been wrongly accused of using AI collected 54,000 likes (thread). The replies split between people who say the accusations have turned into bullying and people who say AI users have earned it.

The platform's own systems joined the confusion: a hand-built animation channel that predates the whole wave was auto-flagged as AI, its reach throttled, and it logged its worst-performing upload since 2013, recovering only because it was large enough to have people who could call (Dexerto). Smaller channels doing the same work never get the call.

There is no clean referee

Watch the enforcement record in order and the pattern is pressure first, policy second. In 2025 the platform renamed its repetitious content rule to "inauthentic content," the rule that has always excluded mass-produced uploads from monetization (policy). After public complaints and coverage in January 2026, some of the largest slop channels began disappearing, including one with 5.9 million subscribers and more than a billion views (Verge). In May, automatic AI labels started rolling out, applied when the system detects a photorealistic AI look and no disclosure was made (blog). In July, YouTube spelled out three categories of content barred from monetization: mass-produced and repetitive, off-putting or distressing, and AI personas discussing sensitive topics (TechCrunch). The framing came with a disclaimer: no new policy, just a clearer breakdown of the old one.

Even the enforcement coverage caught the disease. A termination count carried the slop-purge story all summer, credited to the research paper behind the crackdown; that paper reports no such totals. Between a paper and a headline, a count can harden into evidence (tracing).

The accused network answered on September 10 with its own production: thirteen chapters, 122 numbered sources, thumbnail comparisons with dates attached, and an admission. It watched 17 of the studio's 138 published pieces. "There are 121 we didn't open," the rebuttal says (rebuttal). The three Auschwitz errors are conceded in the same document, alongside a comparison run the other way against the studio and questions about its funding.

The studio had answered the funding question months earlier, when criticism over its investment backing went viral in April. Its co-founder posted a public defense of the wall between the financing and the editorial work (Dexerto, Electrify). Both sides now publish receipts, and neither set is complete. The accuser's evidence cannot prove intent, and the rebuttal's counts cannot prove equivalence. The missing piece is structural: there is no referee in this fight who does not sell something.

Trust the corrections

Watermarks were the promised fix, and the machinery is real. Google's SynthID marks generated media at the point of creation, and most major model makers have signed on. The measurement work keeps finding the same boundary, though: statistical watermarks hold up on untouched output and degrade sharply under paraphrase, which is the pipeline's basic move (research).

Monetization rules are the other lever, and they act at the level of the account, after the damage, and only where uploads are monetized in the first place. The course economy takes payment somewhere else entirely: $75, $67 and $1,499, in servers where the audience was never the customer. No policy reaches those servers and no detector gets near them.

What a reader can check is cheaper and sturdier than any detector. Three questions survive scaling:

1. Does the account publish corrections, with dates, or quietly delete them? 2. Do its claims terminate somewhere outside the platform, in a museum page, an archive, a filing? 3. Is anyone accountable by name, or is the whole operation a voice and a logo?

The network at the center of this fight runs a corrections page. That page is the most expensive thing it owns, and its presence, whatever you think of the contents, is the kind of signal that a farm of channels churning out serialized history will not pay for.

The feed is not going to get clean this year or the next. The tools cheapened for everyone, including people with nothing to say, and the platforms will keep responding after the fact and only where the money flows. What remains for a reader is a smaller question, asked in the two seconds before the next upload loads: can this account be corrected, and does anything it says survive the trip off the platform? Getting fooled is survivable. The habit of checking is what keeps it that way, and it cannot be bought at any tier.
