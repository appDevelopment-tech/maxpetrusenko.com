The Stories Running Our Machines Are Becoming Inhuman

A historian’s warning about information, power, and the systems that learn faster than we do

Yuval Noah Harari’s argument is simple enough to carry into daily life: societies act through shared stories, while institutions keep those stories connected to power. AI changes the network because it can generate decisions, narratives, and strategies at machine speed. The safeguard is a system that can detect and correct its own mistakes.

Five thousand years ago, a clerk stood over wet mud, pressed a reed into it, and recorded who owned a field. That small mark let a distant ruler tax a village the clerk had never seen. A tablet became a lever: ownership moved from memory into an archive, and power moved with it. Yet the same record that empowered an individual also extended the reach of the state.

Harari’s video turns that scene into a warning for the present. Information shapes cooperation, and every new information technology changes who can coordinate, persuade, and correct mistakes. AI matters because it adds decision-making agents to the network itself. When an automated system affects your money, work, reputation, or rights, where is the mechanism that can challenge it?

Information creates power by coordinating strangers

Human beings cooperate at scale through shared stories. A nation, a corporation, a currency, and a legal person exist through networks of people treating an imagined structure as real. The story supplies purpose. Bureaucracy supplies roads, records, salaries, and enforcement.

Writing changed that balance. A field could be represented by a tablet rather than by the memory of neighbors. That record gave an individual a stronger claim, while giving a distant state a new way to tax and administer. The same information technology could widen private rights and expand centralized power.

The pattern repeats with mass media. Telegraphs, radio, and television made large democracies possible, while also giving totalitarian regimes tools for coordination and propaganda. Information increases capacity. It does not automatically increase wisdom.

Research on writing and early Mesopotamian administration shows how record keeping grew with cities and institutions. Work on propaganda and mass communication documents how modern regimes used media to organize belief and action. These are different eras with the same structural lesson: whoever controls the flow of information can alter the shape of collective life.

The mechanism: self-correction

Harari names a useful mechanism: self-correction. A system stays healthy when it can identify mistakes and change course without waiting for an external rescue.

A child learns to walk through repeated feedback. Science updates theories when experiments expose errors. Elections let citizens replace leaders after policies fail. The mechanism depends on reversibility, criticism, and an institution strong enough to tolerate evidence that contradicts its preferred story.

Three concrete examples make the model visible:

1. Science: replication, peer criticism, and published corrections turn error into an input. The National Academies describe science as a process of testing and revising explanations. 2. Democracy: elections create a scheduled opportunity to withdraw authority and try another policy. Freedom House’s methodology shows how political rights include meaningful competition and accountability. 3. AI deployment: a lending, hiring, or medical model needs monitoring, appeal, audit logs, and a human authority able to stop it. The NIST AI Risk Management Framework makes governance and measurement operational rather than ceremonial.

A system that cannot correct itself turns every early mistake into doctrine. Scale then multiplies the error.

AI adds an actor to the network

Earlier information systems carried human decisions. AI can produce decisions, strategies, and new outputs without a person specifying every step. Harari’s coffee-machine example clarifies the boundary. A button-triggered machine follows an instruction. A learning system adapts from data, anticipates a preference, and may produce an option its designers did not explicitly write.

AlphaGo made the idea concrete. In 2016, it defeated Lee Sedol and played moves that professional experts initially found strange. DeepMind’s account of the match describes how the system found strategies beyond familiar human patterns. The important shift was epistemic: people saw a field they had studied for centuries from a new angle.

That pattern can travel into finance, art, politics, and administration. A model can discover a profitable allocation, a persuasive message, or a bureaucratic shortcut. The output may be effective while its reasoning remains difficult to inspect. At that point, the old question “who made the decision?” becomes “which network can challenge the decision?”

The always-on problem

Organic networks have rhythms. People sleep, markets close, offices pause, and private life creates distance between one role and the next. An inorganic network can run continuously. It can watch, classify, and act at every hour.

The risk is less cinematic than a single evil computer. It resembles millions of automated clerks receiving authority inside banks, governments, armies, schools, and platforms. Each system may perform a narrow task. Together they can create a permanent layer of decisions around a person’s life.

The European Union AI Act and the White House blueprint for an AI Bill of Rights both reflect the need for oversight, notice, and recourse. Their details differ. Their shared concern is legibility: people need to know when an automated system is acting and how to contest an outcome.

Stories can become mass delusion

Information is not synonymous with truth. Truth is expensive because it requires checking, competing explanations, time, and institutions willing to publish corrections. A vivid story can travel faster than a verified account, especially when a platform rewards attention.

That is how mass delusion becomes possible. A false story can coordinate real people toward real actions. The information may be inaccurate while the consequences are physical: money moves, votes change, enemies are invented, and institutions redirect resources.

The Stanford History Education Group’s research on civic online reasoning shows why source evaluation matters in a networked public sphere. The Reuters Institute Digital News Report tracks how people encounter news through platforms whose incentives favor speed and engagement. AI raises the volume and lowers the cost of producing persuasive narratives.

The answer cannot be a perfect master story. It has to be a living arrangement that rewards evidence, protects dissent, records decisions, and makes reversal possible.

The institution has to learn in public

Harari’s closing proposal is institutional. We cannot predict every form AI will take, so a static rulebook will always lag behind the technology. Institutions need people with technical skill, public accountability, and the authority to respond as new failures appear.

That means asking four questions before granting an AI power:

  • What evidence shows the system works in this setting?
  • Who can inspect the inputs and the decision path?
  • Who can appeal, pause, or reverse the outcome?
  • What record will let the institution learn from failure?

The OECD AI Principles emphasize robustness, transparency, and accountability. The UK AI Regulation White Paper likewise frames governance as an adaptive process. The details will evolve. The mechanism should remain visible.

A society that hands decisions to systems without a correction path is building authority faster than wisdom. A society that keeps the correction path alive can use powerful tools without treating their outputs as sacred.

The practical test is plain: every automated decision that matters should come with a way to question it, a person or institution able to hear the question, and a record of what changed afterward. The future belongs to the networks that can learn in public.
