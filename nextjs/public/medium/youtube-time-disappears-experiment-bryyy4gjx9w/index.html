A New Experiment Where Time Disappears

Inside a tiny cold-atom universe, the past and future touch when the observer can no longer see the whole system.

Time inside a physical system can be reconstructed from changes available to an observer, rather than assumed as a universal background. In a 2026 cold-atom experiment, a Bose-Einstein condensate was divided into observed and unobserved sectors. An entropy-based internal clock ordered the visible cloud’s expansion and collapse, then stopped when the visible sector became empty.

The source describes the cycle in plain physical terms: the cloud is born, expands to a limit, contracts, disappears, and returns. The outside clock keeps running through the empty interval. Inside the observed sector, the entropy record loses the events that would have ordered that pause, so the internal clock has no step to count.

A cloud of atoms appears. It grows. It shrinks. It vanishes. Then, after a pause measured by an outside laboratory clock, another cloud appears.

From the laboratory, this is an ordinary sequence. There is a beginning, a middle, and an end. Inside the little world, the middle can disappear.

That is the point of a new experiment led by Giovanni Barontini at the University of Birmingham and published in Physical Review Research. The work does not recreate the Big Bang, and it does not show that human time is an illusion. It does something more precise: it builds a controlled system in which an internal observer can have a usable arrow of time while an external observer sees information flowing through a boundary.

The distinction sounds philosophical until you look at the apparatus.

A universe in a trap

The experiment begins with a Bose-Einstein condensate, a collection of atoms cooled so far that they behave as one quantum object. The source description for the experiment reports roughly 24,000 rubidium atoms. A pair of laser beams holds the cloud in an optical trap.

A thin optical barrier crosses the trap. It is only about eight micrometres wide, narrow enough to divide the cloud into two regions while still allowing atoms to pass through. The researchers monitor one region and deliberately leave the other outside the observer’s description.

That choice creates the essential asymmetry. The laboratory knows about both sides. The internal observer gets the bright side only.

In the outside laboratory, a camera records the cloud every two milliseconds. The visible cycle lasts about 120 milliseconds: a blink-sized lifetime for a universe that expands and collapses. The numbers are small, but the logic scales. A system does not need galaxies to raise the question of what its inhabitants can call time.

The first atoms crossing into the observed region mark the experiment’s artificial birth. The cloud expands as more atoms become visible. Later, the cloud contracts. The last visible atoms cross back into the hidden region. From outside, the cycle has a duration. From inside, the visible world has become empty.

Why a clock needs more than motion

At first, the cloud’s position seems like a clock. The visible region starts empty, fills, and empties again. But position alone cannot tell the whole story. The same position can occur on the way up or on the way down. A half-full cloud might be a young universe still expanding or an old universe already collapsing.

A clock needs a record of what has happened.

The researchers use a coarse-grained entropy associated with information available in the observed region. As atoms enter, the observer has access to more of the system. As they leave, that accessible record changes. Combined with the cloud’s physical coordinate, this entropic quantity can order events across repeated cycles.

This is a careful use of “entropy.” It is not a claim that the cloud becomes messy in a vague everyday sense. It is a measurement built from what the observer can distinguish about the system. In the paper, that internal time is used to parameterize the measured evolution and reproduce the observed dynamics.

The external clock and the internal clock therefore answer different questions. The laboratory clock tracks the entire apparatus. The internal clock tracks the history available to the partial observer. Both are useful. Neither is the one true clock for every description.

The missing interval

The strangest moment occurs when the visible sector empties. The laboratory clock keeps running. Atoms remain in the hidden sector, and the full quantum system continues to evolve. But the internal observer has no visible change left to order. Its entropy clock stops.

When the next visible cycle begins, the internal description resumes from the new appearance. On a plot against laboratory time, the cycles are separated. On a plot against internal entropic time, the end of one visible world and the beginning of the next can look like a hard cut.

The graph is not showing time being destroyed as a substance. It is showing a limit of description. The observer’s clock is made from accessible change. When accessible change disappears, the clock has no variable left to count.

That is why the result matters beyond a clever atom trap. It gives a physical demonstration of a relational idea often discussed in quantum foundations: time can be a relation between a subsystem and the rest of the system, rather than a universal stage on which everything happens.

Ignorance can create an arrow

The experiment also turns a familiar intuition upside down. We usually think that more knowledge gives a more complete picture of time. Here, a complete external description removes the mystery. The laboratory sees the hidden sector and knows that the cycles continue. The internal observer sees only atoms arriving and leaving. Its incomplete description is what gives the sequence a usable past and future.

This does not mean ignorance magically creates physical events. The atoms still move according to quantum dynamics. It means the arrow of time, as an observable ordering for a subsystem, depends on which correlations and degrees of freedom are available to that subsystem.

The hidden region acts like a physical memory outside the observer’s reach. It carries information about where the visible atoms came from and where they go. The observer cannot use that information directly, so the visible record acquires direction. The past is reconstructed from traces rather than inspected as a complete object.

That limitation has an everyday cousin. A computer file has timestamps for creation, modification, and access. An attacker can alter those timestamps, a technique called timestomping, to make malicious code look older than it is. Investigators then rely on other traces: logs, filesystem metadata, process history, network records. A timestamp is only as trustworthy as the system that produced and preserved it.

The atom experiment is not a cybersecurity experiment. The analogy is narrower and more useful: history is always a reconstruction from records that some observer can access. Remove the records, and the visible arrow becomes weaker or disappears.

What the experiment does not show

The paper’s result is easy to inflate. It does not establish that our universe is a repeating condensate. It does not demonstrate travel into the past. It does not settle the problem of time in quantum gravity. The authors built a clean test bed for relational constructions of time; they did not turn a 120-millisecond laboratory cycle into cosmology.

The system is also deliberately coarse-grained. The internal observer ignores the hidden sector and the quantum correlations spanning the barrier. That omission is the mechanism being tested, not an accidental flaw. But it means the conclusion must stay at the level of controlled model and measured dynamics.

The useful question is therefore practical: what does a subsystem know, and which changes can it use as a clock?

For any physical or computational system, ask three things:

  1. What is inside the observer’s boundary? Name the sensors, logs, variables, and states it can actually read.
  2. Which change orders events? A position, count, energy, entropy, or retained record can serve as a clock only if it distinguishes earlier from later.
  3. What happens when the record goes empty or stale? The system may continue outside, while the internal timeline freezes, jumps, or becomes ambiguous.

That checklist applies to experiments, distributed software, and forensic timelines without pretending they are the same kind of object.

When the present becomes a cut

The experiment’s little universe begins with atoms crossing a line and ends when the visible side is empty. The laboratory sees a smooth continuation through the hidden region. The internal observer gets something closer to film editing: one sequence ends, and another begins after a cut.

The cut is the shape reality takes when a world cannot access all of its own state.

Time, in this experiment, is less like a river flowing everywhere and more like a record assembled from the changes an observer can retain. When the record stops, the internal present has nowhere to point.

That is a modest conclusion compared with “time disappears.” It is also more unsettling: a clock may be telling us as much about the limits of our view as about the world itself.
