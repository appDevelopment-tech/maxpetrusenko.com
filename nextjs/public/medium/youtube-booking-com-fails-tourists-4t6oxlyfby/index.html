The Apartment Had 1,200 Reviews. The Door Was Locked.

A sold-out festival city, one listing, an estimated 200 guests, and the machinery that decides what you get when the door does not open.

An estimated 200 guests paid for the same Karlovy Vary apartment during festival week, then found a note on the door asking them to cancel. The refunds arrived late, minus the insurance premium. What follows is the shape of that failure, and the short protocol that protects you the next time a door does not open.

In the first week of July, the Karlovy Vary festival drained the city of beds. Hotels, guesthouses and campsites, all full, and the official festival guide was already pointing latecomers toward a tent city and the daily trickle of returned rooms (KVIFF). One listing on Booking.com still showed space. It had about 1,200 guest reviews and a rating near 9, and guests kept booking it, because the package on offer looked like every other confirmation, a number, a window, an address.

Then they stood outside a locked door. A note taped to it announced an "Emergency situation" and said the owner could not accommodate reservations "due to extraordinary reasons," citing a hospital stay, according to reporting by Czech public radio (iROZHLAS). The note had clearly been through several rounds of machine translation, and it ended with a request that sounded like cooperation and worked like a trap. Cancel your reservations. By the count of one stranded guest, at least 200 people held bookings for that single address.

One fraudster and one bad week is the easy read. Yet the harder question is what a booking platform can actually promise. Booking.com takes roughly 15 percent of every booking, calls itself an intermediary when trouble starts, and controls every lever that could have stopped this, from listing checks to review sorting. What it cannot do is open the door.

The promise has a phone number

The failure has a rhythm that anyone who has ever called platform support at midnight will recognize. A guest who reached Booking.com that night was told the property had 45 minutes to respond, after which the reservation would be canceled and alternatives would arrive by email. The first email that arrived asked for a rating of the phone call. The reservation was canceled, and no replacement followed.

A week later, a second call, this time on the Czech line, produced a different Booking.com, one that offered to cover a new hotel if the traveler sent the invoice. Real help existed, just a week late for someone standing on a street in a city with no rooms left.

One traveler booked the same listing after the coverage began, to watch the failure from the inside (source footage). He paid about 120 euros, including a 5-euro protection plan sold against exactly this scenario. When the door did not open, the refund arrived at 115 euros. The premium stayed with the platform.

To be fair to the guests who walked into it, the warning signs were visible in the right view. Sorted by newest, the listing's reviews already described a scam in progress, and a careful reader could have caught it. Almost nobody sorts by newest, and nearly everyone reads the rating near 9 and the thousand-plus reviews as a guarantee, because the product is built to make booking feel like certainty.

This was not one bad man

Pull the camera back and the Karlovy Vary week stops looking like an outlier. The same shape runs through cases across the map, different mechanisms with identical endings. In Jakarta, a traveler received a WhatsApp message carrying her real reservation number and stay dates, demanding card verification within 24 hours (Euronews). In Rome, a reservation resolved to a hijacked farm account in South Tyrol, and an approved reimbursement of 334 euros was recalculated down to 50.49 and reclassified as "overbooking" (a guest's public account). In Toronto, a hotel that never existed held a guest's 800 dollars for more than nine months (r/Bookingcom). In Podgorica, a holiday let stayed fully bookable while 10 of its 12 newest reviews described a scam (Which?).

Owners absorb the same story from the other side. One property owner looked up the address behind a last-minute booking and found 139 scam listings stacked on it (r/Bookingcom). A resident described days of strangers ringing the doorbell for a stay that existed only on a screen. In Exeter, a small property firm watched thousands of fake reservations appear under its name after a phishing attack, then fielded the fury of guests whose bookings never existed (BBC). Which? even listed 10 Downing Street as a holiday rental, and the platform failed to spot the spoof.

Booking.com's head of internet safety has described scam reports rising by as much as 900 percent since 2023, with AI making the fakes cheaper to build (Norton). Regulators have noticed too. The Dutch data authority fined the company 475,000 euros over a late breach notification, and Britain's national fraud reporting center has issued alerts about phishing sent through hotel accounts (industry analysis). The platform's defenses exist, and they keep getting outpaced.

How the machinery fails

Which? spent months testing the listing machinery. Creating a bookable holiday listing took under 15 minutes, with no proof of identity, even as Airbnb and Vrbo asked for documents. Review defaults smoothed over fresh warnings, so a property could drift toward a stellar score while its most recent guests were shouting. And refunds arrived mainly after the consumer group began calling.

Phishing follows a script, and the message arrives inside the genuine booking thread, cites the reservation details accurately, and sets a deadline to verify payment. Guests who comply have paid for nothing, and the property whose account was hijacked spends the aftermath answering to strangers.

Off-platform money requests keep arriving on their own schedule. One guest was told to send a 400-pound "deposit" by transfer or lose the booking, and when she quoted the platform's own printed rules, support answered that adding new conditions "does not violate terms" (MoneySavingExpert). The terms themselves cast Booking.com as an intermediary that is not a party to the stay, with the risk left to the traveler (Checkbook). Read that plainly and the Karlovy Vary night makes sense. The platform sold the introduction, and the contract was with a stranger.

Refunds follow the same logic, because the platform processes what the properties agree to, and the fastest resolutions tend to come once a journalist, a consumer group or a chargeback forces the issue. For contrast, Airbnb holds a host's payout until about 24 hours after check-in, and that delay makes the refund possible for a guest standing outside a door that never opened.

The economics of slow

None of this is mysterious to the company. Fifteen percent of more than a billion reservations a year amounts to billions of dollars, and Which? argues that a sliver of it should buy identity checks, link blocking, and review sorting that surfaces the newest warnings first. The counterargument sits quietly in the terms, where the company is an intermediary and the risk belongs to the traveler. Then the same company that left a listing with pages of scam reviews online for days sends a survey asking how the stay went.

The case for booking anyway

Honesty requires the counterweight, because most stays are fine. One Czech consumer guide that has documented the phishing wave counts that across its own bookings, 95 percent of stays never saw an off-platform request (cestujzababku.cz). Which? conceded the platform is safer than it was the year before, and Karlovy Vary's refunds did arrive, eventually (iROZHLAS). Reading this as a call to delete the app misses the point. The lesson is narrower, and it fits on a card.

Five rules for the night the door does not open

1. Verify the door before you trust the confirmation. Put the address on a map, confirm the building matches the listing photos, and call the property on a number you found yourself. A phone call is the cheapest scam filter that exists. 2. Keep your money on rails you can reverse. Pay inside the platform with a credit card. A chargeback gives you a lever at your bank (Irish Times), while a bank transfer gives a fraudster a clean exit. 3. Save everything at booking time, from the confirmation to the price and every message. The screenshot you take beforehand beats the argument you have on a doorstep. 4. When the door fails, do not cancel. Report it in the app immediately, demand the fix in writing, and let the property's clock run out, because canceling hands the loss to you. European rules treat a booking as a contract from the moment price and dates agree, even without a deposit (ECC-Net), and they offer no cooling-off for dated travel. 5. Build the escape hatch before you need it. For festival weeks and big events, carry a backup you can pay for on the spot, and keep enough card headroom for one night at walk-up prices. Then fight the first charge from indoors.

How was your stay

Days after the festival, the last absurdity arrived by email. The traveler from the locked door was asked to rate the stay, on a scale running from bad to excellent. The options had stopped matching the situation, and that mismatch is the whole arrangement in miniature. A confirmation is a claim on a stranger's door, and the only part of the chain you can verify is the part you can walk to and knock on. Everything else is a promise from a company that calls itself an intermediary the moment the promise needs keeping.
